# ASTRA COMMERCIAL ANGLE MASTER

**Canonical commercial reasoning playbook for Astra Agency**  
**Purpose:** Help AI agents diagnose a company, identify the most commercially meaningful problem Astra can credibly solve, select the strongest angle, and translate that angle into a compelling sales direction without force-fitting services or inventing pain.

**Status:** Canonical working source of truth for commercial angle selection  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Date:** 2 September 2026  
**Internal / confidential**

# 0. THE CORE OPERATING PRINCIPLE

The goal is **not** to find an Astra service that can be sold.

The goal is to identify the **most commercially meaningful problem Astra can credibly help solve**.

If no sufficiently strong problem is evidenced, return:

`NO_STRONG_ANGLE`

Never manufacture urgency, pain, inefficiency, poor conversion, lost revenue, internal chaos, or customer frustration just to create a pitch.

A good Astra angle should make the prospect think:

> They understand something important about how our business works, they have connected it to a meaningful outcome, and their proposed direction actually fits us.

A weak Astra angle sounds like:

> Your website could be more modern.

A strong Astra angle sounds like:

> Prospective partners can understand the event, but they cannot easily understand where their brand could participate, what kind of activation would fit, or what information to provide when enquiring. A partner-acquisition flow could shorten that explanation process and create better-qualified partnership conversations.

The difference is not better copy.  
The difference is **better diagnosis**.

# 2. ASTRA'S FOUR COMMERCIAL PROPOSITIONS

Astra has four broad propositions.

These are **containers**, not pitches.

The AI must not stop reasoning once one proposition has been selected.

## 2.1 GROW YOUR BUSINESS

Customer-facing digital experiences that improve how a business is understood, trusted, evaluated, qualified and converted.

Typical work may include:

- websites;
- landing pages;
- service architecture;
- product architecture;
- audience routing;
- positioning;
- funnels;
- forms;
- booking flows;
- lead qualification;
- customer-facing portals;
- onboarding flows;
- ecommerce journeys;
- CRM / email / booking integrations;
- analytics;
- experimentation;
- content and proof architecture;
- conversion-focused UX.

Typical realistic outcomes:

- faster understanding;
- stronger trust;
- easier service discovery;
- clearer audience routing;
- more relevant enquiries;
- more booking or demo completions;
- better lead qualification;
- fewer avoidable pre-sales questions;
- reduced abandonment;
- stronger continuity between acquisition and conversion;
- better use of existing traffic or attention.

Important:

A website redesign does **not** automatically create traffic.

Do not claim more visitors unless the angle includes a credible acquisition mechanism such as SEO, content distribution, campaigns, partnerships or another source of demand.

---

## 2.2 OPTIMISE YOUR BUSINESS

Internal or customer-facing systems that reduce operational friction, repeated manual work and coordination overhead.

Typical work may include:

- workflow applications;
- customer portals;
- partner portals;
- staff portals;
- approval systems;
- dashboards;
- reporting;
- document flows;
- integrations;
- inventory tools;
- order systems;
- scheduling;
- planning;
- task routing;
- role-based workflows;
- data synchronisation;
- replacement of spreadsheet/email/WhatsApp/paper processes;
- self-service functionality.

Typical realistic outcomes:

- fewer manual steps;
- less repeated data entry;
- fewer errors;
- less status chasing;
- faster approvals;
- faster handovers;
- better visibility;
- lower administrative effort;
- more consistent processes;
- more capacity without proportional headcount growth;
- better customer or employee experience.

Important:

The presence of Excel, email or WhatsApp alone is not proof of a problem.

A manual process is only commercially relevant when it creates meaningful:

- volume;
- duplication;
- delays;
- errors;
- coordination cost;
- visibility problems;
- customer friction;
- compliance issues;
- scaling limits.

## 2.3 INNOVATE YOUR BUSINESS

Structured validation and product thinking that moves an opportunity from idea to evidence, decision and roadmap.

Typical work may include:

- discovery;
- opportunity framing;
- business model analysis;
- service design;
- product concepts;
- clickable prototypes;
- validation;
- feasibility;
- stakeholder workshops;
- technical options;
- MVP definition;
- roadmap creation;
- risk/dependency analysis;
- go / no-go decisions;
- prioritisation.

Typical realistic outcomes:

- less money spent building the wrong thing;
- faster strategic decisions;
- clearer prioritisation;
- better stakeholder alignment;
- stronger evidence before development;
- clearer MVP scope;
- clearer technical direction;
- better understanding of value and risk;
- a concrete next step instead of an abstract idea.

Important:

Innovate is not a euphemism for "build an app."

The purpose is often to decide **whether** something should be built and what the smallest valuable version is.

---

## 2.4 BUILD SQUAD / AGENCY PARTNERSHIP

Reliable additional product, design or development capacity for companies or agencies that already know what they need but cannot efficiently deliver it internally.

Typical situations:

- more work than internal capacity;
- product backlog;
- missing backend or full-stack capability;
- over-reliance on individual freelancers;
- agency wants to accept more technical projects;
- development costs are constraining margin;
- hiring is too slow or too permanent;
- business needs ongoing technical delivery;
- agency needs white-label support;
- product team needs temporary acceleration.

Typical realistic outcomes:

- more delivery capacity;
- more predictable execution;
- broader technical capability;
- ability to accept larger or more complex projects;
- reduced hiring pressure;
- fewer freelancer coordination issues;
- improved gross margin versus expensive local resourcing;
- continuity across projects.

Important:

Build Squad is strongest when the prospect already has demand, backlog, client work, product requirements or an obvious capacity constraint.

Do not pitch "cheap developers."

Astra sells **controlled delivery capacity**, with Dutch-facing scoping, communication, project ownership and quality control.

# 4. THE EVIDENCE → PROBLEM → ANGLE CHAIN

Every angle should follow this chain:

`PUBLIC EVIDENCE`
→ `OBSERVABLE FRICTION`
→ `LIKELY BUSINESS PROBLEM`
→ `WHO IS AFFECTED`
→ `WHY IT MATTERS`
→ `ASTRA ANGLE`
→ `WHAT ASTRA COULD CHANGE`
→ `REALISTIC BUSINESS OUTCOME`

Example:

**Evidence:**  
The homepage presents six unrelated services with equal visual weight and no audience-based routing.

**Observable friction:**  
A new visitor must read across several categories before understanding which offer is relevant.

**Likely problem:**  
Service discovery and qualification are being left to the visitor.

**Affected person:**  
Prospective customer.

**Why it matters:**  
Understanding takes longer and low-intent visitors may contact the business with poorly matched requests.

**Angle:**  
Service-routing angle.

**Astra direction:**  
Rebuild the offer architecture around buyer need / use case, then route each path to tailored proof and CTA.

**Realistic outcome:**  
Faster understanding, stronger relevance and better-qualified enquiries.

# 5. EVIDENCE CONFIDENCE LEVELS

Every material claim should be internally labelled.

## OBSERVED

Directly visible in reliable public evidence.

Examples:

- the website has one generic contact form;
- five services appear with equal hierarchy;
- the founder publicly says reports are assembled manually;
- job posts show a product backlog;
- customers are repeatedly told to email for status updates.

Can be stated relatively confidently.

---

## STRONGLY INFERRED

Multiple facts support the interpretation, but it is not directly confirmed.

Example:

A logistics business provides transport, warehousing and picking, has no customer portal, and directs operational requests to email.

Reasonable inference:

Customer coordination may involve repeated email exchange.

Use language like:

- "this may mean...";
- "it looks like...";
- "if this is still handled through...";
- "from what I can see...".

---

## POSSIBLE

Plausible hypothesis with weak or incomplete evidence.

Do not build the outreach around it unless it can first be validated.

---

## UNKNOWN

No credible evidence.

Do not claim it.

# 6. ROOT CAUSE VS SYMPTOM

The AI must distinguish symptoms from underlying commercial problems.

### Symptom:
The website has a lot of text.

Possible root causes:

- offer complexity;
- too many audiences;
- lack of prioritisation;
- unclear positioning;
- regulatory need;
- deliberate educational strategy.

Do not immediately propose shorter copy.

---

### Symptom:
Customers need to contact the team.

Possible root causes:

- intentionally consultative sales process;
- complex custom service;
- poor self-service;
- unclear information;
- qualification requirement.

Do not automatically propose ecommerce or self-booking.

---

### Symptom:
The business uses spreadsheets.

Possible root causes:

- low process volume where spreadsheets are perfectly adequate;
- early-stage experimentation;
- fragmented workflow;
- reporting dependency;
- operational scaling problem.

Do not automatically propose a custom system.

# 7. THE MASTER ANGLE SELECTION ENGINE

The AI must not select the first plausible angle.

For every meaningful prospect, generate **2–5 candidate angles**.

Then score each candidate from 0–5 on:

### A. Evidence strength
How clearly does public evidence support the diagnosis?

### B. Commercial consequence
If the problem is real, how meaningful is the likely business impact?

### C. Buyer relevance
Would the prospect likely care about this problem personally?

### D. Urgency
Is there evidence this problem matters now?

### E. Astra fit
Can Astra credibly solve it with current capabilities?

### F. Differentiation
Is this angle more insightful than a generic agency pitch?

### G. Demonstrability
Can Astra show what it means through a sketch, prototype or concrete concept?

### H. Implementation likelihood
Can this organisation realistically buy, own and implement the solution?

### I. Risk of being wrong
How damaging would it be if the inference is incorrect?

For positive factors:

`0 = extremely weak`
`5 = extremely strong`

For Risk of being wrong:

`0 = low risk`
`5 = high risk`

Suggested internal score:

`ANGLE SCORE = (A+B+C+D+E+F+G+H) - I`

Do not treat the numerical result as absolute truth.

The score exists to force comparison.

---

# 8. THE COUNTERFACTUAL TEST

Before selecting the winning angle, ask:

> If Astra could only discuss one business issue with this founder, would this still be the issue worth discussing?

Then:

> If this problem were fixed tomorrow, would something commercially meaningful improve?

Then:

> Is there another problem that is less visually obvious but more economically important?

Then:

> Are we selecting this because it is easy for Astra to prototype, or because it matters to the business?

If the answer indicates a stronger alternative, change the angle.

---

# 9. NO-STRONG-ANGLE RULE

Return `NO_STRONG_ANGLE` when:

- all identified problems are cosmetic;
- evidence is too weak;
- the proposed impact is speculative;
- the business already handles the journey well;
- the company appears unable to buy or implement;
- the problem is outside Astra's credible capabilities;
- another supplier category is clearly better suited;
- the identified problem is too small to justify personalisation;
- the prospect is only interesting because the prototype would look good.

Skipping a lead is a successful outcome when the evidence does not justify outreach.

# 10. MASTER COMMERCIAL ANGLE LIBRARY

The following angles are reusable patterns.

They are **not keyword triggers**.

---

# 10A. GROW ANGLES

## G01 — Offer Clarity

**Signals**
- homepage does not quickly explain what the company sells;
- abstract wording dominates;
- multiple concepts appear before a concrete offer;
- website assumes visitor already understands the category.

**Underlying problem**
The visitor must work too hard to understand the offer.

**Potential buyer impact**
Lower comprehension and weaker confidence.

**Astra direction**
Clarify the value proposition, offer hierarchy and supporting proof.

**Sell the benefit as**
Faster understanding and a clearer route to the relevant next step.

**Do not say**
"Your messaging is bad."

---

## G02 — Service Routing

**Signals**
- many services;
- equal visual hierarchy;
- long service lists;
- visitors must inspect several pages to determine fit.

**Underlying problem**
The website organises services around the company instead of the buyer's decision.

**Astra direction**
Route visitors by problem, audience, use case or stage.

**Benefit**
Faster service discovery and better-qualified enquiries.

---

## G03 — Audience Segmentation

**Signals**
- several distinct customer types;
- same homepage speaks to all;
- proof and CTA are generic;
- buyer groups have materially different needs.

**Underlying problem**
One message is trying to serve several buying contexts.

**Direction**
Create clear audience pathways with tailored proof and next steps.

**Benefit**
Higher relevance and easier self-identification.

---

## G04 — Intent Routing

**Signals**
- one CTA for every visitor;
- "Contact us" is the only next step;
- high- and low-intent prospects are treated the same.

**Underlying problem**
The site does not recognise different levels of buyer readiness.

**Direction**
Provide appropriate next actions: learn, compare, calculate, book, enquire, request information, etc.

**Benefit**
Lower friction and better qualification.

---

## G05 — Pre-Sales Education

**Signals**
- service is complex;
- buyers need explanation;
- founder/sales team publishes frequent educational posts;
- website offers limited decision support.

**Underlying problem**
Humans may repeatedly explain information the digital experience could communicate earlier.

**Direction**
Build structured education, FAQs, use cases, proof and decision support.

**Benefit**
Better-informed prospects and fewer repetitive pre-sales questions.

---

## G06 — Founder Dependency

**Signals**
- founder appears central to sales;
- company messaging depends heavily on founder posts or calls;
- website is thin relative to founder knowledge;
- custom explanation seems required before prospects understand value.

**Underlying problem**
Commercial knowledge lives disproportionately in the founder.

**Direction**
Turn founder knowledge into scalable digital explanation, proof and qualification.

**Benefit**
Reduce repetitive explanation and make sales less dependent on founder availability.

**Caution**
Do not claim founder overload unless evidenced.

---

## G07 — Proof Transfer

**Signals**
- strong LinkedIn/customer stories;
- testimonials elsewhere;
- partnerships or milestones visible publicly but not surfaced at buying points;
- website looks less credible than the company's real traction.

**Underlying problem**
Existing credibility is not appearing where prospects evaluate the company.

**Direction**
Move relevant proof into the decision journey.

**Benefit**
Stronger trust using assets the business already has.

---

## G08 — Case Study Architecture

**Signals**
- logos without context;
- projects listed but no problem / solution / outcome;
- strong work difficult to evaluate.

**Underlying problem**
Prospects cannot connect past work to their own situation.

**Direction**
Structure cases by buyer problem, context, delivery and evidence.

**Benefit**
Make proof more persuasive and easier to self-qualify against.

---

## G09 — Booking Friction

**Signals**
- booking requires email;
- external booking experience is disconnected;
- forms are long or unclear;
- no indication of what happens after booking.

**Underlying problem**
Intent can be lost between interest and confirmed appointment.

**Direction**
Simplify the booking journey and clarify expectations.

**Benefit**
Higher booking completion and fewer scheduling questions.

---

## G10 — Lead Qualification

**Signals**
- generic contact form;
- custom/high-ticket service;
- sales team likely needs context before responding;
- no structured intake.

**Underlying problem**
Every enquiry enters the same funnel regardless of fit.

**Direction**
Build progressive qualification around business need, timing, fit and scope.

**Benefit**
More useful first conversations and less time spent on poorly matched leads.

---

## G11 — Application / Eligibility Flow

**Signals**
- coaching, accelerator, specialist service, programme, financing or membership;
- not every prospect should buy;
- application is email or generic form.

**Underlying problem**
The business needs to determine fit before committing sales or delivery time.

**Direction**
Create an application flow with clear expectations and qualification logic.

**Benefit**
Higher-quality applicants and easier triage.

---

## G12 — Partner Acquisition

**Signals**
- sponsorships;
- ecosystem partners;
- channel partners;
- event partners;
- public partnership logos but unclear partner proposition;
- partnership enquiries depend on founder explanation.

**Underlying problem**
Potential partners cannot easily understand what participation looks like.

**Direction**
Build partner proposition, activation paths, proof and structured enquiry.

**Benefit**
Faster partner understanding and better-qualified partnership conversations.

---

## G13 — Investor Comprehension

**Signals**
- fundraising;
- complex technology;
- several product possibilities;
- evidence, projections and vision mixed together;
- unclear next milestone.

**Underlying problem**
Investors struggle to distinguish present evidence from future thesis.

**Direction**
Create an evidence-led investment story, milestone hierarchy and qualified data-room route.

**Benefit**
Faster thesis comprehension and more relevant investor conversations.

**Caution**
Never promise funding.

---

## G14 — Recruitment Conversion

**Signals**
- hiring;
- strong culture content on LinkedIn but weak careers section;
- vacancies lack employee proof or clear process;
- candidates are a strategic audience.

**Underlying problem**
Employer value is not being communicated during candidate evaluation.

**Direction**
Build stronger careers / role journey.

**Benefit**
Better-informed applicants and improved candidate fit.

---

## G15 — Referral Conversion

**Signals**
- referral-heavy business;
- professional services;
- referrals arrive with limited context;
- site does not support credibility or next steps.

**Underlying problem**
Warm referrals still need to understand and trust the business after introduction.

**Direction**
Design website as a referral-support system.

**Benefit**
Help referred prospects validate the recommendation and move forward faster.

---

## G16 — Product / Service Comparison

**Signals**
- several packages;
- multiple tiers;
- adjacent offers;
- buyer must contact sales simply to understand differences.

**Underlying problem**
Comparison is difficult.

**Direction**
Build comparison logic and guided selection.

**Benefit**
Faster self-selection and fewer basic comparison questions.

---

## G17 — Pricing Transparency / Expectation Setting

**Signals**
- high variability;
- no indication of commercial model;
- sales process gets many poor-fit enquiries;
- public pricing may be possible without harming strategy.

**Underlying problem**
Buyer expectations are not calibrated.

**Direction**
Use pricing ranges, packaging or qualification cues where appropriate.

**Benefit**
Reduce mismatch and improve enquiry quality.

**Caution**
Do not recommend public pricing when custom procurement makes it inappropriate.

---

## G18 — Customer Onboarding Journey

**Signals**
- complex first steps after purchase;
- customers need documents/setup/training;
- fragmented onboarding information.

**Underlying problem**
The post-sale transition is harder than necessary.

**Direction**
Create guided onboarding, checklist, portal or education layer.

**Benefit**
Faster activation and fewer onboarding questions.

---

## G19 — Self-Service Customer Experience

**Signals**
- repeated customer questions;
- information exists but is hard to find;
- status, documents, bookings or settings require staff involvement.

**Underlying problem**
Simple customer needs consume human service capacity.

**Direction**
Create self-service tools or customer portal.

**Benefit**
Fewer repetitive service requests and better customer visibility.

---

## G20 — Ecommerce Decision Support

**Signals**
- technically complex product;
- many variants;
- configurable product;
- customers need help choosing;
- category navigation is confusing.

**Underlying problem**
Choice complexity suppresses confident purchase decisions.

**Direction**
Guided product selection, configurator, comparison or education.

**Benefit**
Better product discovery and fewer wrong-fit purchases.

---

## G21 — Abandoned Handover

**Signals**
- strong landing site then abrupt transfer to another system;
- booking/payment/application changes branding and context;
- users must re-enter information.

**Underlying problem**
Momentum and trust can be lost at system boundaries.

**Direction**
Reduce handover friction and maintain context.

**Benefit**
More continuous conversion journey and lower abandonment risk.

---

## G22 — Local / Multi-Market Routing

**Signals**
- multiple languages, countries, offices or service areas;
- inconsistent pages;
- unclear location eligibility;
- visitors must determine which branch applies.

**Underlying problem**
Users cannot easily identify the correct local offer.

**Direction**
Location/language-aware routing.

**Benefit**
Faster access to relevant services and fewer misrouted enquiries.

Revenue growth requires proportional administrative growth.

**Direction**
Automate the highest-volume coordination points.

**Benefit**
Increase capacity without immediately adding equivalent admin headcount.

**Caution**
Only use when growth or workload signals support it.

# 10E. HYBRID ANGLES

Some strong opportunities span multiple propositions.

A hybrid angle should still have **one primary business outcome**.

Examples:

### H01 — Growth + Operations Portal
Customer portal both improves customer experience and reduces internal status coordination.

Primary outcome must be selected based on evidence.

### H02 — Lead Generation + Qualification Automation
Website creates demand capture while structured intake reduces poor-fit sales work.

### H03 — Partner Acquisition + Reporting
A partner portal supports both acquisition and post-campaign proof / renewal.

### H04 — Innovation + Build
Astra validates an MVP first, then provides delivery capacity.

### H05 — Brand / Positioning + Conversion
Brand strategy only matters commercially when translated into clearer buyer recognition, trust or conversion.

Do not sell "everything Astra can do" in one message.

Choose one entry point.

# 12. BENEFIT LADDER

Do not stop at features.

Translate:

`FEATURE`
→ `PROCESS EFFECT`
→ `USER EFFECT`
→ `BUSINESS EFFECT`

Example:

**Feature**  
Customer portal.

**Process effect**  
Customers see status, documents and next actions without emailing.

**User effect**  
Less uncertainty for customers and fewer repeated updates for staff.

**Business effect**  
Lower coordination effort and more service capacity.

Another example:

**Feature**  
Audience-specific website paths.

**Process effect**  
Each visitor reaches relevant offer, proof and CTA faster.

**User effect**  
Less confusion.

**Business effect**  
Better-qualified enquiries from existing traffic.

---

# 13. COST OF DOING NOTHING

Cost-of-doing-nothing framing can strengthen an angle, but it must remain evidence-based.

Valid categories:

- continued founder time;
- repeated admin work;
- slow approvals;
- poor qualification;
- lost context;
- avoidable customer questions;
- underused proof;
- slower project throughput;
- margin pressure;
- inability to accept work;
- delayed validation;
- unnecessary development investment.

Never invent:

- exact revenue loss;
- conversion rate;
- customer churn;
- employee hours;
- missed deals;
- compliance penalties.

Unless credible data exists.

Prefer:

> If this is still handled manually, the coordination load grows with every additional customer.

Not:

> You are losing €100k every year.

# 14. PROTOTYPE DECISION ENGINE

A prototype is worth creating when it materially increases the prospect's ability to understand the idea.

Score:

- visual demonstrability;
- business specificity;
- strength of evidence;
- implementation plausibility;
- prospect value;
- effort required.

## Strong prototype candidates

- homepage / audience routing;
- booking journey;
- application flow;
- customer portal;
- partner portal;
- investor narrative;
- dashboard;
- reporting portal;
- workflow;
- product concept.

## Weak prototype candidates

- abstract strategy;
- capacity partnership;
- backend integration with little visible UX;
- problems dependent on unknown internal data;
- ideas where requirements are too uncertain.

Possible outcomes:

- `YES_FULL`
- `YES_KEY_JOURNEY_ONLY`
- `ONLY_AFTER_INTEREST`
- `NO`

A visually exciting prototype does not make a weak lead strong.

# 15. MESSAGE TRANSLATION RULE

Research deeply.

Message selectively.

The final outreach should usually contain:

1. accurate business understanding;
2. one strongest friction;
3. one Astra direction;
4. one realistic benefit;
5. low-friction CTA or validating question.

Do not include:

- the full audit;
- multiple angles;
- long strategic explanation;
- every problem found;
- unsupported internal assumptions.

The message is the **compressed output of the reasoning**, not the reasoning itself.

---

# 16. WHEN TO ASK A VALIDATION QUESTION INSTEAD OF PITCHING

When the strongest angle depends on an internal process that cannot be confirmed publicly, ask a smart question.

Example:

> Are customer shipment updates still mostly coordinated through email today?

This is better than asserting:

> Your team wastes hours every week answering shipment questions.

Use validation questions when:

- internal workflow is central to the angle;
- public evidence is suggestive but incomplete;
- being wrong would damage credibility;
- the question itself demonstrates useful understanding.

# 17. ANTI-PATTERNS

Never use these shortcuts.

## Anti-pattern 1
"Old-looking website → redesign."

Why wrong:
Aesthetics may have little commercial consequence.

---

## Anti-pattern 2
"Uses Excel → custom app."

Why wrong:
Excel may be completely appropriate.

---

## Anti-pattern 3
"Multiple services → website is confusing."

Why wrong:
The site may already route them well.

---

## Anti-pattern 4
"Founder posts a lot → founder dependency."

Why wrong:
Thought leadership can be deliberate, not a bottleneck.

---

## Anti-pattern 5
"No pricing → pricing problem."

Why wrong:
Custom B2B services may intentionally require qualification.

---

## Anti-pattern 6
"External booking platform → broken journey."

Why wrong:
The external tool may work perfectly.

---

## Anti-pattern 7
"Company is growing → automate everything."

Why wrong:
Growth does not prove process pain.

---

## Anti-pattern 8
"Complex technology → investor confusion."

Why wrong:
Investors may have specialist knowledge and the public website may not be the primary diligence channel.

---

## Anti-pattern 9
"Agency hires developers → capacity problem."

Why wrong:
Hiring may be strategic expansion rather than shortage.

---

## Anti-pattern 10
"Could benefit from Astra → should pitch."

Why wrong:
Almost every business could theoretically use digital services.

The threshold is **commercial relevance + credible evidence**.

# 18. RED-TEAM CHECKLIST

Before finalising an angle, answer:

1. Could this be the wrong company or person?
2. Has the company changed direction?
3. Is the issue actually important?
4. Is it only a design preference?
5. Does another page already solve it?
6. Is the workflow deliberately manual?
7. Is the current system appropriate for the company's scale?
8. Am I assuming internal pain?
9. Am I assuming the buyer's priority?
10. Is another Astra proposition stronger?
11. Is another supplier category better suited?
12. Can Astra realistically implement this?
13. Is the commercial consequence believable?
14. Is this angle company-specific?
15. Would the founder immediately correct the premise?
16. Am I choosing it because it is easy to prototype?
17. What evidence would falsify my conclusion?
18. Is `NO_STRONG_ANGLE` more honest?

# 20. REAL ASTRA CASE PATTERNS

These cases are learning examples, not universal templates.

## Case: On a Plod

Weak obvious angle:
"Improve the event website."

Stronger selected angle:
**Partner acquisition.**

Reasoning:
The runner proposition was understandable, but prospective partners had a less developed route to understand activation possibilities and submit commercially useful enquiries.

Direction:
Partner-acquisition page / activation planner.

Commercial benefit:
Faster partner understanding, better-structured enquiries and less repeated founder explanation.

Lesson:
The strongest angle may target a secondary audience rather than the website's primary user.

---

## Case: Gravity Fellow

Weak obvious angle:
"Make the site clearer."

Stronger selected angle:
**Investor comprehension and evidence hierarchy.**

Reasoning:
The company communicated a large technology thesis involving several layers and possible applications. The important question was not visual polish but whether investors could distinguish nearer-term engineering evidence from the longer-horizon system thesis and understand the next validation milestone.

Direction:
Evidence-led investor story and qualified investor-room journey.

Benefit:
Faster thesis comprehension and more relevant diligence conversations.

Lesson:
For complex businesses, structure uncertainty instead of oversimplifying it.

---

## Case: Decruy Transport

Weak obvious angle:
"Modern logistics website."

Stronger selected angle:
**Customer operations portal.**

Reasoning:
The business combines transport, warehousing and picking. A more valuable opportunity was improving how customers could request shipments, submit stock/picking instructions, view status, exchange documents and access proof of delivery.

Benefit:
Less status coordination and better customer visibility.

Lesson:
Operational leverage can be more valuable than marketing polish.

---

## Case: DigiLite

Weak obvious angle:
"Better advertiser landing page."

Stronger angle:
**Advertiser proof / repeat-campaign portal.**

Reasoning:
Novelty may help secure an initial campaign, but repeat purchasing depends on credible campaign proof and the ability to translate results into the next media plan.

Direction:
Portal showing campaign activity, creative status, exposure and results.

Benefit:
Less manual report assembly and stronger repeat-campaign conversations.

Lesson:
Think beyond acquisition to retention and repeat purchase.

---

## Case: Alan coaching business

Possible angle set:
- positioning clarity;
- brand consistency;
- referral conversion;
- booking/application journey;
- trust architecture;
- discoverability;
- client portal.

Correct lesson:
Do not pitch all of them simultaneously.

Select the constraint most relevant to the business's current stage.

When the business is early and referral-led, the first priority may be making referred prospects understand the offer, trust the coach and take the next action—not building the entire long-term platform immediately.

Lesson:
Stage matters.

# 25. ANGLE PRIORITY HIERARCHY

When multiple valid angles exist, prefer:

1. High commercial consequence over visual polish.
2. Current bottleneck over future nice-to-have.
3. Strong evidence over clever speculation.
4. Buyer-relevant pain over generic best practice.
5. Specific improvement over broad transformation.
6. Demonstrable outcome over vague innovation.
7. One strong entry point over full-service pitching.
8. Low-risk credibility over aggressive claims.

# 27. FINAL ANGLE QUALITY BAR

An angle is ready for outreach only when the AI can clearly answer:

### What did we observe?

### Why might that matter?

### Who experiences the friction?

### What commercial outcome is affected?

### What exactly would Astra change?

### Why is this direction more important than the alternatives?

### What evidence supports it?

### What are we still uncertain about?

### What must we not claim?

### Is the insight useful even if the prospect never buys from Astra?

If any of those answers are weak, keep researching or return `VALIDATE_FIRST` / `NO_STRONG_ANGLE`.

# 30. FINAL PRINCIPLE

Astra should not sound like an agency looking for something to redesign.

Astra should sound like a business partner capable of spotting where digital strategy, design, workflows or technology can remove a meaningful constraint.

The best angle is not:

**"What can we build for them?"**

It is:

**"What is getting in the way of an important outcome, and can Astra credibly change it?"**

That is the commercial reasoning standard every Astra AI agent should follow.
