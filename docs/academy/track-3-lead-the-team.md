# Track 3. Lead a GTM Engineered Team

The full course for sales managers. Every lesson, quiz, assignment, the practical test, the final exam and the capstone.
It follows the rulebook in `README.md` in this folder. Prices are suggestions for Raka to set.

## Who it's for

Sales managers, heads of sales, sales directors and founders who run a sales team of 3 to 30 people.

You don't build the flows yourself. You decide what gets built, you get the team to use it, and you answer for the
forecast. You may know selling very well and still feel lost when someone says "webhook" or "enrichment waterfall".
That's fine. This track explains every technical word in plain language the first time it comes up, and it never asks
you to code.

**Not for you if** you have no team and want to build the systems yourself. Take Track 1. If you're a seller who wants
more meetings for yourself, take Track 2.

## The promise

In four weeks (self learn) or six to eight weeks (1:1) you'll have a working operating system for your team. That means
a sales bible your people actually follow, an ICP and routing design the team agreed to, CRM hygiene rules with owners, a
dashboard you trust, a forecast you can defend, a win and loss review from real calls, and a 90 day plan that says
whether to build, buy or hire a GTM engineer, with the cost written down.

### The team workshop, minute by minute (90 minutes)

**Goal.** Your team leaves the room having written the sales bible with you, so it's theirs and not yours. Held after S2,
in person or on video, with every seller and SDR invited. You (the manager) host, Raka facilitates.

**Before.** Share version one of the sales bible 2 days ahead. Ask each person to bring one deal they won and one they lost
in the last 6 months. Book a room or a video call with a shared screen. Have the sales bible open in Google Docs with
comments switched on.

| Minutes | What happens | Who leads | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Why we're here. The manager says in two sentences what's broken today (for example, "we all mean different things by qualified, so the forecast is a guess") and what the team gets from fixing it (fewer CRM questions from me, fairer lead routing) | Manager | Everyone knows the why |
| 5 to 10 | How the session works. Raka explains the rules. Every definition must be testable ("could two people look at the same deal and agree?"). Disagreements get written down, not argued for ten minutes | Raka | Ground rules on screen |
| 10 to 25 | Stages and exit criteria. Go through each stage on screen. For each one ask "what must be true before a deal moves here?" Team members call out answers, Raka types them in. Cut anything nobody can check | Raka | Exit criteria per stage, agreed or flagged |
| 25 to 35 | Definitions. Five words the team uses differently. Usually "lead", "qualified", "opportunity", "decision maker", "closed lost". One sentence each, agreed by show of hands | Raka | Definition list |
| 35 to 40 | Break | | |
| 40 to 60 | Won and lost deals. In pairs (or breakout rooms), each pair takes two deals people brought and answers three questions. What did the buyer care about? Which stage took longest? What would we do differently? Each pair reports back in 2 minutes | Raka, pairs | Real objections and patterns, written in the doc |
| 60 to 75 | Objections and plays. From what the pairs said, pick the top 5 objections. For each one the team writes the best answer they've actually used that worked. Assign one person per play to tidy it up | Manager | 5 objections with answers and an owner each |

# The modules

### Module 0. What GTM engineering changes for a manager

**Goal.** Understand what GTM engineering is, what it changes in your job, and write down where your team stands today.
**Time.** 24 minutes of video + 1 hour assignment.
**You need.** Your CRM login, last quarter's pipeline export (or the practice pack), a Google account, the template
`T3-00 Team current state` copied into your Drive.

#### Lesson 0.1 From headcount to systems (6 min)

**In plain words.** For years the answer to "we need more sales" was "hire more people". GTM engineering is the other
answer. You build systems (lists, data, automations, dashboards) so each person you already have spends more time selling
and less time searching, typing and guessing.

**You'll be able to.**
- Explain GTM and GTM engineering in one sentence each
- Name three tasks in your team that a system could do instead of a person
- Say why adding people to a broken process makes the problem bigger

**What's shown.**
1. Definitions on screen. **GTM (go to market)** is everything a company does to find customers and sell to them, who you
   target, what you say and which channels you use. **GTM engineering** is building the systems behind that, so selling
   runs on a machine instead of on memory.
2. The headcount answer, drawn as a simple picture. One SDR (sales development representative, the person who finds and
   books first meetings) books a certain number of meetings. Hire two more and you expect three times as many. Show why
   that rarely happens. Each new person searches for leads by hand, writes messages from scratch, and types notes into the
   CRM differently.
3. The systems answer, drawn next to it. The same SDR gets a ready list of good fit companies every Monday, a research
   summary per account, a message template that's been tested, and a CRM that fills in half the fields by itself.
4. Astra's own example. Raka's engine sent 330 researched first messages between 14 July and 25 October 2026, and 26
   people said yes (7.9%). In October 30% replied and 15% said yes, against 4% in September.
5. The lesson that came with it. Consumer businesses said yes most often and then couldn't pay. The B2B companies with a
   budget were the ones worth a meeting. Deciding who to target is a management call more than a technical one.
6. Screen demo. Open the practice pipeline in Google Sheets. Click Data, then Create a filter. Filter the Stage column to
   Lost. Show how many lost deals have no loss reason filled in. That missing column is a systems problem, and you can't
   hire your way out of it.
7. The three questions this track keeps asking. What should the system do? Who uses it? How do we know it's working?

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write down the last five things your team complained about that took up their time (for example,
"finding phone numbers", "updating the CRM after calls"). Next to each, write "person" if it truly needs a human, or
"system" if a tool could do it. Keep the list, you'll use it in Assignment 0.

#### Lesson 0.2 What a GTM engineer actually does (6 min)

**In plain words.** A GTM engineer is the person who builds those systems. They connect your tools, clean your data, set
up the automations and build the dashboards. As a manager you don't need to do their job, you need to know what to ask
them for and how to judge their work.

**You'll be able to.**
- Describe a GTM engineer's weekly work in plain language
- Tell the difference between a GTM engineer, RevOps and marketing ops
- Write one clear request a GTM engineer could act on

**What's shown.**
1. What a GTM engineer builds, as a list with one line each. **Lists** (finding the companies and people that fit).
   **Enrichment** (adding missing facts to a record, like an email address or the company's size). **Signals** (things
   that happen which make a company more likely to buy now, like hiring planners or opening a new depot). **Sequences**
   (a planned series of messages over days or weeks). **Automations** (steps that run by themselves, like "when a form is
   filled in, create a contact and tell the right seller"). **Dashboards** (a page of charts showing how things are going).
2. A word you'll hear, **API**. It's a way for one piece of software to talk to another, ask it for data or tell it to do
   something. You don't need to use one. You need to know that if two tools "have an API", they can usually be connected.
3. The tools a GTM engineer uses, shown briefly. Clay for lists and enrichment, lemlist for sequences, n8n for connecting
   tools, HubSpot as the CRM. You'll see them in action in later modules. You won't need to buy any of them for this track.
4. RevOps (revenue operations) against GTM engineer. RevOps runs the processes, tools and data for sales, marketing and
   customer success. A GTM engineer builds the machinery. In small companies one person often does both.
5. A bad request to a GTM engineer on screen. "Can you make the CRM better?" Then a good one. "When a deal moves to Demo
   done, I want the AE to get a task to send the proposal within 2 days, and I want a weekly report of deals where that
   didn't happen."
6. How to judge their work. Ask three things. Does it run without them? Is it written down? Can you see in a number
   whether it helped?

**Do it now.** (5 min) Take one item from your "system" list in Lesson 0.1. Write it as a good request using the shape on
screen. When this happens, I want this to happen, and I want to see this number.

#### Lesson 0.3 Roles, and how your week changes (6 min)

**In plain words.** When a team works this way, the jobs shift. Sellers stop doing data entry and searching. The manager
stops chasing updates and starts reading numbers, coaching and fixing the process. This lesson shows the before and after
of a manager's week.

**You'll be able to.**
- List who does what in a GTM engineered team
- Compare your current week with the week you want
- Pick the two hours a week you'll protect for this course

**What's shown.**
1. The roles, one line each. **SDR** books first meetings. **AE** (account executive) runs deals from first meeting to
   signed contract. **CS** (customer success) keeps customers happy and buying after the sale. **Marketing** brings people
   in. **GTM engineer or RevOps** builds and runs the systems. **Manager** decides the process, coaches people and owns the
   forecast (your best estimate of what will be sold this month or quarter).
2. The Routeful team on screen. Eva (manager), Sanne and Joris (SDRs), Lotte, Daan and Mira (AEs). No GTM engineer yet.
   Show who currently does the CRM cleaning (Eva, on Sunday evenings).
3. A manager's week before, as a calendar. Hours spent asking "what's the status of this deal?", fixing the CRM, making
   the forecast in a spreadsheet from memory, and very little coaching.
4. A manager's week after. A 30 minute pipeline review from a dashboard. A 10 minute hygiene check. Two hours of coaching
   from call recordings. One hour improving the process.
5. What doesn't change. You still own the forecast, you still hire, you still close the big deals with your AEs.
6. The honest part. The first month is more work, not less, because you're building and changing habits at the same time.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Open your calendar for last week. Colour or mark each block as "chasing", "admin", "coaching",
"selling" or "improving the process". Add up the hours per type. Write the totals down for Assignment 0.

#### Assignment 0. Your team's current state on one page

**Goal.** One page that shows where your team stands today, so you have a "before" to measure everything against.
**Time.** 1 hour.
**You need.** Template `T3-00 Team current state` (a one page Google Doc), your pipeline export, your calendar notes from
Lesson 0.3, your "person or system" list from Lesson 0.1.

**The template has four boxes.**

| Box | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| 1. Team | Each person, their role, how long they've been in it, what they're measured on |
| 2. Tools | Every tool the team uses for selling, who pays for it, how much it's actually used (daily, weekly, rarely) |
| 3. Pipeline numbers | Last quarter. Deals created, deals won, deals lost, win rate, average deal value, average days to close, open pipeline value today |
| 4. Biggest leak | The one place where most deals or most time is lost, with the number that shows it, and your guess at why |

**Steps.**
1. Open `T3-00 Team current state` in your Templates folder. Click File, then Make a copy, and name it "Assignment 0,
   [your name]".
2. Box 1. List every person on the team. Write their role in plain words and what they're targeted on (meetings booked,
   revenue, deals closed).
3. Box 2. List every tool. To check usage, ask your CRM admin, or in HubSpot click Settings (the gear icon), then Users &
   Teams, and look at the Last active column. Write "daily", "weekly" or "rarely".
4. Box 3. Open your pipeline export in Google Sheets (File, Import, Upload, then Insert new sheet).
5. Count deals created. Click an empty cell and type `=COUNTA(A2:A)` where column A holds the deal names.
6. Count won and lost. Type `=COUNTIF(F2:F,"Won")` and `=COUNTIF(F2:F,"Lost")`, changing F to your Stage column and the
   words to your stage names.
7. Win rate is won divided by (won plus lost). Type `=won_cell/(won_cell+lost_cell)` with the real cell names and format
   it as a percentage (Format, Number, Percent).
8. Average deal value. Type `=AVERAGEIF(F2:F,"Won",G2:G)` where G is the amount column.
9. Average days to close. If you have a create date and a close date, add a new column with `=close_date_cell-create_date_cell`
   and average it for won deals.
10. Box 4. Look at where deals stop. Count how many deals sit in each stage (one `COUNTIF` per stage). The stage with the
    biggest drop to the next one is your likely leak. Write the number and your guess at why.
11. Add your calendar totals from Lesson 0.3 at the bottom ("I spend X hours a week chasing updates").
12. Keep it to one page. Click File, Download, PDF.

**Hand in.** The PDF, plus a link to the Google Sheet where you did the counts (set sharing to "Anyone with the link can
view" only if the sheet holds no personal data, otherwise send a screenshot of the formulas).

**Marking rubric.**

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 | Must? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Team box complete | Missing | Names only | Every person, role and target | No |
| Tools box shows real usage | Missing | Tools listed, no usage | Each tool with usage and owner | No |
| Pipeline numbers correct and from data | Missing or guessed | Some numbers, or formulas wrong | All seven numbers, traceable to the sheet | Yes |
| Biggest leak named with a number | Missing | Named but no number | Named, with the number and a reason | Yes |
| Fits on one page and readable | Several pages | One page but cramped | One clean page | No |

Pass at 7 of 10 points, with no 0 on a must.

**Good example.** Routeful version. Box 3 says "Last quarter. 60 deals created, 9 won, 21 lost, win rate 30%, average won
value €18,400, average 74 days to close, €690,000 open today." Box 4 says "Biggest leak is Demo done to Proposal sent.
18 deals reached Demo done, only 9 got a proposal. Guess. AEs wait for the prospect to ask for pricing instead of sending
it." (The numbers above illustrate the shape. Use the counts you get from the pack.)

**Bad example.** "Pipeline is okay, win rate about 25 to 30%, biggest problem is the team doesn't update the CRM." It
fails because there are no counts from data, the leak isn't a stage with a number, and "doesn't update the CRM" is a
cause you haven't shown yet.

**Stuck?**
1. *My COUNTIF returns 0.* The stage name in the formula must match the sheet exactly, including capitals and spaces.
   Copy it from a cell.
2. *I don't have a close date column.* Use "last modified date" for won deals and write that you did, or use the practice
   pack.
3. *Everything looks like a leak.* Pick the single biggest drop between two stages. You'll find the others later.

### Module 1. The operating model

**Goal.** Draw how revenue moves through your team, who owns each step, and where deals and customers leak out.
**Time.** 32 minutes of video + 1.5 hours assignment.
**You need.** Assignment 0, Google Slides (or paper and a phone camera), template `T3-01 Operating model`.

#### Lesson 1.1 Your revenue model (6 min)

**In plain words.** A revenue model is how your company actually makes money. Who pays, how much, how often, and how long
they stay. Your team's process has to fit it. A team selling one big deal a year needs a different process from one selling
hundreds of small monthly subscriptions.

**You'll be able to.**

#### Lesson 1.2 Roles and who owns what (6 min)

**In plain words.** Every step a customer goes through needs exactly one owner. When two people own a step, nobody does it.
When nobody owns it, it falls through the cracks. This lesson maps each step to one role.

**You'll be able to.**

- Give each step exactly one owner
- Find steps with no owner or two owners

**What's shown.**

   renew.
2. The Routeful owners. Marketing owns attract and capture. SDRs own qualify and meet. AEs own discover to close. Nobody
   owns onboard, the founder does it when he has time. Nobody owns renew.
3. A RACI, explained simply. It's a table saying who is Responsible (does it), Accountable (answers for it), Consulted and
   Informed. For this track you only need the R and the A. One R and one A per step.
4. Screen demo. In Google Sheets, make a table with steps down the side and roles across the top. Type R or A in the cells.
   Then click Format, Conditional formatting, and colour any row with no A red.
5. What usually shows up red. Onboarding, renewals, and "lead that came in but nobody called".
6. The fix isn't always a new hire. Often it's one line in the sales bible ("the AE who closed the deal runs the kick off
   call") or one automation ("when a deal is won, create an onboarding task for CS").

**Do it now.** (10 min) Build the steps and roles table for your team. Mark every step with no clear owner in red.

#### Lesson 1.3 Handoffs between marketing, SDR, AE and CS (7 min)

**In plain words.** A handoff is the moment one person passes a lead or a customer to the next. It's the riskiest moment in
selling, because information gets lost and nobody feels responsible for a few days. This lesson shows how to write each
handoff down so nothing drops.

**You'll be able to.**
- Name the four main handoffs in a sales team
- Write the minimum information that must travel with each
- Set a time limit for each handoff

**What's shown.**
1. The four handoffs. Marketing to SDR (a lead came in). SDR to AE (a meeting is booked). AE to CS (the deal is won). CS
   back to AE (an upsell or renewal opportunity).
2. For each one, three things written down. What triggers it, what must be passed on, and how fast the next person must act.
3. Routeful's SDR to AE handoff before. Sanne books a meeting and puts it in Lotte's calendar. Lotte walks in knowing only
   the company name.
4. After. Sanne fills four fields in the CRM before the meeting counts as booked. Fleet size, current planning tool, who
   will attend, and why they agreed to meet. The meeting only moves to Meeting booked once all four are filled.
5. Time limits. A lead from the website must be called within one working hour. A won deal gets a kick off call within five
   working days. Write these as numbers, because "quickly" means something different to everyone.
6. Screen demo in HubSpot. Click CRM, then Deals, then open any deal. Show the fields on the left side. Explain that a GTM
   engineer can make some of these required before a deal changes stage. You'll do that in Module 4.

   filled.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write your SDR to AE handoff (or the one that hurts most) in three lines. The trigger, the
information, the time limit.

#### Lesson 1.4 The loyalty side after the sale (6 min)

**In plain words.** Most sales teams stop thinking when the contract is signed. But a customer who stays and buys more is
worth far more than a new one. Loyalty or retention flows are the messages and steps after the sale that keep customers
happy, using the product and buying again.

**You'll be able to.**
- Map the steps from signed contract to renewal
- Name the warning signs that a customer might leave
- Pick one loyalty flow to add to your operating model

**What's shown.**
1. Two words. **Churn** means customers who stop buying. **Customer health** is a score that shows whether a customer is
   likely to stay or leave.
2. The after sale steps. Kick off call. First value (the moment they actually get something from you). Regular check in.
   Renewal conversation. Upsell.
3. Warning signs, as a list. Usage drops. The person who bought from you leaves their company. Support tickets pile up.
   They stop replying.
4. Routeful example. A customer's planners stop logging in for three weeks. Nobody notices until the renewal is refused.
5. A simple loyalty flow, drawn as boxes. When logins drop by half for two weeks, create a task for the account owner to
   call. When the main contact changes job (spotted on LinkedIn), send a welcome note to their replacement.
6. Who owns it. If you have no CS person, write the AE's name in that box. An owner who also sells is better than no owner.

**Do it now.** (5 min) Write the three warning signs that would tell you a customer of yours is about to leave, and who
would notice each one today.

#### Lesson 1.5 Where deals leak (7 min)

**In plain words.** A leak is a place where deals or customers fall out of the process for a reason you could fix. Most
teams have two or three big ones. This lesson shows how to find them in your own numbers and mark them on your operating
model.

**You'll be able to.**
- Calculate the conversion rate between each pair of stages
- Find the biggest leak with numbers
- Mark leaks on your operating model diagram

**What's shown.**
1. **Conversion rate**, explained. The share of deals that move from one stage to the next. If 30 deals reached Discovery
   done and 18 reached Demo done, the conversion is 18 divided by 30, which is 60%.
2. Open the Routeful pipeline in Sheets. Add a small table with the stages down the side. Next to each, use `COUNTIF` on a
   "furthest stage reached" column to count deals that got at least that far.
3. In the next column type `=B3/B2` (next stage count divided by this stage count) and drag it down. Format as percent.
4. Read the table. The lowest percentage is the biggest leak. Show it with a red fill.
5. Three common leak types. Speed (leads wait too long). Fit (wrong companies get in). Process (a step everyone skips, like
   sending the proposal).
6. Leaks after the sale count too. Customers who don't renew are a leak, even if they're not in the pipeline.
7. Why you look at numbers first and ask the team second. People remember the dramatic lost deal. The numbers show the
   quiet leak that costs the most.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Build the conversion table on your pipeline export. Write down your two lowest conversions.

#### Lesson 2.1 What a sales bible is, and stages with exit criteria (7 min)

**In plain words.** A sales bible (or playbook) is the written way your team sells. Who you sell to, how, what to say, and
what each stage means. Its backbone is the list of stages, and for each stage the exit criteria, which are the things that
must be true before a deal moves to the next stage.

**You'll be able to.**
- Explain what a sales bible is for
- Write exit criteria that two people would judge the same way
- Spot a vague exit criterion and rewrite it

**What's shown.**
1. Why it exists. When every rep decides for themselves what "Demo done" means, the pipeline is a collection of opinions,
   and your forecast is too.
2. **Stage** means a step in the pipeline, like Meeting booked. **Exit criteria** means what must be true before a deal
   leaves that stage.
3. The test for a good exit criterion. Could two people look at the same deal and agree whether it's true? If not,
   rewrite it.
4. Routeful's Discovery done, bad version. "We understand their needs." Good version. "We know their fleet size, the
   planning tool they use today, who signs, and the date they want to start. All four are in the CRM."
5. Walk through every Routeful stage on screen with a good exit criterion for each. New (company fits the ICP, one named
   contact). Meeting booked (date in calendar, four handoff fields filled). Discovery done (four facts above). Demo done
   (demo held with the person who signs or their direct report, next step agreed with a date). Proposal sent (proposal sent
   by email, price included, follow up date set). Won (contract signed). Lost (loss reason chosen from the list).
6. Exit criteria should be visible in the CRM. If you can't see it in a field, you can't check it, and the team can't
   either.
7. Keep it short. Three to five criteria per stage at most. More than that and nobody reads them.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write your current stages down. Next to each, write one exit criterion that passes the "two people
agree" test.

#### Lesson 2.2 Definitions everyone uses the same way (6 min)

**In plain words.** Words like "lead", "qualified" and "opportunity" mean different things to different people. A
definition list fixes the meaning of the five to ten words your team argues about, so reports and conversations line up.

**You'll be able to.**
- Find the words your team uses differently
- Write a one sentence definition for each
- Put definitions where people will see them

**What's shown.**
1. The test. Ask three team members separately "what's a qualified lead?" and write their answers down. Show three
   different Routeful answers on screen.
2. The usual suspects. Lead, contact, qualified, opportunity, decision maker, pipeline, closed lost, churned.
3. Routeful's definitions. **Lead** is a person at a company that fits the ICP, with no conversation yet. **Qualified**
   means we've spoken, they have a fleet of 15 or more trucks, plan routes by hand or in an old TMS, and want to change
   within 12 months. **Opportunity** is a qualified company with a meeting booked. **Decision maker** is the person who
   signs the contract.
4. One sentence each, no examples needed in the definition itself. Examples go below it.
5. Where to put them. The first page of the sales bible, and in the CRM as the description on each field. Screen demo in
   HubSpot. Click Settings (the gear icon), then Properties, search for the field, click it, and type the definition into
   the Description box. It now shows when anyone hovers over the field.
6. When the team disagrees, you decide, write it down, and review it in a month. A decided definition beats a perfect one
   argued about forever.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Message three team members the same question ("what makes a lead qualified, in one sentence?").
Compare the answers when they come back.

#### Lesson 2.3 Playbooks per segment and objection handling (8 min)

**In plain words.** A segment is a group of companies inside your ICP that you treat the same way. A playbook is the short
plan for selling to one segment. Who to talk to, what problem to lead with, which proof to use, and how to answer the
objections that come up.

**You'll be able to.**
- Split your market into two or three segments
- Write a one page playbook for each
- Write objection answers from what actually worked

**What's shown.**
1. **Segment**, defined, with Routeful's three. Small fleets (15 to 40 trucks, owner decides). Mid fleets (40 to 150
   trucks, operations director decides). Large fleets (150 or more, head of planning plus IT).
2. The playbook page, five boxes. Who we talk to. The problem we lead with. The proof we use. The usual objections and
   answers. The next step we ask for.
3. Filled in for Routeful small fleets. The owner. "Your planners spend every morning rebuilding the plan in Excel." A
   short story from a similar customer. Objection "we're too small for software". Next step, a 20 minute screen share of
   their own routes.
4. The message structure to teach your team, shown as six numbered lines. A greeting with a specific observation about
   their company. The problem you noticed. What it costs them. Why it matters now. One line of proof. A low effort
   question (offer to show something, not "book a call"). One problem from start to finish.
5. Objection handling. An **objection** is a reason a buyer gives for not moving forward. Write the objection in the
   buyer's words, then the answer your best rep actually used, then the question to ask next.
6. Routeful objection on screen. "We already have a TMS." Answer. "Most of our customers do. We plan the routes, your TMS
   still handles the paperwork. Which part of planning takes your team the longest today?"
7. Where answers come from. Not from you. From won deals and from your best reps. That's what the team workshop is for.
8. Honesty rule. No made up proof, no fake "Re:" subject lines, no invented personal connections. If you can't back a claim
   up, it doesn't go in the bible.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write the top three objections your team hears, in the buyer's own words. Leave the answers blank
for now.

#### Lesson 2.5 Keeping it alive (6 min)

#### Lesson 3.1 Agreeing the ICP from won deals (8 min)

**In plain words.** An ICP (ideal customer profile) is a description of the companies most likely to buy from you and stay.
Most teams have one that someone wrote years ago. This lesson builds yours from evidence, by comparing the deals you won

**You'll be able to.**

#### Lesson 3.2 Territories (6 min)

**In plain words.** A territory is the set of accounts a seller owns. Good territories are fair (each seller gets a similar

**You'll be able to.**

- Balance territories by opportunity, not just count

**What's shown.**

#### Lesson 3.3 Signals that trigger action (7 min)

**In plain words.** A signal is something that happens which makes a company more likely to buy now. Hiring for a certain
role, a new boss, opening a new location, a visit to your pricing page. Signals tell your team who to contact this week
instead of working the list from A to Z.

**You'll be able to.**
- Pick three to five signals that matter for your market
- Decide what action each signal triggers
- Judge whether a signal is worth tracking

**What's shown.**
1. **Signal** and **intent** (signs a company is actively looking for something like what you sell), one sentence each.
2. Routeful's signals. A job ad for a transport planner (they're struggling to plan). A new depot opening. A new operations
   director (new bosses change tools in their first months). A visit to the pricing page. A customer contact moving to a
   new company.
3. The signal table, four columns. Signal. Where we see it. What it triggers. Who acts and how fast.
4. Example row. "Job ad for transport planner. Seen on job boards, checked weekly. Triggers a message about planning time.
   SDR in that territory within 2 working days."
5. How a GTM engineer would collect these, shown briefly. Clay can watch job boards and company news. HubSpot can track
   website visits from known contacts. A tool like n8n (which connects apps so one can trigger another) can post a signal
   into your team chat. You don't build this, you decide what's collected.

   it's a guess. Start with three signals, not thirty.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write three signals for your market with where you'd see each one. Check one won deal from last

#### Lesson 3.4 Routing rules (6 min)

**In plain words.** Routing means automatically giving a lead to the right person. Without rules, leads go to whoever sees
them first, or to nobody. This lesson shows how to write routing rules in plain sentences that a GTM engineer (or HubSpot)
can turn into an automation.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write routing rules as "if this, then that" sentences
- Cover the cases where no rule fits
- See how a routing rule looks inside HubSpot

**What's shown.**
1. The shape of a rule. If (where it came from, what kind of company, which territory), then (who owns it, what task they
   get, how fast).
2. Routeful's rules on screen. "If a website demo request comes from a company in the Netherlands, assign it to Lotte and
   create a call task due within 1 working hour." "If the company has fewer than 15 trucks, send it to the nurture list,
   not a seller." "If the company is already owned by someone, keep the owner."
3. The catch all. "If no rule matches, assign it to the manager, who assigns it within 4 working hours." Without this, the
   odd leads are lost.
4. Round robin, explained. Giving leads in turn to each person in a group, so it's fair. Used when territory doesn't decide.
5. Screen demo in HubSpot. Click Automation, then Workflows, then Create workflow, From scratch, Contact based. Set the
   trigger to "Form submission". Add an action "Rotate record to owner" and choose the team members, or an If/then branch
   on Country. Add "Create task". Explain that the exact options depend on your HubSpot plan, so check which plan has
   workflows.
6. If you don't have workflows, the rules still go in the sales bible and the manager routes by hand twice a day until
   you do.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write your routing rules as sentences. At least three rules and a catch all.

#### Lesson 3.5 Speed to lead (7 min)

**In plain words.** Speed to lead is how fast someone follows up after a lead comes in. The faster the follow up, the more
likely you reach the person while they still care. This lesson shows how to measure your team's speed and set a target.

**You'll be able to.**
- Measure your current speed to lead
- Set a target the team can hit
- Set up a simple alert when a lead waits too long

**What's shown.**
1. Why speed matters, in plain terms. Someone who just filled in a form is thinking about the problem now. Tomorrow they're
   in meetings. (If you want a figure for your boss, look up current research and check its date. Don't quote numbers you
   can't source.)
2. How to measure it. The time between "lead created" and "first call or email logged". In HubSpot, contacts have a create
   date and a "last contacted" or first activity date.
3. Screen demo. Export new contacts from last month with Create date and First outreach date (or Last contacted). In
   Sheets, add a column `=(D2-C2)*24` to get hours. Then `=MEDIAN(E2:E)` for the typical wait.
4. **Median**, explained. The middle value when you sort them. Better than the average here because one lead that waited
   three weeks would distort the average.
5. Set the target as a rule. "Inbound demo requests get a first call within 1 working hour, other inbound within 1 working
   day."
6. An alert. In a HubSpot workflow, add a delay of 1 hour, then an If/then branch "Last contacted is unknown", then "Send
   internal notification" to the manager. Without workflows, a saved view of new leads with no activity, checked at 11:00
   and 16:00.
7. Speed counts for outbound replies too. When someone replies yes to a cold message, the clock starts. Astra's own rule is
   a first message within 2 days of a connection being accepted.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Measure the median speed to lead for last month's inbound leads, or the Routeful pack's inbound
rows.

#### Lesson 4.1 Why reps don't update the CRM, and the minimum data you need (7 min)

**In plain words.** CRM hygiene means keeping CRM data correct, complete and without duplicates. Reps don't skip CRM updates

**You'll be able to.**

#### Lesson 5.1 The five numbers a manager needs (7 min)

**In plain words.** A dashboard is a page of charts and numbers that shows how things are going. Most dashboards show too
much, so nobody reads them. This lesson gives you the five numbers that tell a manager almost everything, plus the weekly
scorecard that feeds them.

**You'll be able to.**
- Name the five numbers and what each one tells you
- Tell a leading number from a lagging one
- Set up a weekly scorecard

**What's shown.**
1. The five numbers. **Pipeline created** (value of new deals added this month). **Pipeline coverage** (open pipeline for the
   period divided by the target). **Conversion by stage**. **Win rate**. **Sales cycle** (average days from created to won).
2. What each tells you, in one line. Are we filling the pipe? Is there enough to hit target? Where do we leak? Do we win
   when it matters? Are we getting faster or slower?
3. Leading and lagging. A **lagging** number tells you what already happened (revenue won). A **leading** number tells you
   what's coming (meetings booked this week). You manage the leading ones.
4. Astra's weekly scorecard as an example of leading numbers. Invites or messages sent, accepts, first messages sent within
   2 days, positive replies, meetings booked, meetings held with the decision maker, proposals sent, wins and value.
5. Pipeline coverage worked through. Routeful's monthly target and open pipeline closing this month. Coverage of 3 means
   three euros of pipeline for every euro of target. The right level depends on your win rate. If you win 30%, you need
   more than 3. Work it out as 1 divided by win rate.
6. Activity that matters. Count meetings held and proposals sent, not calls made or emails sent. Busy isn't the same as
   progress.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write your five numbers for last month from your Assignment 0 sheet. Where you can't calculate one,
write what's missing.

#### Lesson 5.2 Pipeline analysis, conversion by stage and stage aging (8 min)

**In plain words.** Pipeline analysis means looking at all your open deals to see if they're real, moving and enough.
Conversion by stage shows where they drop out. Stage aging shows how long deals have sat in their current stage, which is
the fastest way to spot deals that are quietly dead.

**You'll be able to.**
- Calculate conversion by stage for a period
- Calculate stage aging for every open deal
- Flag deals that are older than normal for their stage

**What's shown.**
1. **Pipeline** (all open deals and where each one is), one sentence.
2. Conversion by stage, built in Lesson 1.5. Now compare two quarters side by side to see if a leak is getting better.
3. **Stage aging**, defined. Days since the deal entered its current stage. In Sheets with a "last stage change date" column,
   type `=TODAY()-H2` and drag down.
4. Normal time per stage. For won deals, work out the typical days spent in each stage (the median). Routeful example,
   Demo done to Proposal sent usually takes about a week for won deals.
5. Flag the stuck ones. Add a column `=IF(I2>2*normal_days,"STUCK","")`. A deal at twice the normal time in its stage is
   at risk.
6. In HubSpot. Deals have "time in stage" properties (names vary by version, search for "time in" in the properties list).
   Add them as columns in a deals view.
7. The Routeful pipeline shows several deals in Proposal sent for over 60 days. They're still counted in the forecast. They
   shouldn't be.
8. Pipeline created by source and by rep, as a pivot table. Shows who's filling the pipe and from where.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Add the stage aging column to your open deals, or the Routeful pipeline. Count the deals at twice
the normal time.

#### Lesson 5.3 Forecast categories (8 min)

**In plain words.** A forecast is your best estimate of what will be sold this month or quarter. Instead of one number from
gut feel, you put every deal into one of three categories. Commit, Best case and Pipeline. Then the forecast is built from
deals you can name and defend.

**You'll be able to.**
- Put any deal in the right category using clear rules
- Build a forecast from categories
- Compare it with a weighted forecast and explain the gap

**What's shown.**
1. The three categories, with Routeful's rules. **Commit** means you'd bet your bonus on it. Proposal accepted verbally, the
   signer is engaged, close date agreed with the buyer, no open blocker. **Best case** means it could close this month if
   things go well. Proposal sent, signer met, close date in this month, one open question. **Pipeline** means open and real,
   but not this month.
2. The rules go in the sales bible, next to the stages, so reps and manager categorise the same way.
3. The forecast. Commit total is your call. Commit plus Best case is your upside. Report both.
4. A **weighted forecast**, explained. Each deal's value times the chance of winning it at its stage, added up. The chance
   per stage comes from your conversion data (for example, deals at Proposal sent historically win 45% of the time).
5. Screen demo in Sheets. Add a column "Stage win chance" with a lookup (`=VLOOKUP(F2,stage_table,2,FALSE)`), then a column
   `=G2*J2` for weighted value. Sum it.
6. Compare the two. If your Commit is far above the weighted number, you're optimistic. If far below, check whether reps
   are sandbagging (keeping good deals out of Commit to look good later).
7. Add a forecast category field to deals in HubSpot (Settings, Properties, Create property, dropdown with the three
   values). HubSpot also has a built in forecast category on some plans, check yours.
8. Track your accuracy. At month end, write what you forecast and what closed. Over three months you'll see your own bias.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Put 10 of your open deals (or 10 Routeful deals) into the three categories using the rules. Write
the totals.

#### Lesson 5.5 Running a pipeline review meeting (7 min)

**In plain words.** A pipeline review is a weekly meeting where you and the team go through open deals using the dashboard.
Done badly, it's an hour of reps reading out every deal. Done well, it's 30 minutes on the deals that need a decision.

**You'll be able to.**
- Run a 30 minute pipeline review from the dashboard
- Ask the questions that move deals forward
- Update the forecast at the end of the meeting

**What's shown.**
1. Before the meeting, reps update their deals by Monday 9:00. The hygiene check runs at 9:00. The review is Monday 10:00.
2. The agenda. 5 minutes, the five numbers and anything changed since last week. 15 minutes, only three kinds of deal.
   Commit deals (are they still commit?), stuck deals (twice normal age), and big deals. 5 minutes, deals to close out (mark
   as lost, with a reason). 5 minutes, forecast confirmed.
3. The questions that work. "What's the next step and when?" "Who signs, and have we met them?" "What would make this slip?"
   "If this were your money, would you still call it commit?"
4. Questions to avoid. "How's it going?" "Any updates?" They invite stories, not facts.
5. Closing out dead deals counts as a win. A clean pipeline makes the forecast honest.
6. Coaching moments get noted, not done in the meeting. "Lotte, let's look at that demo call on Thursday."

#### Lesson 6.3 Using Claude to summarise transcripts, with checking (7 min)

**In plain words.** Claude (an AI assistant) can read a long transcript and summarise it in a minute. But AI can get things
wrong or make them up. So every summary you use has to quote the transcript, and you check the quotes. This lesson gives you
the prompt and the checking routine.

**You'll be able to.**
- Use a prompt that makes Claude quote its source
- Check a summary in three minutes
- Know what not to paste into an AI tool

**What's shown.**
1. **Prompt**, the instruction you give an AI. **AI agent**, an AI set up to do a task in several steps. You only need
   prompts here.
2. Data first. Check your company allows call transcripts in the AI tool you use. Remove personal details you don't need
   (phone numbers, private remarks). Not legal advice, check your policy.
3. The prompt on screen, copied into the template.
   ```
   You are reviewing a sales call transcript. Use only the transcript below.
   1. List the buyer's main problems. For each, give an exact quote from the transcript.
   2. List anything that stopped or slowed the deal. Exact quote for each.
   3. Which of these tags apply? [paste your tag list]. Give a quote for each tag you choose.

   [paste]
   ```

#### Lesson 7.1 Adoption and change management (7 min)

**In plain words.** Change management means helping people move from the old way to the new way so it sticks. Most new
processes fail because people slip back after two weeks. The design is rarely the problem. This lesson gives you the steps
that make it stick.

**You'll be able to.**
- Plan how you'll introduce each change
- Spot early that people are slipping back
- Handle the team members who resist

**What's shown.**
1. Why changes fail. Too many at once. No reason given. The manager stops checking after week two. The old way still works.
2. The order for each change. Tell them why. Show them how (5 minutes, on screen). Make the old way harder (for example,
   required fields mean they can't skip). Check it weekly. Praise the first people who do it well, by name.
3. One change at a time per area. Don't launch the bible, new routing and a new dashboard in the same week.
4. Adoption signals you can measure. Share of deals meeting the stage exit criteria. Hygiene score trend. Bible comments
   per month. Dashboard views (HubSpot and Looker Studio show who opened a dashboard).
5. The person who resists. Ask what they'd lose with the change. Often it's a real problem (more clicks, less freedom). Fix
   what you can, explain what you can't.
6. Your own behaviour. If you ask for updates in Slack instead of reading the dashboard, the team learns the dashboard
   doesn't matter.

**Do it now.** (5 min) List every change from your assignments so far. Put them in the order you'd introduce them.

#### Lesson 7.2 Sprints and what to automate first (7 min)

**In plain words.** A sprint is a fixed block of time, usually two weeks, with a short list of things to get done. Working
in sprints keeps the rollout moving without overwhelming the team. And it forces you to choose what to automate first.

**You'll be able to.**
- Plan the rollout in six two week sprints
- Score automation ideas to pick the first ones
- Write a sprint goal that can be checked

**What's shown.**
1. **Sprint**, defined. Two weeks, three to five tasks, one goal. At the end, a 20 minute look back. What worked, what to
   change.
2. The scoring table for automation ideas. Hours saved per week (1 to 5). How often errors happen today (1 to 5). How easy
   it is to build (1 to 5). How much the team will notice (1 to 5). Add them up.
3. Routeful's list scored on screen. Stale deal alert scores high (easy, saves Eva's Sundays). Inbound routing scores high.
   Full enrichment of all accounts scores lower (more cost, less urgent).
4. What usually comes first. Required fields per stage, a stale deal alert, inbound routing, email and meeting logging.
   These are cheap and reps feel them quickly.
5. What usually waits. Complex scoring, AI research agents, full multichannel sequences. Good, but they need clean data
   first.
6. A good sprint goal. "By 20 March, every inbound demo request is routed and called within 1 working hour, measured on the
   dashboard." A bad one. "Improve routing."

**Do it now.** (10 min) Score five automation ideas from your earlier assignments with the four questions.

#### Lesson 7.3 Build, buy or hire a GTM engineer (7 min)

**In plain words.** You have three ways to get the systems built. Build it yourself or with someone already on your team.
Buy it from an agency or freelancer who builds it for you. Or hire a GTM engineer as an employee. This lesson gives you a
simple cost comparison so you can decide with numbers.

**You'll be able to.**
- Describe the three options and when each fits
- Fill in a cost comparison for your team
- Explain the choice to your boss

**What's shown.**
1. **Build** (you or someone on the team). Low cash cost, high time cost, risky if that person leaves. Fits small teams with
   one technical person.
2. **Buy** (agency or freelancer). Faster, someone has done it before. Costs a project fee. You need someone to keep it
   running afterwards. Fits teams that need the base built quickly.
3. **Hire** (a GTM engineer on staff). Ongoing salary, but they keep improving the system. Hiring takes months. Fits teams
   big enough to keep one person busy.
4. The cost comparison template on screen, rows for each option. Setup cost. Monthly cost (salary, fees, tools). Hours of
   your own time per month, times what your hour is worth. Months until it's running. Risk notes.

   reports) and note the date. Agency fees, ask two for a quote. Tool costs, check each tool's pricing page. Never guess.

#### Lesson 7.4 Measuring payback (6 min)

**In plain words.** Payback means how long it takes for what you gain to cover what you spent. Your boss will ask. This
lesson shows how to estimate it before you start and measure it after, using numbers from your own dashboard.

**You'll be able to.**
- Estimate payback in months
- Pick the before and after numbers to compare
- Report payback honestly, including when it's unclear

**What's shown.**
1. Two sources of gain. Time saved (hours per week times people times hourly cost). More revenue (better conversion or more
   pipeline, times average deal value times win rate).
2. The formula. Payback months equals total cost divided by monthly gain. Shown in the template.
3. A Routeful worked example with illustrative numbers. Time saved, Eva's 3 hours a week plus 2 hours per rep a week on
   admin. Revenue gain, Demo done to Proposal sent improving from 50% to 60%. Work through the sheet.
4. Before numbers come from Assignment 0 and the dashboard. That's why you wrote them down at the start.
5. Honest reporting. Separate what you measured (hours saved, conversion change) from what you estimate (revenue it caused).
   Other things change at the same time, so say so.
6. Astra's own discipline. Raka reports what the engine produced (replies, yes rate, meetings) and doesn't claim revenue
   that hasn't closed yet. Do the same with your boss.

**Do it now.** (5 min) Write the three before numbers you'll compare at day 90.

# Final exam

**When.** After all module quizzes and assignments are passed, and after the practical test.
**Length.** 25 questions, 30 minutes, timed. About 60% knowledge questions (15), 40% scenarios (10).
**Pass mark.** 80%, which is 20 of 25.
**Retakes.** Two, at least 48 hours apart, from a different question pool (README section 5.3).

### Knowledge questions

1. What is GTM engineering?
   A. Hiring more sellers
   B. Building the systems behind finding and selling to customers so selling runs on a machine instead of on memory
   C. Product development
   D. Marketing design
   **Answer.** B. **Why.** Systems, not headcount. **Module.** 0
2. Which is the best request to a GTM engineer?
   A. "Clean up the CRM"
   B. "When a deal has no activity for 14 days, create a task for the owner, and show me a weekly count"
   C. "Make sales faster"
   D. "Add AI"
   **Answer.** B. **Why.** Trigger, action and a number to check. **Module.** 0
3. In an operating model, how many people should be accountable for each step?
   A. None
   B. One
   C. Two
   D. Everyone
   **Answer.** B. **Why.** Two owners means nobody does it. **Module.** 1
4. What three things should be written down for every handoff?
   A. Date, time, place
   B. Trigger, information passed on, time limit
   C. Owner, budget, tool
   D. Name, email, phone
   **Answer.** B. **Why.** So nothing drops and the next person acts fast. **Module.** 1
5. True or false. Loyalty flows start after the contract is signed.
   **Answer.** True. **Why.** They keep customers using and buying after the sale. **Module.** 1
6. What makes an exit criterion good?
   A. It's long and detailed
   B. Two people looking at the same deal would agree whether it's true
   C. It matches a sales book
   D. The manager wrote it
   **Answer.** B. **Why.** That's the test from the sales bible module. **Module.** 2
7. True or false. A sales bible works best when the manager writes it alone and hands it over finished.
   **Answer.** False. **Why.** The team writing it with you is what makes them use it. **Module.** 2
8. An ICP should be built mainly from
   A. Your won and lost deals
   B. A competitor's website
   C. The largest companies in your country
   D. Marketing's wish list
   **Answer.** A. **Why.** Evidence beats opinion. **Module.** 3
9. What's a signal?
   A. A company's logo
   B. Something that happens which makes a company more likely to buy now
   C. A CRM field
   D. A sales stage
   **Answer.** B. **Why.** Hiring, new boss, new site, a pricing page visit. **Module.** 3
10. Speed to lead is
    A. How fast a deal closes
    B. How fast someone follows up after a lead comes in
    C. How fast a rep types
    D. How fast leads are imported
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** Measured from lead created to first contact. **Module.** 3
11. Which is the minimum data test for a CRM field?
    A. Is it in the default CRM setup?
    B. Which report or decision uses it?
    C. Do reps like it?
    D. Is it free text?
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** If nothing uses it, don't require it. **Module.** 4
12. Why must commission data fields be treated differently?
    A. They're private to each rep
    B. They change more often than other fields
    C. Pay depends on them, so owner, amount and close date must be right and protected
    D. Finance doesn't use the CRM
    **Answer.** C. **Why.** One wrong payment costs a lot of trust. **Module.** 4
13. Your win rate is 20%. Roughly what pipeline coverage do you need?
    A. 2
    B. 3
    C. 5
    D. 20
    **Answer.** C. **Why.** 1 divided by 0.2. **Module.** 5
14. Which three forecast categories does this track use?
    A. Hot, warm, cold
    B. Commit, Best case, Pipeline
    C. Won, lost, open
    D. A, B, C
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** Each with written rules. **Module.** 5
15. What must every point in an AI call summary include, so you can check it?
    A. A score
    B. An exact quote from the transcript
    C. An emoji
    D. A recommendation
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** You search for the quote to prove it's real. **Module.** 6

### Scenario questions

16. Lotte's deals reach Demo done as often as the team's, but far fewer of hers reach Proposal sent. Where do you look first?
    A. Her discovery calls
    B. Her demo calls and what's agreed at the end of them
    C. Her email signature
    D. Her territory size
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** The leak is between Demo done and Proposal sent. **Module.** 6
17. A rep marks a deal Commit. The signer has never been met and the deal has been in Proposal sent for 70 days, against a
    normal 10. What do you do?
    A. Keep it in Commit
    B. Move it out of Commit and ask in the review what would make it real
    C. Delete it
    D. Double the amount
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** It fails the commit rules and is stuck. **Module.** 5
18. Your CRM shows "NL", "Holland", "Netherlands" and "Nederland" in the Country field. What's the lasting fix?
    A. Fix them by hand every month
    B. Change Country to a dropdown, clean existing values once, and own the rule
    C. Delete the field
    D. Ask reps to be careful
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** A fixed list stops it happening again. **Module.** 4
19. Three reps give three different answers to "what's a qualified lead?" What's your next step?
    A. Accept that everyone's different
    B. Write one sentence definition, decide where they disagree, put it in the bible and the CRM field description
    C. Stop using the word
    D. Hire a new SDR
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** One decided definition, visible where they work. **Module.** 2
20. Inbound demo requests wait a median of 19 hours for a first call. What's the first change?
    A. A routing rule with a 1 working hour target and an alert when it's missed
    B. More ads
    C. A new CRM
    D. A longer form
    **Answer.** A. **Why.** Speed to lead is fixed by routing and an alert. **Module.** 3
21. Most lost deals in your review mention "IT must approve", but the CRM loss reason says "Price". What do you change?
    A. Nothing, the CRM is right
    B. Add "IT contact named" to Discovery done exit criteria and an IT objection answer to the bible
    C. Lower prices
    D. Stop selling to companies with IT
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** Feed the real reason back into the process. **Module.** 6
22. A team of 5 sellers with no technical person needs routing and hygiene automation running within a month. Which option
    fits best?
    A. Hire a full time GTM engineer and wait for them
    B. Buy the first build from an agency or freelancer, then have someone on the team maintain it
    C. The manager learns to code at weekends
    D. Do nothing until next year
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** Fast and sized to the team. **Module.** 7
23. You launched the sales bible two weeks ago. Exit criteria are met on fewer deals each week. What's the most likely cause
    and fix?
    A. The bible is wrong, rewrite it
    B. People are slipping back. Check weekly, make the old way harder with required fields, praise those doing it well
    C. Fire the rep
    D. Launch three more changes
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** Adoption fails more often than design. **Module.** 7
24. Claude's summary of a call says the buyer "chose a competitor because of price". You can't find that quote in the
    transcript. What do you do?
    A. Use it, Claude is usually right
    B. Strike the point out and read that part of the call yourself
    C. Ask the rep to confirm from memory
    D. Tag it price anyway
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** No quote, no claim. **Module.** 6
25. Your boss asks for payback on the rollout after 90 days. Hours saved are measured, revenue gain is partly due to a price
    change in the same period. How do you report it?
    A. Claim all revenue growth as payback
    B. Report measured hours saved and conversion change, and state that the revenue gain is an estimate with other causes
    C. Don't report it
    D. Report only the cost
    **Answer.** B. **Why.** Separate what's measured from what's estimated. **Module.** 7

# Capstone. Your GTM operating system

**Goal.** Bring everything together into one 10 slide deck and present it to your own team or your boss. It shows the
system you've designed and the plan to make it real.
**Time.** 3 to 4 hours to prepare, 15 minutes to present.
**You need.** Assignments 1 to 7 (passed), the practical test, template `T3-CAP Capstone deck` (Google Slides, 10 slides with
titles ready), your dashboard live with current numbers.

### The brief

You've designed your team's GTM operating system over this course. Now you have to get it agreed and used. Build a 10 slide
deck that a busy person (your boss, or your team) understands in 15 minutes, and that ends with a clear decision or
commitment.

**Who you present to.** Pick one. Your boss, if you need budget or approval (the build, buy or hire decision). Your team, if
the main job is getting them to use it. Write who on slide 1.

### The 10 slides

| Slide | Title | What goes on it | From |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Where we are today | Team, the before numbers, the biggest leak, in one picture | Assignment 0 |
| 2 | How revenue moves through us | The operating model diagram with owners and the two biggest leaks circled | Assignment 1 |
| 3 | How we sell, written down | Stages and exit criteria table, link to the sales bible, what the team changed | Assignment 2 |
| 4 | Who we sell to and who gets each lead | ICP with evidence, territories, routing rules and speed to lead target | Assignment 3 |
| 5 | Data we can trust | Hygiene rules with owners, the audit before and (if you have it) after | Assignment 4 |
| 6 | The dashboard | Screenshot with the five numbers circled, how the pipeline review runs | Assignment 5 |
| 7 | Next month's forecast | Commit, Best case, weighted, the reasoning and last month's accuracy if you have it | Assignment 5 |
| 8 | Why we win and lose | The three patterns with counts and quotes, the changes they led to, the coaching plan in one line | Assignment 6 |
| 9 | The next 90 days | Six sprints on one slide, build, buy or hire and its 12 month cost, payback with the honest line | Assignment 7 |
| 10 | What I'm asking for | One clear ask. Approval, budget, or the team's commitment to three specific behaviours, with dates | All |

### Steps

1. Open `T3-CAP Capstone deck`, click File, Make a copy, name it "[Team] GTM operating system".
2. For each slide, copy the key visual from the assignment. To copy a chart from Sheets, click it, click the three dots, Copy
   chart, then paste in Slides and choose "Link to spreadsheet" so it updates.
3. Update every number to today. Pull a fresh export or refresh the dashboard. Write the date in the corner of slides 1, 6
   and 7.
4. One message per slide. Write the slide title as a claim ("Half our demos never get a proposal"), not a label ("Demos").
5. Put detail in the speaker notes (View, Show speaker notes), not on the slide.
6. Remove any personal data. No contact names or emails on slides. Rep names are fine for your own team, but take them off
   coaching details if presenting to your boss.
7. Rehearse once with a timer. 15 minutes maximum.
8. Present it. To your team in a team meeting, or to your boss in a booked slot. Ask one person to note questions.
9. Write a short reflection (half a page). What they asked, what they agreed, what you'll change.

### Hand in

1. The deck (view link).
2. The half page reflection.
3. Self learn, Completed level, mark it yourself against the rubric. For Certified, buy the capstone review (€149) and Raka
   marks it from the deck and a recording of the presentation (or a 15 minute recording of you presenting it to camera).
4. 1:1, send the deck 48 hours before S6 and present it live in S6.

### Marking rubric (16 points, pass at 12, no 0 on a must)

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 | Must? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before numbers and biggest leak, from data | Missing or guessed | Partly from data | All from data, dated | Yes |
| Sales bible with testable exit criteria, team involvement shown | Missing | Bible shown, involvement unclear | Testable criteria and the team's changes named | No |
| ICP, territory and routing backed by evidence | Missing | Present without evidence | Won and lost evidence, routing with target | No |
| Hygiene rules owned, audit shown | Missing | Rules without owners or audit | Owned rules, audit counts | No |
| Dashboard and forecast, categories with reasons | Missing | Dashboard or forecast only | Both, reasoning clear | Yes |
| Win and loss patterns with counts, checked quotes, changes | Missing | Patterns without evidence | Counts, checked quotes, changes and coaching | No |
| 90 day plan with sourced build, buy or hire cost and honest payback | Missing | Plan without costs or measures | Six measurable sprints, sourced cost, measured and estimated separated | Yes |
| Clear ask and presentation quality | No ask, or over time | Ask vague, or slides cluttered | One clear dated ask, claim titles, within 15 minutes | No |

**Good example.** Routeful's deck, presented by Eva to the managing director. Slide 1 title, "We lose half our deals between
the demo and the proposal". Slide 7 shows Commit €37k, Best case €55k more, and "last month I forecast €40k, we closed €33k".
Slide 9 recommends buying the first build and has every figure sourced and dated. Slide 10, "I'm asking for approval of the
first build and 4 hours a week of Sanne's time to maintain it, decision by 30 November." Reflection, "The MD asked how we'd
know it worked. I pointed to the Demo done to Proposal sent conversion on the dashboard and agreed we'd look at it at day 60."
(Illustrative numbers.)

**Bad example.** 34 slides, the first eight about GTM engineering in general, numbers without dates, no forecast reasoning,
and a final slide saying "Questions?". It fails because the system isn't shown on the team's own data, it runs well over
time, and there's no ask.

### Stuck?

1. *Too much to fit.* Each slide gets one picture and one claim. Everything else goes in the notes or the linked assignment.
2. *My boss won't give me 15 minutes.* Present slides 1, 7, 9 and 10 in five minutes and send the rest.
3. *My numbers got worse during the course.* Show them. Often it's because the data got honest (dead deals closed out). Say
   that on the slide.

### The 1:1 presentation format (last 45 minutes of S6)

| Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Learner sets the scene. Who the real audience is and what they want from them |
| 5 to 20 | Presentation, 15 minutes, timed. Raka plays the real audience (the boss or the team) and doesn't interrupt |
| 20 to 30 | Questions in character. The hard questions that audience would ask ("Why should I trust this forecast?", "What if Daan won't use it?", "Why not just hire another AE?") |
| 30 to 40 | Live marking. Raka goes through the 8 criteria on screen, score and one line of feedback each. Pass or the exact fixes needed |
| 40 to 45 | What's next. The date the learner presents it for real, and the date of the 30 day check in |

Within 24 hours Raka sends the written marks, the feedback, and (on a pass) the Certified certificate.
