## Who this level is for

Level 1 is for people who want to run a working sales engine, even if someone else built the tools around it. You don't
need to be technical. You need to be curious, comfortable in a spreadsheet and willing to do the work every week.

| You are | Why Level 1 fits you |
|---|---|
| An SDR or BDR (the person who books first meetings for a sales team) | You want to stop copying names into sheets by hand and start working from a ranked, researched list |
| In marketing ops or RevOps (the people who run sales and marketing tools, processes and data) | You know the tools and want to own the chain from ICP to first message |
| A data analyst or junior developer | You can handle data and want a role closer to revenue |
| A founder's first hire | You've been told "go get us customers" and need a method, not a pile of tabs |
| A career switcher | You want a clear first step into GTM engineering |

**Not for you if.** You sell for a living and only want more meetings for yourself. Take the GTM Seller course (Track 2).
You manage a sales team. Take the GTM Leader course (Track 3). The intake form (README section 7.3) will tell you if
you've picked the wrong one.

## The promise

When you finish Level 1, you can take a market you've never seen, decide who's worth contacting, find them, fill the gaps
in the data cheaply, rank them, research the best ones without letting AI make things up, and write a first message a
real buyer would answer. You'll have five builds and a passed practical test to show for it.

**GTM** means go to market, everything a company does to find customers and sell to them. **GTM engineering** means
building the systems behind that so selling runs on a machine instead of on someone's memory. A **GTM engineer** is the
person who builds and runs those systems. Level 1 makes you the person who runs them well.

#### Lesson 1.1 Your first researched opener in 30 minutes (7 min)

**In plain words.** Before any theory, you'll do the job once. You pick one company, let Claude read three of its pages
with a prompt we give you, check what it found, and write a four line first message. It won't be perfect. It will be
true and specific, which already beats most cold messages people get.

**You'll be able to.**
- Pick a company worth researching
- Run a research prompt that quotes its sources
- Check two claims by opening the source yourself
- Write a four line opener built on one checked fact

**What's shown.**
1. Raka says what's about to happen. "In 30 minutes you'll have done, roughly, everything this level teaches. The rest
   of the course makes each step better."
2. Pick one company. It should be a business you'd like to sell to, with a website and at least one person you could
   message. Routeful learners use RF-031, Kanaalweg Transport, from the practice pack. Its research snippets are in the
   pack, so they skip step 3.
3. Collect three pages. Open the company's homepage, its careers or jobs page, and one recent LinkedIn post by the
   company or its boss. For each page, copy the link, then select the text on the page (Ctrl+A then Ctrl+C, or Cmd on a
   Mac) and paste it into a blank Google Doc under its link.
4. Open claude.ai (Lesson 1.3 shows the sign up if you haven't got an account yet, it takes two minutes). Paste the quick
   win prompt below, then paste your three pages under it.
5. The prompt on screen.
   "You're helping me write a first message to this company. Read only the pages below, nothing else. Answer four
   questions. 1. What does the company do, in one sentence? 2. What's one thing that changed or is happening right now
   (hiring, a new site, a new service, a new boss)? 3. What problem might that cause them, connected to what I sell,
   which is [one sentence on what you or Routeful sell]? 4. Which line on these pages shows it? Rules. Quote the exact
   words from the page and give its link for every answer. If the pages don't say, write UNKNOWN. Don't guess and don't
   use anything you know from outside these pages."
6. Claude answers. Raka opens the source for two of the quotes, uses Ctrl+F (Cmd+F on Mac) to find the words, and
   confirms they're there word for word. If a quote isn't there, that answer is out.
7. The four line opener on screen, built only from checked answers.
   - Line 1. A specific, checked observation about the company.
   - Line 2. The problem that observation points to, and what it costs them.
   - Line 3. Why you're worth listening to, in one true sentence, or skip it.
   - Line 4. A small, easy question, like an offer to send something short. Not "can we book a call".
8. The Routeful example. "Hi Sanne, I saw Kanaalweg Transport is hiring two planners and the post asks for advanced
   Excel. When the week's plan lives in a spreadsheet, one sick driver on Monday means rebuilding it by hand. Routeful
   plans routes for fleets your size. Want me to send a two minute video of that Monday in Routeful?"
9. Raka reads it out loud and checks three things. Is every fact checked? Is it about one problem? Would it make sense
   sent to 10,000 other companies? If yes to the last one, it's too generic, rewrite line 1.
10. Close. "Don't send it yet. Save it. You'll rebuild it properly in Module 5 and compare."

**Do it now.** (30 min, keep a timer running)
- Minutes 0 to 5. Pick your company and write one sentence on why it's worth contacting.
- Minutes 5 to 15. Copy three pages with their links into a Google Doc called "Quick win".
- Minutes 15 to 20. Run the prompt in Claude.
- Minutes 20 to 25. Check two quotes against the source with Ctrl+F. Cross out anything you can't find.
- Minutes 25 to 30. Write your four line opener under the research. Don't send it.

#### Lesson 1.2 The job, the chain and what interviews test (7 min)

**In plain words.** A company makes money when the right people hear the right thing at the right time and say yes. A
GTM engineer builds and runs the machine that finds those people, works out what to say and when, sends it, and keeps
the records straight. You just did a small version of it by hand.

**You'll be able to.**
- Describe the job in two sentences
- Name the nine links in the chain and which ones Level 1 covers
- Tell the role apart from SDR, RevOps and marketing ops
- Name the four things interviews usually test

**What's shown.**
1. Raka in one line per role. Seven years in revenue, GTM and data. Global GTM and Campaign Manager at Betty Blocks,
   where he built segmentation, enrichment, scoring, routing and follow up loops. Data and insights lead across 23
   markets at HEINEKEN. Founded a stroopwafel brand from zero. He runs this engine at Astra today.
2. The chain on screen, one box at a time. ICP, list, signal, research, message, send, reply, CRM, measure. **ICP**
   (ideal customer profile) describes the companies most likely to buy and stay. A **list** is the companies and people
   that match. A **signal** is something that happens which makes a company more likely to buy now. **Research** finds
   the specific reason to talk to this one company. The **message** is what you say. **Send** is the tool that delivers
   it. **Reply** is how you handle the answer. The **CRM** (customer relationship management system) is where every
   company, person and deal is recorded. **Measure** is the weekly numbers.
3. Level 1 covers the first five boxes, ICP to message. Level 2 builds send, reply, CRM and measure.
4. The manual way against the engineered way. An SDR with 14 browser tabs and a reminder in their head, against a list
   that rebuilds itself, signals that arrive by themselves, research drafted by AI and checked by a person.
5. Raka's rule. Machines do the repetitive parts, people do the judgement. Automate the judgement (what's true, who to
   contact, whether to send) and you build a machine that's fast and wrong.
6. Four roles side by side. An **SDR or BDR** (sales or business development representative) contacts prospects and is
   measured on meetings booked. **RevOps** (revenue operations) runs the tools, processes and data, measured on data
   quality and forecast accuracy. **Marketing ops** runs marketing tools, measured on lead flow. A **GTM engineer**
   builds the systems that create pipeline, measured on pipeline created and cost per meeting. In small companies one
   person often does all four.
7. Screen demo. On LinkedIn, click **Jobs** in the top bar, type "GTM engineer", set your country, press Enter. Raka
   opens three posts and highlights the tools, the tasks and the outcomes they ask for. He doesn't quote salaries or
   numbers of openings. "They change fast. Check current figures yourself."
8. What interviews test, drawn from those posts. Can you build a list and enrich it. Can you write a message that gets
   replies. Can you connect tools. Can you show numbers and explain them. Many send a take home task, and the practical
   test at the end of this level has the same shape.
9. Close. "Every assignment here is a portfolio piece. Save each one from day one."

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write three sentences. Your current job title. Two things you already do that count as GTM
engineering (for example "I build lead lists in Sheets"). One link in the chain you've never done. Then find three GTM
engineer job posts in your country and note the three requirements that come up most.

#### Lesson 1.4 Who pays, and what your best customers share (9 min)

**In plain words.** An ICP describes the companies most likely to buy from you and stay. It starts with one question.
Who has this problem badly enough to pay to fix it? Your best existing customers and your real losses answer it better
than any guess.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write the problem in the buyer's words and an ICP sentence
- Tell the user, the buyer and the payer apart
- Sort criteria into firmographic and behavioural
- Use won and lost deals as evidence

**What's shown.**
1. Routeful's ICP on screen. "Transport and logistics companies in NL, BE and DE with 20 to 500 staff, 15 or more
   trucks, planning in Excel or an old TMS." A **TMS** (transport management system) is software transport firms use to
   plan and track shipments.
2. Three people in a deal. The **user** uses the product every day (a planner). The **buyer** chooses it (head of
   planning). The **payer** signs off the money (managing director). Sometimes one person is all three. A **persona** is
   the type of person you talk to, with one line on what keeps them up at night.
3. **Firmographics** are facts about a company, like size, industry and location. **Behaviour** is what it does, like
   the tools it uses and who it's hiring. RF-007 and RF-012 are both 80 staff transport firms. RF-007 posted a job for a
   "planner, Excel expert". RF-012 says on its site it plans with a modern platform. Same size, different fit.
   "Firmographics build the list. Behaviour ranks it."
4. The money. A **revenue model** is how money comes in (one off, monthly, per user, per truck). **Unit economics** is the
   money one customer brings in against the cost to win and serve them. In Sheets, Raka opens the Routeful pipeline,
   filters **Stage** to "Won" and uses `=AVERAGE()` on the deal value. Then he splits by size. Firms under 20 staff took as
   many sales hours and paid a fraction. That's the evidence for the 20 staff minimum.
5. Best customers. He sorts won deals by value and by days to close, and fills a "what they share" column for the top 6.
   All have 25 or more trucks and all mentioned Excel in the call. Then the 4 lost calls. Two too small, one already
   installing a new TMS, one on price. Losses are ICP data too.
6. The Astra lesson. Astra sent 330 researched cold first messages between 14 July and 25 October 2026. 26 people said
   yes, 7.9%. In October, of 119 first pitches, 30% replied and 15% said yes, against 4% saying yes in September. By 25
   October that had booked 7 meetings. No paid project has closed yet, so these are reply and meeting numbers, not
   revenue. The lesson. Consumer businesses (shops and brands selling to the public) said yes most often and couldn't
   pay. B2B companies (businesses selling to other businesses) with a budget were the ones worth meeting.
7. So someone saying yes isn't the same as someone who can pay, and "everyone with a website" isn't an ICP. Your ICP
   filters for both.
8. The ICP sentence formula. "[Industry] companies in [countries] with [size] that [situation that causes the problem]."
   Close. "An ICP is a guess you'll test. Write it down so you can be proven wrong."

**Do it now.** (15 min) Write the problem in one sentence in the buyer's words. Name the user, buyer and payer. Write your
ICP sentence. In a sheet called "ICP draft", list three firmographic and two behavioural criteria, your top 5 customers

#### Lesson 1.5 Tiers and disqualifiers (8 min)

**In plain words.** Not every company that fits deserves the same effort. A tier says how good a fit an account is, so
tier 1 gets the most research and tier 3 a lighter touch. Disqualifiers are facts that rule a company out completely, so
you don't waste a single message on them.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write testable rules for tier 1, 2 and 3
- Write at least four disqualifiers
- Apply both to a list with formulas

**What's shown.**
1. An **account** is a company you might sell to. A **contact** is a person at an account. A **tier** is a label for how
   good a fit an account is. A **disqualifier** is a fact that means "don't contact".
2. Routeful's disqualifiers. Under 20 staff. No own fleet (only brokers). Already a customer. Installed a new TMS in the
   last 12 months. Outside NL, BE and DE.
3. Routeful's tiers. Tier 1 means 50 to 500 staff, 25 or more trucks and a behaviour sign (Excel or old TMS). Tier 2
   matches the firmographics with one behaviour sign missing or unknown. Tier 3 matches firmographics only.
4. Testable means a number or a yes/no fact. "Strong fit" isn't testable. "25 or more trucks" is.
5. Screen demo in the Routeful accounts sheet. Add a column "Disqualified" with
   `=IF(OR(C2<20, D2="No", E2="Customer"), "Yes", "No")` where C is staff, D is own fleet and E is status. Drag it down.
6. Add a column "Tier" with nested `=IF()` on staff, trucks and the behaviour column, showing "DQ" for disqualified rows.
   Raka types it slowly and explains each part.
7. Click **Data**, **Create a filter**, filter "Disqualified" to "No", sort "Tier" A to Z. Count each tier with
   `=COUNTIF(F:F, 1)`. If tier 1 is more than about a third of the list, the rules are too loose.
8. Close. "Tiers decide effort. Disqualifiers protect your time and your reputation."

**Do it now.** (15 min) Write your tier rules and four disqualifiers in your "ICP draft" sheet and apply them with formulas
to 20 rows of your list or the Routeful accounts sheet.

#### Lesson 2.1 Where B2B data comes from (8 min)

#### Lesson 2.2 Records, fields and data quality (8 min)

**In plain words.** A CRM stores data as records, one company, one person or one deal each, and every piece of
information on a record is a field. Bad data costs you twice, once when you pay for it and again when a message bounces
or reaches the wrong person. Five checks tell you how good a data set is.

**You'll be able to.**
- Name the three main objects and their unique identifiers
- Score a data set on the five quality checks
- Count blanks and duplicates, and fix spellings with a lookup table

**What's shown.**
1. A **record** is one row about one thing. An **object** is a type of record. The three main ones are companies,
   contacts and deals. A **field** (HubSpot calls it a **property**) is one piece of information, like job title.
2. In HubSpot, click **CRM**, then **Contacts**, **Companies** and **Deals** to show the three objects, then open one
   record to show its fields.
3. A **unique identifier** is a field that's different on every record. Website domain for companies, email or LinkedIn
   URL for people. Names are typed too many ways to work.
4. The five checks on screen. **Complete** (filled in), **correct** (true), **fresh** (still true today), **consistent**
   (written the same way everywhere), **unique** (no duplicates).

   `=COUNTIF(D:D, D2)` and filter for values above 1.

   (every spelling in A, the clean value in B) and `=XLOOKUP(F2, 'Country map'!A:A, 'Country map'!B:B, "CHECK")`.
7. Freshness. People change jobs, so any contact older than about a year gets checked again before you message them.
8. Close. "Measure the mess before you fix it, so you can show what you fixed."

**Do it now.** (10 min) On your export or the Routeful export, count blank emails and duplicate emails and list every
spelling of country. Write the three numbers in a small table.

#### Lesson 2.3 GDPR basics for outbound in the EU (8 min)

**In plain words.** GDPR is the EU law on personal data, and it applies to cold outreach too. A work email with a name in
it is personal data. You need a lawful reason to use it, you keep only what you need, you tell people where you got it if
they ask, and you stop at once when they say stop. This isn't legal advice, check your own country and company.

**You'll be able to.**

- Say which fields you need and which you shouldn't store
- Handle an opt out and a "where did you get my data" request

**What's shown.**
1. Raka says it first. "This isn't legal advice. Check the rules in your country and with your company."
2. A **lawful basis** is the reason the law accepts for using someone's data. For B2B outreach it's usually **legitimate
   interest**. You have a real business reason, it's relevant to their job, and it doesn't override their rights. You
   must be able to justify it and write it down.
3. Screen demo. Open the "Legitimate interest note" template. Three boxes for Routeful. Purpose (offering route planning
   to people responsible for planning). Necessity (only planning and operations roles, work contact details only).
   Balance (relevant to their job, we say where we got their data, opting out takes one reply).
4. **Data minimisation** means keeping only what the task needs. Raka crosses out a personal mobile number and a home
   address in the Routeful export.
5. If someone asks where you got their details, you tell them. When someone says stop, you stop at once and record it so
   nobody on your team contacts them again.

**In plain words.** Enrichment means adding missing facts to a record, like an email address or a company's size. A
waterfall tries one data source, and only if it finds nothing tries the next, then the next. You find more than any
single source would, and you only pay for the lookups you need.

**You'll be able to.**
- Say which fields you'll enrich and why
- Draw a waterfall for one field
- Choose the order of providers

**What's shown.**
1. **Enrichment** adds missing facts. Company enrichment fills facts about the account (staff, industry, tools).
   Contact enrichment fills facts about the person (title, LinkedIn URL, work email).
2. Raka opens the data source map and highlights the facts marked "credits". Those are the targets. Enrich only what a
   rule or a message needs.
3. Providers are good at different things. Some find more emails in one country, some are better on company size.
   Nobody finds everything.
4. A **waterfall** drawn for work email. Provider A, found? Stop. Not found? Provider B. Then C. Last step "not found",
   which is an answer too.
5. A made up example of 10 contacts. A finds 5, B finds 2 of the remaining 5, C finds 1 more. 8 of 10 against 5 with A
   alone, and B and C only ran on the rows A missed.
6. Order by cost and coverage, cheapest and best for your country first. A pattern guess (like firstname@domain) can be
   step zero, but only when verification follows.
7. What enrichment can't do. It can't tell you a company's current problem. That's research, Module 4.
8. Close. "Every enriched field gets a 'found by' column and a date."

**In plain words.** Fit says "is this the right type of company". Intent says "are they looking for something like what

### Module 5. The message

**Goal.** Write a short sequence built around one problem, true in every line, with follow ups that add something and an
A/B test plan you can trust.
**Time.** 41 minutes of video, about 1.5 hours for the assignment.

#### Lesson 5.1 One problem, start to finish (9 min)

**In plain words.** A good first message talks about one problem the reader really has, from start to finish. It shows
you looked, names the problem, says what it costs, says why now, gives one line of proof, and ends with a small, easy
question. Six parts, under 100 words. Your four line quick win was the short version.

**You'll be able to.**
- Name the six parts of an opener
- Write each part from your research, with its source
- Run the one thread test

**What's shown.**
1. An **opener** is the first message to someone. The six parts on screen.
   - Part 1. Greeting and a specific, checked observation about their company.
   - Part 2. The problem you noticed.
   - Part 3. What it costs them (time, money, customers).
   - Part 4. Why it matters now (their current goal or a recent change).
   - Part 5. One line of proof, true and checkable.
   - Part 6. A low effort question. Offer to show something. Avoid "can we book a call".
2. Raka builds a Routeful opener from the RF-031 research, part by part, showing each source.
   "Hi Sanne, I saw Kanaalweg Transport is hiring two planners and the post asks for advanced Excel. When the week's plan
   lives in a spreadsheet, one sick driver on Monday means rebuilding it by hand. That's a planner's morning gone and late
   drops for your customers. With the second depot opening in March, that gets harder. A fleet of a similar size cut
   Monday replanning to under an hour with Routeful. Want me to send a two minute video of that Monday in Routeful?"
3. The proof line comes from the practice pack's won call W3. In real life the proof must be your own and checkable.
4. The one thread test. Read parts 2, 3, 4 and 6. Are they the same problem? If part 6 offers something unrelated to part
   2, the message breaks.
5. Raka counts the words. Under 100 for LinkedIn and email openers.
6. The honesty rules from README section 10. No fake "Re:" subjects, no invented personal connections, no claims you
   can't back up. Close. "One problem. Six parts. Every word true."

**Do it now.** (15 min) Rewrite your Lesson 1.1 opener in six parts for your top account, with the source under each
part. Run the one thread test and put the old and new versions side by side.

#### Lesson 5.2 Personalisation that's real (6 min)

**In plain words.** Personalisation means making a message specific to that person or company. Real personalisation uses
a checked fact that leads into the problem. Fake personalisation drops in a first name, a city or a compliment that says
nothing, and readers can tell in one line.

**You'll be able to.**
- Tell real from fake personalisation
- Decide how much research each tier gets
- Say what can and can't be automated

**What's shown.**
1. Fake on screen. "Hi {first name}, I love what {company} is doing in {city}!" "Saw your great post!" "Fellow logistics
   fan here."
2. Real on screen. "Your careers page asks planners for advanced Excel." "Your post on 20 Nov mentions the second depot."
3. The test. Does the fact lead straight into the problem? If you can delete it and the message still works, it's
   decoration.
4. By tier. Tier 1 gets a fully researched opener. Tier 2 gets a researched first line and a shared middle. Tier 3 gets a
   **segment** message, written for a group with the same problem, with no fake personal line.
5. Automate pulling facts, drafting and filling shared parts. Don't automate the decision that a claim is true, or the
   first line of a tier 1 message. Close. "One true fact that leads to the problem beats five decorations."

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write first lines for three different accounts. Under each, write the fact, its source, and how
it leads into the problem.

#### Lesson 5.3 Writing with AI without sounding like AI (6 min)

**In plain words.** AI drafts quickly, and it has habits that make readers switch off. Long words, lists of three, empty
praise and dashes everywhere. You give it a tight brief, then edit until it sounds like a person who looked at the
company.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write a drafting prompt with rules
- Spot the usual AI giveaways and edit them out

**What's shown.**
1. The drafting prompt. Raka gives Claude only the passed claims from the research log, the six parts, and rules. Under
   90 words. Use contractions. No dashes. No praise like "impressive". One problem only. Only facts from the table.
2. The draft comes back. He highlights the giveaways. "I hope this finds you well", "streamline", a list of three
   benefits, "game changer".
3. He edits live. "Streamline your operations" becomes "rebuild the week's plan by hand every Monday", which is what the
   customer said in the transcript.
4. The test on every sentence. Would it fit 10,000 other companies? If yes, cut it or make it specific. Then read it out
   loud. If you wouldn't say it to someone's face, rewrite it.
5. Close. "AI drafts. You make it true and make it sound like you."

**Do it now.** (10 min) Ask Claude to draft one opener from your checked research with the rules from step 1. Edit it and
keep both versions side by side.

#### Lesson 5.4 Follow ups that add something (6 min)

**In plain words.** A sequence is a planned series of messages over days or weeks. A good follow up adds something new
about the same problem. "Just bumping this" adds nothing.

**You'll be able to.**
- Plan a three step sequence with timing
- Write follow ups that stay on the same problem
- Know when to stop

**What's shown.**
1. A **sequence** (also called a cadence) is a planned series of messages across email, LinkedIn and calls.
2. Three steps. Step 1, the opener (day 1). Step 2, something useful on the same problem (day 4 or 5), like a short
   example or a picture of the problem. Step 3, a short close (day 10 to 14) that says you'll leave it there and offers
   the thing once more.
3. Step 2 for Kanaalweg Transport. "Sanne, a quick picture of what I meant. This is a Monday plan rebuilt after two
   drivers call in sick, before and after. Happy to send the two minute version if it's useful."
4. Step 3. "I'll leave it here. If Monday planning gets heavier when the second depot opens, the video's yours, just
   reply 'send'."
5. After step 3, stop. If they say no, stop at once and mark do not contact if they ask. A promise in writing ("this is
   my last message") is a promise. Close. "Every follow up earns its place by adding something."

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write steps 2 and 3 for your Lesson 5.1 opener and check each stays on the same problem.

#### Lesson 5.5 A/B testing honestly (8 min)

**In plain words.** An A/B test sends two versions to two similar groups to see which works better. Done badly, it tells
you a story that isn't true. Test one thing at a time, set the size before you start, and don't call a winner on a
handful of replies.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write an A/B plan with one variable, a size and a decision date
- Judge by positive reply rate
- Avoid the three common ways to fool yourself

**What's shown.**
1. An **A/B test** sends two versions to two similar groups. **Reply rate** is replies divided by people messaged.
   **Positive reply rate** is replies that show interest divided by people messaged. Judge by the positive one.
2. One variable. Change only the first line, or only the closing question. Change two and you can't tell which worked.
3. Similar groups. Split randomly within the same tier and segment. Raka adds `=RAND()`, sorts by it, top half gets A,
   bottom half B.
4. Size before you start. Write how many people get each version and the date you'll decide. With 20 per version, any
   difference is a hint, not a result. The course default is about 100 per version before calling a winner.
5. Three ways to fool yourself. Stopping as soon as one looks ahead. Changing the list while the test runs. Counting "no
   thanks" and "unsubscribe" as success.
6. The honest Astra example. Astra's yes rate was 4% in September and 15% in October. More than one thing in the process
   was different between those months, so Astra knows October went better and can't say which single change did it.
   That's fine to say out loud.
7. The plan template. Hypothesis, variable, version A, version B, groups, size, measure, decision date, decision rule.
   Close. "One change, groups set before you start, decide on the date you wrote down."

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write one A/B hypothesis for your sequence with the single variable, the two versions and the
measure.

#### Lesson 5.6 What Astra's 330 messages taught (6 min)

**In plain words.** Between 14 July and 25 October 2026, Astra sent 330 researched cold first messages. This lesson goes
through what that taught, using only the numbers Astra actually measured, and the weekly scorecard that tracks them.

**You'll be able to.**
- Say what Astra measured and what it learned
- Set up a weekly scorecard before your first send

**What's shown.**
1. The numbers. 330 researched first messages. 26 said yes, 7.9%. In October, 119 first pitches, 30% replied and 15%
   said yes, against 4% saying yes in September. 7 meetings booked by 25 October. No paid project has closed yet, so
   these are reply and meeting numbers, not revenue.
2. Lesson 1. A yes isn't a customer. Consumer businesses said yes most often and couldn't pay. B2B companies with a
   budget were the ones worth meeting.
3. Lesson 2. The research gate and the falsification pass. Messages only went out when every claim had passed, and the
   pass caught wrong claims before they were sent.
4. Lesson 3. Per thread checks before every send. The whole conversation with that person was opened again first, so
   nobody got pitched twice or got a cold opener on top of a reply.
5. Lesson 4. Measure every week. A **scorecard** is a short list of the few numbers you check every week. Astra's has
   invites or messages sent, accepts, first messages sent within 2 days of accepting, positive replies, meetings booked,
   meetings held with the decision maker, proposals sent, and wins with their value.
6. Close. "Right people, one true problem, checked before sending, measured every week."

**Do it now.** (5 min) Copy the weekly scorecard template into "05 Messages" and fill in the row names. You'll fill the
numbers once you send, through your company's existing setup or the one you build in Level 2.

### Stuck during the test?

1. *"My XLOOKUP returns #N/A."* That provider found nothing for the account. Wrap it in `=IFERROR(XLOOKUP(...), "")` so
   the waterfall moves on to the next provider.
2. *"I'm running out of time."* Do Task 5 before you polish Task 3. A typed score with a note beats no opener. Criterion
   5 is a must, criterion 3 isn't.
3. *"Two snippets disagree."* That's the point. The newer, more direct source usually wins (a company announcing its own
   new tool beats a job post that might be old copy). Write down which one you trusted and why.

## Level 1 final exam

**When.** After every Level 1 quiz is passed and every assignment handed in. It can be taken before or after the practical
test.
**Length.** 20 questions, 30 minutes, timed.
**Mix.** 12 knowledge questions and 8 scenarios (60% and 40%), four questions per module.
**Pass mark.** 80%, so 16 of 20 correct.
**Retakes.** Two, at least 48 hours apart, from a different question pool. When building the platform version, write a
second variant of every question for the pool (README section 5) and shuffle the option order so the right answer moves.
**After submitting.** The learner sees each answer, the one line why, and the lesson to rewatch for every wrong answer.

**Q1. (Knowledge)** What does a GTM engineer do?
A. Builds the systems behind selling, like lists, data, research and messages B. Writes the company's website C. Signs
contracts with customers D. Makes cold calls all day
**Answer.** A. **Why.** GTM engineering is building the systems so selling runs on a machine instead of memory.
**Rewatch.** 1.2

**Q2. (Scenario)** Astra found that consumer businesses said yes most often but couldn't pay, while B2B companies with a
budget were the ones worth meeting. What should that change first?
A. The ICP, adding ability to pay as a fit criterion B. Nothing, a yes is a yes C. The sending domain D. The email font
**Answer.** A. **Why.** A yes from someone who can't buy isn't a customer, so the ICP must include ability to pay.
**Rewatch.** 1.4

**Q3. (Knowledge)** What does "firmographics" mean?
A. The company's logo B. Email open data C. A person's job history D. Facts about a company like size, industry and
location
**Answer.** D. **Why.** Firmographics are company facts. Behaviour is what the company does. **Rewatch.** 1.4

**Q4. (Knowledge)** Which tier rule is testable?
A. "Innovative companies" B. "25 or more trucks" C. "Strong growth mindset" D. "Good company culture"
**Answer.** B. **Why.** A testable rule is a number or a yes/no fact you can check. **Rewatch.** 1.5

**Q5. (Knowledge)** Under GDPR, which lawful basis is usually used for B2B outreach, and must be written down?
A. No reason is needed for work emails B. Consent from a friend C. Public interest D. Legitimate interest
**Answer.** D. **Why.** Legitimate interest is the usual basis, and you must be able to justify and document it. Not legal
advice. **Rewatch.** 2.3

**Q6. (Scenario)** A prospect replies "where did you get my email?" What do you do?
A. Say it was random B. Ignore them C. Tell them the source honestly and offer to remove them D. Block them
**Answer.** C. **Why.** You tell people the source of their data when they ask, and honour opt outs at once.
**Rewatch.** 2.3

**Q7. (Knowledge)** Data minimisation means...
A. Making the sheet smaller with formatting B. Deleting everything after a week C. Keeping only the data you need for
the task D. Using fewer tools
**Answer.** C. **Why.** Collect and store only what you need. **Rewatch.** 2.3

**Q8. (Scenario)** You built a list two ways. Way A has 200 rows but 60 are outside your size band. Way B has 140 rows, all
inside. Which is the better starting list?
A. Way B, because every row fits the ICP B. Neither C. Way A, because it's bigger D. Combine both without checking
**Answer.** A. **Why.** Fit beats volume. Rows that don't fit waste credits, messages and reputation. **Rewatch.** 2.5

**Q9. (Knowledge)** What does an enrichment waterfall do?
A. Sends emails in a fixed order B. Tries one data source, and only if it finds nothing tries the next C. Ranks leads by
score D. Deletes bad data
**Answer.** B. **Why.** That's the waterfall. It finds more and you only pay for the lookups you need. **Rewatch.** 3.1

**Q10. (Knowledge)** What must happen to an email address your waterfall found before you send to it?
A. Guess a better one B. Send to it straight away C. Verify it and only send to valid addresses D. Mark it as verified
in the CRM
**Answer.** C. **Why.** Unverified addresses bounce, and bounces push your mail towards spam. **Rewatch.** 3.2

**Q11. (Knowledge)** You used 600 credits on 150 rows and found 50 valid emails. What's the cost per verified email in
credits?
A. 4 B. 12 C. 0.33 D. 50
**Answer.** B. **Why.** 600 divided by 50 valid emails is 12. Cost per row would be 4. **Rewatch.** 3.3

**Q12. (Knowledge)** How do a fit score and an intent score differ?
A. They're the same B. Fit says whether they're the right type of company, intent says whether they're ready now
C. Intent is only website visits D. Fit is for people, intent is for companies
**Answer.** B. **Why.** Fit decides if, intent decides when. **Rewatch.** 3.4

**Q13. (Scenario)** A tier 1 account just posted two route planner jobs and its head of operations changed last month.
What are these?
A. Noise B. Fit criteria C. Signals that raise the account's priority now D. Reasons to disqualify
**Answer.** C. **Why.** Hiring and a new leader are classic signals that make buying more likely now. **Rewatch.** 3.4

**Q14. (Knowledge)** What is the eavesdrop loop?
A. Recording calls without consent B. Feeding what customers and prospects say back into your ICP, scores and messages
C. Reading competitors' emails D. A LinkedIn feature
**Answer.** B. **Why.** Their words beat your guesses. **Rewatch.** 3.6

**Q15. (Scenario)** Your research agent says a prospect "recently expanded to Germany". What do you do before using it?
A. Use it, the AI is usually right B. Ask the AI if it's sure C. Soften it to "I think you expanded" D. Open the source
it cites, try to prove the claim wrong, and drop it if you can't confirm it
**Answer.** D. **Why.** That's the falsification pass. Astra's research gate caught wrong claims this way.
**Rewatch.** 4.4

**Q16. (Knowledge)** Which rule belongs in a research prompt?
A. "Be as creative as you can" B. "Use everything you know about this company" C. "Keep it short" D. "Quote the exact
words and give the link for every answer, or write UNKNOWN"
**Answer.** D. **Why.** No quote, no claim, and UNKNOWN stops guessing. **Rewatch.** 4.2

**Q17. (Scenario)** You open an account's domain and it shows a removals company with the same name. What does your agent
design say?
A. Write WRONG COMPANY and take the next account B. Research it anyway C. Guess the right domain D. Ask Claude which
company it is
**Answer.** A. **Why.** The stop rule protects you from researching and messaging the wrong business. **Rewatch.** 4.3

**Q18. (Knowledge)** Which order matches the message structure taught in this course?
A. "Re:" subject, case study, calendar link B. Specific observation, the problem, what it costs, why now, one line of
proof, a low effort question C. Compliment, three problems, a discount D. Pitch, price, call to book, greeting
**Answer.** B. **Why.** One problem from start to finish, ending on a low effort question. **Rewatch.** 5.1

**Q19. (Scenario)** 300 people were messaged. Replies were 12 out of office, 9 "no", 3 "wrong person" and 15 positive.
What's the positive reply rate?
A. 13% B. 5% C. 10% D. 39%
**Answer.** B. **Why.** 15 divided by 300 is 5%. Only positive replies count, and you divide by people messaged.
**Rewatch.** 5.5

**Q20. (Scenario)** In one month a team changed both its target segment and its opener, and positive replies went up.
What's the honest reading?
A. The opener caused it B. It's luck, ignore it C. The segment caused it D. Something in the changes worked, and to know
which one you change one thing at a time
**Answer.** D. **Why.** Two changes at once can't be separated. **Rewatch.** 5.5

**Answer key at a glance.** 1 A, 2 A, 3 D, 4 B, 5 D, 6 C, 7 C, 8 A, 9 B, 10 C, 11 B, 12 B, 13 C, 14 B, 15 D, 16 D, 17 A,
18 B, 19 B, 20 D.
