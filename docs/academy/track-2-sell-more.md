## Who it's for

Account executives, SDRs and BDRs, founders who still do the selling, consultants and freelancers who sell their own
work. You don't want a new job. You want more meetings with the right people, and less time lost on copying, pasting and
remembering.

You don't need any technical background. If you can use a spreadsheet and LinkedIn, you can do every step here.

**Not for you if** you want to build automations and data pipelines as your job (take Track 1), or you manage a team and
want them all working this way (take Track 3).

## The promise

In four weeks you run your own small pipeline engine. A pipeline engine is a simple, repeatable routine that brings you
meetings without depending on your memory. Yours will have four parts.

1. The right accounts, written down and ranked.
2. A reason to reach out now, from signals you've set up once.
3. A researched message that's about their problem, written in minutes with AI and checked by you.
4. A follow up that happens by itself, plus a weekly review that tells you what's working.

All of it fits into about 3 hours a week once it's set up.

**Goal.** Measure honestly where your selling time goes and where your meetings come from, pick the one number you want

**Time.** 24 minutes of video, plus about 1.5 hours of assignment spread over 5 working days (5 to 10 minutes a day,

#### Lesson 0.1 Where your time goes (8 min)

**In plain words.** Most sellers think they spend their week selling. When they log it, a big share goes on finding
people, copying data and chasing admin. You can't fix time you haven't measured, so this week you measure it.

**You'll be able to.**
- Log your working time in six simple buckets without slowing down
- See how much of your week actually goes to talking with buyers
- Spot the one bucket that eats the most time for the least result

**What's shown.**
1. Why we start here. At the end of the course you'll compare your results with this week. Without a starting number,
   you can't tell whether anything worked.
2. The six buckets, each explained in one line. **Finding** (looking for accounts and people). **Researching** (reading
   about an account before you contact it). **Writing** (messages, emails, LinkedIn notes). **Talking** (calls and
   meetings with buyers). **Admin** (CRM updates, internal meetings, reports). **Other** (everything else).
3. Screen. Open the course Drive folder, right click **Time and Source Audit**, choose **Make a copy**, rename it
   "Audit, [your name], [week date]".
4. Screen. Open the **Time log** tab. Each row is one block of work. Columns are Date, Start, End, Bucket (a dropdown),
   What I did, Minutes (it fills itself).
5. Show three filled rows as an example. "Mon, 9.00 to 9.40, Finding, searched LinkedIn for planners at 6 firms."
6. The rule that makes it work. Log at the end of each block or at lunch and end of day, never from memory on Friday.
   Memory always flatters you.
7. Screen. The **Summary** tab. Show how the pie and the hours per bucket fill in as rows are added.
8. What a typical first week shows. Ask yourself one question when you look at it on day 5. "If I could hand one bucket
   to a machine, which one would it be?"

**Do it now.** Copy the template, rename it, and log what you've done today so far, block by block. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 0.2 Where your meetings come from (8 min)

**In plain words.** A meeting comes from somewhere. A cold message, a referral, an inbound form, an event, an old
customer. If you know which sources really bring meetings, you know where to spend your time. Then you pick one number to
move for the next 30 days.

**You'll be able to.**
- List every meeting from the last 3 months with its source
- Work out which source brings the most meetings per hour spent
- Choose one number to move and write it down

**What's shown.**
1. Two words. A **meeting** here means a first call with a possible buyer that actually happened. A **source** is how
   that meeting started.
2. The five sources in the template, explained. **Outbound** (you contacted them first). **Inbound** (they contacted you
   first, for example through a website form). **Referral** (someone introduced you). **Event**. **Existing customer**
   (more business from someone who already buys).
3. Screen. Open your calendar, go back 3 months. For each first call with a buyer, add a row to the **Meetings** tab.
   Columns are Date, Company, Person, Their role, Source, Did it become a deal (yes or no).
4. Screen, for CRM users. In HubSpot, click **Sales** then **Deals** (or **CRM** then **Deals**, depending on your
   menu), filter on **Create date** in the last 3 months, add the column **Original source**, export. If your company's
   CRM is different, look for the deal list, a created date filter and a source field. Ask your admin which field holds
   the source if you can't find it.
5. Screen, for practice pack learners. Open the pipeline sheet, filter the 60 deals on the stage **Meeting booked** or
   later, and use the source column.
6. Screen. The **Summary** tab now shows meetings per source and, from your time log, hours per source.
7. Picking the one number. Show three examples. "Outbound meetings held per month, from 2 to 5." "Hours per week spent
   finding people, from 6 to 2." "Meetings held with the person who decides, from 1 in 4 to 3 in 4." Pick one, and make
   it something you can count every week.
8. Why only one. If you try to move five numbers you can't tell what changed anything.

**Do it now.** Fill the Meetings tab with every first call from the last 3 months. If you have more than 30, do the last
30. 15 minutes.

#### Assignment 0. One week time and source audit

**Goal.** Know where your selling hours go and where your meetings come from, and commit to one number to move.

**Time.** 5 to 10 minutes a day for 5 working days, plus 30 minutes on day 5. About 1.5 hours in total.

**You need.** Your copy of the Time and Source Audit template, your calendar, your CRM or the practice pack pipeline

**Steps.**
1. Open your copy of **Time and Source Audit**. Check it has the tabs Setup, Time log, Meetings, Summary, One number.
2. For 5 working days, add a row to **Time log** for every block of work of 15 minutes or more. Pick the bucket from the

3. Log at lunch and at the end of each day. Set two phone reminders now so you don't forget.
4. In **Meetings**, list every first call with a possible buyer from the last 3 months (or the last 30 if there are

5. Practice pack learners. Use the pipeline sheet. Every deal at **Meeting booked** or later is a meeting. Copy company,

6. On day 5, open **Summary**. Check the hours per bucket add up to roughly your working hours. If they're far off, you

7. Read the Summary and write three sentences in the box at the bottom. Where most of your time went. Which source

9. Click **Share** (top right), set **General access** to **Anyone with the link**, role **Viewer**, click **Copy link**.

**Hand in.** The view link to your audit sheet, with all five tabs filled, and the three sentences and the one number
visible.

**Marking rubric.**

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 | Must? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time log covers 5 working days | Fewer than 3 days | 3 or 4 days, or big gaps | 5 days, hours roughly match a working week | Must |
| Every row has a bucket | Buckets mostly empty | Some rows without bucket or wrong bucket | Every row in the right bucket | |
| Meetings tab complete | Empty | Some meetings, sources missing | 3 months (or 30 meetings) with source and outcome | Must |
| Summary read in 3 sentences | Missing | Vague ("I'm busy") | Names the biggest bucket, the best source and the best source per hour | |
| One number | Missing | No start value or no target | Countable, with today's value and a 30 day target | Must |

**Good example.** 5 days, 41 rows. Finding 9.5 h, Researching 4 h, Writing 3 h, Talking 6 h, Admin 8 h, Other 6.5 h. 14
meetings in 3 months, 6 outbound, 5 inbound, 3 referral. Sentences. "Finding and admin took 17.5 hours, more than talking.
Outbound brought the most meetings. Referrals brought the most per hour, but I can't make more of them on demand." One
number. "Outbound meetings held per month. Today 2. Target 5."

**Bad example.** A time log filled on Friday afternoon with one row per day ("Mon, selling, 8 h"), no meetings tab, and
the one number "get more deals". It fails because nothing is measured, nothing is countable, and there's no start value to
compare against in 30 days.

**Stuck?**
- **"I forgot to log for two days."** Rebuild those days from your calendar and sent messages, mark them "rebuilt", and
  log the next days properly. Don't start over.
- **"I have no meetings in the last 3 months."** That's a real result. Write 0, and make your one number "first meetings
  held per month, from 0 to 3".
- **"My CRM has no source field."** Use your memory and your inbox for each meeting, it's only 3 months. Write
  "unknown" where you really can't tell.

#### Lesson 1.1 Accounts that buy (9 min)

**In plain words.** An ICP (ideal customer profile) is a short description of the companies most likely to buy from you
and stay. You don't guess it. You read it off the customers who already bought. A persona is the type of person inside
those companies you talk to.

**You'll be able to.**
- Pull your won deals and spot what they have in common
- Write your ICP in one sentence with numbers in it
- Name 2 or 3 personas who buy

**What's shown.**
1. Two words. **Firmographics** are facts about a company, like size, industry and location. Your ICP is built from
   firmographics plus one or two facts about how they work today.
2. Screen. In HubSpot, click **CRM**, then **Deals**, filter **Deal stage** is **Closed won**, **Close date** in the last
   12 months. Export. In your own CRM, find the same filter on won deals.
3. Screen, practice pack. Open the pipeline sheet, filter the stage **Won**, then look up each won company in the 50
   account sheet.
4. Screen. Paste the won accounts into the **Won deals** tab of the ICP template. Columns are Company, Industry, Staff,
   Country, What they used before, Who signed, Deal value.
5. Read the rows out loud and look for patterns. Show it on Routeful. Most won accounts have 40 to 200 staff, 20 or more
   trucks, planners on Excel, and the operations director signed.
6. Write the ICP sentence. Routeful's. "Transport and logistics firms in the Netherlands, Belgium and Germany with 20 to
   500 staff, 15 or more own trucks, planning in Excel or an old TMS."
7. Personas. List the job titles of the people who signed and the people who were in the meetings. Routeful's are
   operations director, head of planning, owner or managing director.
8. Astra's lesson, honestly told. Raka's own outreach got yeses from consumer businesses, cafes and shops, that couldn't
   pay. The companies that went further were B2B firms with a budget. A yes from someone who can't buy is a wasted
   meeting. Put "can they pay" into your ICP.

**Do it now.** Export your won deals (or the practice pack's) into the Won deals tab and write your ICP sentence and
personas in the **ICP** tab. 15 minutes.

#### Lesson 1.2 Tiers, who gets the most effort (8 min)

your best research and personal messages. Tier 3 get a lighter touch or wait.

**You'll be able to.**

2. The tier rule, built from your ICP. **Tier 1** matches every part of the ICP and has a sign they could buy soon (more

#### Lesson 1.4 Building the list without buying junk (9 min)

**In plain words.** A bought list is usually old, wrong and full of people who'll never buy. A list you build yourself
from your ICP is smaller and much better. This lesson shows how to build it fast and keep it clean.

**You'll be able to.**

- Keep only the data you need

**What's shown.**

   deal, leave it out or ask first. Nothing kills trust faster than two people from one company contacting the same buyer.

   for example "planning". Pick 1 to 3 people. Copy name, role and profile link.

   come back to it in Module 5. Not legal advice, check your country's rules.

**Do it now.** Add contacts for your first 10 accounts and run the duplicates check on each. 15 minutes.

### Module 2. Signals, who to contact now

**Goal.** Set up free alerts that tell you which accounts on your list have a reason to talk this week, and a 15 minute
weekly routine to act on them.

**Time.** 34 minutes of video, plus about 1 hour of assignment (30 minutes setup, then 15 minutes a week).

**You need.**
- Your Assignment 1 list
- LinkedIn (Sales Navigator if you have it)
- A Google account for Google Alerts

#### Lesson 2.1 What a signal is, and the six that matter (9 min)

**In plain words.** A signal is something that happens at a company that makes it more likely to buy now. A new boss, a
job ad, a funding round. Contacting an account the week a signal appears gives you a real reason to talk, and the timing
does half the work.

**You'll be able to.**
- Name the six signal types and give an example of each for your market
- Pick the 3 signals that matter most for what you sell
- Explain the difference between fit and timing

**What's shown.**
1. Fit and timing. Your ICP and tiers tell you if an account is the right type (fit). A signal tells you if now is a good
   moment (timing). You want both.
2. **Hiring.** They post a job that your product helps with. Routeful example, a haulier hiring a second planner means
   planning is stretched.
3. **Funding or growth.** New money, a new site, a takeover, a big new contract. Growth breaks old ways of working.
4. **Leadership change.** A new operations director in the first 90 days often reviews tools and suppliers.
5. **Tech change.** They start or stop using a tool, often visible in a job ad ("experience with [tool]") or on their
   website.
6. **Content.** The person or company posts, comments or speaks about the problem you solve. "Our planners worked the
   weekend again."
7. **Website visits.** If your company tracks which companies visit its site, a visit to the pricing page is a strong
   signal. Ask marketing whether this exists and how you can see it. If you have nothing, skip this one for now.
8. Picking your 3. Think back to your won deals from Module 1. What happened at those companies in the months before they
   bought? Those are your signals.
9. Screen. Open the **Signal Tracker** template, **Signals** tab. Write your 3 signals, one line each, with what you'd
   say about it.

**Do it now.** Look at 3 of your won deals. For each, write what changed at that company before they bought. Pick your 3
signals. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 2.2 Free alerts on LinkedIn and Google (9 min)

**In plain words.** You don't check 100 accounts every morning. You set alerts once, and they bring the news to you. This
lesson sets up the two free ones everyone can use.

**You'll be able to.**

**What's shown.**

2. Screen. LinkedIn job alert. Click **Jobs** in the top bar. Type a role that signals need, for example "transport
   planner", set the location, press Enter. Turn on the **Set alert** switch (or **Job alert**, near the top of the
   results). Choose daily or weekly and email.
3. Screen. Google Alerts. Go to google.com/alerts. Type a query in the box.
4. How to write the query. A company name in quotes, for example "Brugveld Transport" (made up). Or a topic plus a place,
   for example "new distribution centre" Utrecht. Or several companies at once with OR between them.
5. Screen. Click **Show options**. Set **How often** to at most once a day, **Sources** to automatic, **Language** and

**Do it now.** Follow your Tier 1 accounts, set one job alert and one Google Alert, and make the Signals label. 15
minutes.

#### Lesson 2.4 The eavesdrop habit and your 15 minute routine (8 min)

**In plain words.** Eavesdropping, in Raka's sense, means listening to what customers and prospects say in calls, posts
and reviews, and using their words in your list and messages. The 15 minute routine is when you turn the week's signals
into actions, every week, same time.

**You'll be able to.**
- Keep a list of the exact words buyers use about their problem

**What's shown.**

   and trade press.
2. Screen. The **Their words** tab in the Signal Tracker. One row per phrase. "Planning a week ahead is impossible when
   half the loads change the same morning." Where you heard it, and the date.
3. Why it matters. In Module 4 these phrases make your message sound like it comes from someone who knows their world.
4. The routine, step by step. Same day and time every week, 15 minutes, a calendar block called "Signals".
5. Minutes 0 to 5. Open the Signals label in Gmail and your Sales Navigator alerts. Log anything about an account on your

7. Minutes 10 to 15. For each new signal, move the account up a tier if your rule says so and write the action. "Research
   and message this week." Pick no more than 5 actions, so they get done.
8. Screen. A filled week in the tracker. 9 items logged, 3 actions, 1 account moved to Tier 1.

**Do it now.** Put the 15 minute "Signals" block in your calendar, weekly, and add 3 phrases to Their words from anything
you've read or heard this week. 10 minutes.

### Module 3. Research in minutes with AI

**Goal.** Research any account in about 10 minutes with an AI prompt, check every fact you'll use, and turn it into one
verified reason to reach out.

**Time.** 33 minutes of video, plus about 1.5 hours of assignment.

**You need.**
- Claude (free plan is enough) or your company's approved AI tool
- Your Assignment 1 list and Assignment 2 Signal Tracker
- The **Research Card** template and the **Prompt Library** doc

#### Lesson 3.1 The research prompt (9 min)

**In plain words.** A prompt is the instruction you give an AI. A good research prompt asks for facts with their source,
keeps them apart from guesses, and focuses on the one thing you sell. You write it once and reuse it on every account.

**You'll be able to.**
- Copy and fill the course research prompt for your market
- Give the AI the right material to work from
- Read the answer and know which parts are facts and which are guesses

**What's shown.**
1. What you want from research. Not everything about the company. Three things only. What they do and for whom. What
   might be hard for them right now in the area you help with. Why now.
2. Two ways to feed the AI. If your AI tool can search the web, it can read pages itself. If it can't, open the pages
   yourself (homepage, about page, a job ad, a recent post) and paste the text in. Pasting is slower but you know
   exactly what it read.
3. Screen. Open the **Prompt Library** doc and show the research prompt.

   ```
   You are helping me prepare to contact a company. I sell [what you sell, one sentence] to [your ICP sentence].
   The company is [company name], website [URL]. The person is [name], [role].
   Here is what I found [paste page text, job ads, posts, or say "search the web"].
   Give me five short sections.
   One. What they do and for whom, in two sentences.
   Two. Facts that matter for what I sell. Each fact on its own line with the exact source link or the pasted text it
   came from. If you can't point to a source, leave it out.
   Three. What might be hard for them right now in [your area]. Mark every point as a guess and say what it's based on.
   Four. Why now. Any recent change, such as hiring, a new site, a new leader, a post. Give the date and source.
   Five. Three things I should check myself before I write to them.
   Don't invent numbers, names or quotes. Say "not found" when you don't know.
   ```

4. Screen. Fill it for a practice pack account. Brugveld Transport (made up), with the made up job ad and homepage text
   from the pack pasted in. Run it in Claude.
5. Screen. Read the answer out loud. Point at a fact with a source, a guess that's marked as a guess, and a "not found".

#### Lesson 3.2 Checking facts (8 min)

**In plain words.** AI tools sometimes state things that aren't true, with total confidence. If you put a wrong fact in a
message to a buyer, you lose them. So every fact you'll use gets opened at its source by you, before it goes anywhere.

**You'll be able to.**
- Open the source of each fact and check it says what the AI said
- Spot the usual AI mistakes, like old news, wrong company, made up numbers
- Fill the Research Card with checked facts only

**What's shown.**
1. Why this matters, told from Astra. Raka's research gate caught wrong claims before they went out. A wrong company with
   the same name, an old page, a link that looked missing but wasn't. Each would have reached a buyer.
2. The rule. A fact goes into a message only if you opened the source yourself in this session and it says the same
   thing. A search result snippet isn't a source. The page is.
3. Screen. Take each fact from lesson 3.1's answer. Click its link. Find the sentence on the page (Ctrl+F or Cmd+F). Tick
   it, fix it, or delete it.
4. The usual mistakes. **Same name, different company** (check the address or the people match). **Old news** (check the
   date, a job ad from two years ago isn't a signal). **Made up numbers** (if the number isn't on the page, delete it).
   **Mixed up people** (check the person still works there on their LinkedIn).
5. Screen. Open the **Research Card** template. Fill Account, Person, Role, Tier, Signal. Then the **Facts** section, one
   checked fact per line with its link and the date you checked it.
6. What not to keep. Private details about the person, anything about their family or health, anything you couldn't use
   in a professional message. Data minimisation again.

**Do it now.** Check every fact from your first account's answer, and fill its Research Card with only the ones that
passed. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 3.3 Turning research into a reason to talk (8 min)

**In plain words.** Research is only useful if it gives you a reason to talk that the buyer would care about. A good
reason is one problem they probably have, what it costs them, and why it matters now. One problem, not five.

**You'll be able to.**
- Turn facts into one likely problem, written as a guess you can check
- Say what that problem costs them in their terms
- Write the reason to reach out in two sentences

**What's shown.**
1. Three parts of a reason. **The problem** (what's hard for them in your area). **The cost** (time, money, customers,
   staff, risk). **Why now** (the signal).
2. Screen. Brugveld's card. Facts. Hiring a second planner, the ad asks for strong Excel skills, they opened a second depot
   last month. Problem guess. Planning two depots in Excel is getting too big for one person. Cost. Late changes mean
   empty kilometres and overtime. Why now. New depot and the job ad, both this month.
3. Pick the biggest problem, not the first one you found. If you found three, ask which one costs them the most. That's
   the one.
4. Write it in their words. Use the Their words tab from Module 2. "Loads changing the same morning" beats "dynamic route
   optimisation".
5. Screen. Fill the **Reason to reach out** box. Two sentences. "Brugveld opened a second depot and is hiring a planner who
   knows Excel. Planning two depots in a spreadsheet usually means late changes and empty kilometres, which is what we fix."
6. A reason isn't a pitch. It doesn't list your features. It shows you understood their situation.
7. When there's no good reason. Some accounts have no clear problem or signal right now. Write "no reason yet", leave them
   at Tier 2 and wait for a signal. That's a correct answer, not a failure.

**Do it now.** Write the reason to reach out for your first account in two sentences. 5 minutes.

#### Lesson 3.4 The falsification pass (8 min)

**In plain words.** Falsification means trying to prove your own claim wrong before you send it. You open the page that
would show you're wrong, not the one that made you think you're right. If the claim survives, you use it. If not, you
change it or drop it.

**You'll be able to.**
- Find the page that would disprove your reason
- Run the pass in 3 to 5 minutes per account
- Record what you checked and set a confidence level

**What's shown.**
1. Where this comes from. Raka's outreach runs a falsification pass before every message. It's caught claims that would
   have embarrassed us, like calling something missing when it was there on another page.
2. The question to ask. "If I'm wrong about this, where would I see it?"
3. Brugveld example. Claim, "they plan in Excel". Where would I see I'm wrong? Their careers page might mention a planning
   tool. Their LinkedIn posts might show one. A planner's LinkedIn profile might list a TMS. Open all three.
4. Screen. Open the careers page and search for "TMS", "software", "system". Open one planner profile. Result. The ad
   asks for Excel and names no tool. The planner lists Excel. Claim survives.
5. Claims about something missing are the riskiest. "You have no online booking" is the sentence most likely to be false.
   Check at least two pages, and search the site for the word before you say it.
6. Screen. The **Falsification** box on the card. What I opened, what it showed, keep or change or drop.
7. Confidence. **High** if two different sources agree. **Medium** if one solid source. **Low** if it's a guess. Only send
   on high or medium, and write a low as a question, not a statement.
8. Screen. A second made up account where the pass kills the claim. The "slow website" turned out to be a slow connection
   on our side. Reason changed to "no reason yet".

**Do it now.** Run the falsification pass on your first account and fill the box and the confidence. 5 minutes.

### Module 4. Messages that get replies

**Goal.** Write a 3 step sequence (a planned series of messages over days) that's about one problem the buyer has, short
enough to read on a phone, and honest from the first word.

**Time.** 34 minutes of video, plus about 1 hour of assignment.

**You need.**
- Your Assignment 3 Research Cards
- The Their words tab from your Signal Tracker

#### Lesson 4.1 The six part message (10 min)

**In plain words.** An opener is the first message you send someone. The one taught here has six short parts, and every
part is about the same single problem. You'll write it from your Research Card, so each part already has its material.

**You'll be able to.**
- Name the six parts and what goes in each
- Write an opener from a Research Card in about 10 minutes
- Keep it under about 100 words

**What's shown.**
1. The six parts, each explained.
   **1. Greeting and a specific observation.** Their name and one checked fact about their company. Something only true
   of them.
   **2. The problem you noticed.** The likely problem that fact points to, in their words.
   **3. What it costs them.** Time, money, customers, staff. In their terms.
   **4. Why it matters now.** The signal, or their current goal.
   **5. One line of proof.** Something real you've done or seen. Never invented.
   **6. A low effort question.** Offer to show them something small. Not "can we book a 30 minute call".
2. Screen. Open the Research Card for Hollandse Ritten BV (made up) next to the **Sequence Template**. Map each card box to
   a part.
3. Screen. Write it live.

   > Hi Mark, I saw Hollandse Ritten opened a second depot last month and is hiring a planner who knows Excel.
   >
   > Two depots in one spreadsheet usually means every morning change lands on one person, and drivers wait while the plan
   > gets redone. That's paid hours and empty kilometres every day.
   >
   > With the new depot just open, the way you plan now is the way you'll plan all winter.
   >
   > Hauliers your size use Routeful to plan both depots on one screen and redo a plan in minutes.
   >
   > Shall I send a 2 minute video of what a 60 truck Monday looks like in it?

4. Count it. About 90 words. Read it on your phone. If you have to scroll, cut.
5. Why the question works. "Shall I send" is a yes they can give in one word. A meeting request asks for 30 minutes from a
   stranger.
6. Proof rules. Only use proof that's true and you can back up. If you have no customer story yet, use what you know.
   "I've spent 5 years with planning teams" is proof if it's true. Never invent a client, a number or a result.
7. Practice pack note. Routeful and its customers are made up, so the example proof is made up too. In your real messages,
   proof is always real.

**Do it now.** Write an opener for your best Research Card using the six parts. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 4.2 One problem, short and honest (8 min)

**In plain words.** Messages fail for the same few reasons. Too long, about too many things, about you instead of them, or
dishonest in a small way the reader spots at once. This lesson is the edit you run on every message.

**You'll be able to.**
- Cut a message to one problem
- Remove the phrases that make a message look automated
- Spot and remove fake personalisation and tricks

**What's shown.**
1. One problem, start to finish. If parts 2, 3 and 6 aren't about the same thing, the reader can't follow. Screen. A
   message that mentions planning, then invoicing, then driver apps. Cut to planning.
2. About them, not you. Count the sentences that start with "we" or "I". More than one, rewrite.
3. Phrases that make it look automated. "I hope this finds you well." "I came across your profile." "I'd love to pick your
   brain." "Quick question." "Just following up." Cut them all.
4. Fake personalisation, explained. Personalisation means making a message specific to the person. Fake personalisation
   is a detail that looks personal but says nothing, like "I saw you're based in Utrecht" or "great post!" without saying
   what was in it. It reads worse than none.
5. Tricks that cost you trust. "Re:" or "Fwd:" on a first email that isn't a reply. A made up mutual connection. "As
   promised" when nothing was promised. Never.
6. Plain words. Swap your product words for their words from the Their words tab.
7. Screen. Edit a bad practice message live, from 180 words and three topics to 85 words and one.
8. The edit checklist, in the Sequence Template. One problem. Under about 100 words. One "we" sentence at most. A checked
   fact in line 1. Real proof. A one word yes question. No tricks.

**Do it now.** Run the checklist on the opener you wrote in 4.1 and cut it. 5 minutes.

#### Lesson 4.3 Follow ups, and LinkedIn with email (8 min)

**In plain words.** Most replies come after the first message, not to it. A follow up is a later message to someone who
hasn't replied. Each one adds something new about the same problem. Using LinkedIn and email together (multichannel)
means you're seen in two places without sending more of the same.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write follow up 1 and 2 that each add one new thing
- Plan which channel each step uses
- Write a last message that gives them an easy way out

**What's shown.**
1. The 3 step sequence used in this course.
   **Step 1, day 1.** The opener. By LinkedIn message if you're already connected, otherwise by email. If you're not
   connected, send a LinkedIn invite the same day with no pitch, or no note at all.
   **Step 2, day 4.** Follow up on the other channel. One new thing about the same problem. A short example, a number from
   their own world, or the thing you offered, made smaller.
   **Step 3, day 10.** A short last note. Restate the offer in one line and make it easy to say no.
2. Screen. Step 2 for Hollandse Ritten.

   > Mark, one more thought on the second depot. The planners we talk to say the hard part isn't the plan, it's the five
   > changes before 8 am. I can show you how one of those changes gets redone in Routeful in under a minute. Worth a look?

3. Screen. Step 3.

   > Mark, I'll leave this here. If the second depot is running smoothly, ignore me. If the mornings are getting long, the
   > 2 minute video is ready whenever you want it.

4. What a follow up never says. "Just bumping this up." "Did you see my last email?" Neither adds anything.
5. Channel tips. LinkedIn messages are shorter than emails. An email needs a subject line, 2 to 4 plain words, like
   "second depot planning". No clickbait.
6. Timing. Days between steps give people room. Never two steps on the same day on two channels.
7. When to stop. After step 3, stop. If a signal appears later, start again with a new reason.

**Do it now.** Write steps 2 and 3 for the opener you wrote in 4.1. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 4.4 What never to automate, and Astra's lesson (8 min)

**In plain words.** Tools can send your sequence. They shouldn't write your reason, read your replies or decide who gets a
message. This lesson draws that line, and shows what Raka's own outreach learned from 330 first messages.

**You'll be able to.**
- Say which parts of the sequence a tool may do and which you do
- Check a thread before every send
- Explain why targeting and the first lines matter more than volume

**What's shown.**
1. A tool may send the steps on time, stop the sequence when someone replies, and fill in name and company.
2. You always do these. The research and the reason. The first line of every Tier 1 message. Reading and answering every
   reply. Deciding who goes into the sequence.
3. AI written first lines in bulk, explained. Some tools offer an AI line per contact. Without a fact check, they invent
   things. If you use one, check every line like you checked facts in Module 3.
4. The per thread check. Before any manual message, open the whole conversation with that person on LinkedIn and in your
   inbox, from the first message. Have they replied? Did a colleague already talk to them? Did you promise anything? Raka's
   outreach runs this check before every send, because a cold opener on top of a reply can't be undone.
5. Astra's lesson, with real numbers. Between 14 July and 25 October 2026, Raka's engine sent 330 researched first
   messages and 26 people said yes, 7.9%. In October, after changing who was targeted and how messages opened, 119 first
   messages got replies from 30% and yeses from 15%, against 4% in September. By 25 October that had led to 7 meetings.
6. What changed. Who it went to (B2B companies with a budget, the person who decides), and the first lines (one researched
   problem, checked before sending). Not more volume.
7. What it doesn't show yet. No paid project had closed by then. Meetings come first, sales take longer. Be wary of anyone
   who promises you revenue from messages alone.
8. Your numbers will differ. Your market, offer and channel aren't Raka's. Module 7 shows you how to measure your own.

**Do it now.** Write your own "never automate" list of 3 to 5 lines at the top of the Sequence Template. 5 minutes.

#### Lesson 5.2 Safe limits, deliverability and your sending domain (10 min)

**In plain words.** Deliverability means whether your emails land in the inbox instead of spam. A sending domain is a
separate web address you send cold email from, so your company's main address isn't put at risk. Safe limits keep both
your email and your LinkedIn account in good standing.

**You'll be able to.**
- Decide if you need a sending domain, and set one up if you do
- Explain SPF, DKIM and DMARC in one sentence each
- Set safe daily limits for email and LinkedIn
- Check that a test email lands in the inbox

**What's shown.**
1. Do you need a sending domain? If your company already runs cold email, use their setup and their rules, and ask IT or
   marketing which domain and inbox to use. If you're on your own, yes, you need one.
2. What it is, simply. If your company is routeful.com, you buy a similar address like getrouteful.com, make one inbox on
   it with your real name, and send cold email from there. Replies come to a real person. If that address gets a bad
   reputation, your main address is safe. A domain registration costs about the price of a lunch a year.
3. Screen. Buy the domain at a registrar, then set up an inbox for it in Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. Make it send
   people who type the domain into a browser to your main website (a "redirect" or "forwarding" setting at the
   registrar).
4. Three settings, one sentence each. **SPF** lists which servers may send email for your domain. **DKIM** adds a
   signature to each email that proves it wasn't changed. **DMARC** tells inboxes what to do with mail that fails the
   other two. Your email provider's help pages give the exact records to paste into your domain's DNS settings (the
   settings page at the registrar where these records live).
5. Screen. Paste the three records the provider gives you. Then send a test to a free checking site that shows whether
   SPF, DKIM and DMARC pass. All three green before any cold email.
6. Warm up, explained. A new inbox starts with no reputation. Warming up means sending a few emails a day and slowly
   increasing over 2 to 4 weeks. lemlist and other tools have a warm up feature. Turn it on as soon as the inbox exists.
   Check the tool's guidance for how long.
7. Safe limits to start with. These are cautious course settings, not official numbers. For a new inbox after warm up,
   about 20 cold emails a day. For LinkedIn, about 10 to 15 invites a day and never in bursts. Set these in the
   sequencer's daily limit settings. Check the tool's current advice and LinkedIn's own terms, because LinkedIn caps
   invites and restricts accounts that use automation too hard.
8. Verification, explained. Checking an email address exists before you send. A bounce is an email that couldn't be
   delivered, and many bounces hurt your reputation. Verify every address first, with your sequencer's built in checker
   or a verification tool.
9. Plain text wins. No images, no attachments, one link at most, and usually none in the first email.

**Do it now.** Decide which path you're on (company setup or your own domain). If your own, buy the domain, make the
inbox and turn on warm up today, because warm up takes weeks. 15 minutes.

#### Lesson 5.3 The legal basics (7 min)

**In plain words.** Cold outreach uses people's personal data, so data protection law applies. This lesson gives the
basics every seller in Europe should follow. It isn't legal advice. Check your own country's rules and your company's
policy.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write a short legitimate interest note for your outreach
- Answer "where did you get my data?" correctly
- Handle an opt out at once
- Know which countries need extra care for cold email

**What's shown.**
1. GDPR, in one sentence. The EU law on personal data. It covers names, work emails and LinkedIn profiles of business
   buyers too.
2. A lawful reason. For B2B outreach this is usually **legitimate interest**, meaning you have a real business reason
   that the person would reasonably expect and that doesn't override their rights. You must be able to explain it and
   write it down.
3. Screen. Open the **Legitimate Interest Note** template and fill it. Three questions. Why I contact these people (my
   purpose). Why I need their data for that (only name, role, company, work email, LinkedIn). Why it's fair to them (it's
   about their job, the message is relevant, they can opt out in one reply). One page is enough. Keep it with your list.
4. Where you got their data. If someone asks, tell them. "I found your name and role on your company's website and your
   LinkedIn profile." Keep the source in your list so you can answer.
5. Opt outs. If someone says stop, not interested in being contacted, or unsubscribe, remove them from every sequence at
   once and mark them "do not contact" in your CRM. Never argue.
6. Data minimisation, again. Keep only what you need. Delete people you'll never contact.
7. Countries differ. The UK and the Netherlands are more permissive for emailing business addresses. In Germany,
   unsolicited B2B cold email is risky without consent. For German contacts, use LinkedIn or a call first, or ask your
   company's legal team. Check the rules for every country you email.
8. LinkedIn. It has its own terms and limits, and automated activity can get an account restricted. Stay inside the
   limits from lesson 5.2 and read LinkedIn's terms.
9. Honesty. No fake "Re:" subjects, no invented connections, no claims you can't back up. Your real name and company in
   every message.

**Do it now.** Fill your Legitimate Interest Note and add a **Source** column and a **Do not contact** column to your
account list. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 5.4 Handling replies and objections (9 min)

**In plain words.** Replies are where meetings happen, so you answer each one yourself, the same working day. An objection
is a reason the buyer gives for not going further. Most are a question in disguise. The Reply Playbook gives you a
starting answer for each kind.

**You'll be able to.**
- Sort every reply into one of eight types
- Answer each type with the playbook, in your own words
- Stop the sequence and log the reply in your CRM

**What's shown.**
1. Before you answer, the per thread check. Open the whole conversation from the first message, on both channels.
2. The eight types. **Yes** (they want the thing you offered). **Question** (they want to know more first). **Objection**
   (a reason not to). **Not now** (timing). **Not me** (they point you to someone else). **Opt out** (stop contacting
   them). **Out of office**. **No**.
3. **Yes.** Send what you offered within the same day. Then offer two specific times for a short call. "Here's the 2
   minute video. If it's close to what your mornings look like, would Tuesday at 10 or Thursday at 2 work for 20 minutes?"
4. **"Just send me some information."** Send one short, specific thing about their problem, not a brochure, and ask one
   question. "Here's how a morning change gets redone. Is morning replanning the main pain, or is it the weekly plan?"
5. **"We already have a tool."** Don't attack it. Ask what it does well and where planners still use a spreadsheet next to
   it.
6. **"No budget."** Ask when they plan next year's spending, and offer to send something useful before then. Log the date
   in the CRM.
7. **"Not now."** Ask when, put a task in your CRM for that date, and mention the signal that would bring you back.
8. **"Not me."** Thank them, ask who it is, and contact that person saying who pointed you there, only if they agreed.
9. **Opt out** and **No**. One polite line, remove at once, mark do not contact. **Out of office**. Note the return date
   and move the sequence on.
10. Screen. In lemlist, a reply stops the sequence for that lead. Mark the lead as interested or not interested. In your
    CRM, log the reply on the contact (Module 7 shows a quick way).

**Do it now.** Copy the **Reply Playbook** and rewrite each of the eight answers in your own words for your offer. 15
minutes.

#### Lesson 6.1 AI call prep in 10 minutes (8 min)

**In plain words.** Call prep means knowing, before the call, who you're talking to, what you think their problem is, and
what you want to happen next. You already did most of it in your Research Card. This lesson turns that into a one page
sheet with AI and checks it.

**You'll be able to.**
- Fill the Call Prep template from your Research Card and the thread
- Use a prompt to draft questions and likely objections
- Check whether the person who decides will be on the call

**What's shown.**
1. Screen. Open the **Call Prep** template. Boxes. Who (name, role, time in role). Company in one line. Why they agreed
   (their words from the thread). What we think the problem is. Questions to check the problem. Questions about the cost.
   Who decides and how they buy. What I'll show. The next step I want. Likely objections and my answer.
2. Screen. Copy the problem, cost and why now from the Research Card. Copy their reply from the thread word for word into
   "Why they agreed".
3. Screen. The prep prompt, from the Prompt Library.

   ```
   I have a 30 minute first call with [name], [role] at [company]. They agreed after this message [paste thread].
   My research card says [paste the card]. I sell [one sentence].
   Write 3 open questions to check whether the problem is real for them, 2 questions to understand what it costs them,
   1 question about who else decides and how they buy, and the 3 objections they're most likely to raise with a short
   answer to each. Use plain words. Don't invent facts about the company.
   ```

4. Read the questions and rewrite them in your own voice. Delete any that assume a fact you haven't checked.
5. The person who decides. Raka's lost proposals had one thing in common. The person who decides wasn't on the call. Ask
   before the call, in the confirmation. "Will anyone else be part of this decision? Happy to include them."
6. What you'll show. One thing, linked to their problem. The video you offered, a sample plan, a short screen share.
7. The next step you want, written as a date. "Demo with the operations director and head of planning, next week."

**Do it now.** Fill a Call Prep sheet for your next real call, or for practice pack transcript 3 before reading the
transcript. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 6.3 Follow up in 10 minutes, next step before you hang up (8 min)

**In plain words.** The best time to send a follow up is straight after the call, while it's fresh for both of you. And
the best next step is one you agree on the call, with a date, not one you chase afterwards.

**You'll be able to.**
- Book the next step before the call ends
- Write the follow up email from your notes with AI in under 10 minutes
- Log the call and the next step in your CRM

**What's shown.**
1. Booking before you hang up. Five minutes before the end, say "Based on what you said about the mornings, the useful
   next step is [step]. Shall we put it in now? I can do Tuesday at 10 or Wednesday at 3." Send the invite while you're
   still talking.
2. If they can't commit. Agree on a date to check in, and put it in both calendars. Never end on "let's stay in touch".
3. Screen. The follow up prompt.

   ```
   Write a short follow up email after a sales call. Use only these notes [paste the four note boxes or the transcript].
   Structure. Thank them in one line. What I heard about their problem, in their own words. What it costs them, using
   only numbers they said. What we agreed. The next step with its date. The one thing I'll send and when.
   Under 150 words, plain words, no sales phrases.
   ```

4. Screen. Read the draft. Check every number and quote against the notes. Delete anything the AI added. Send.
5. Time it. From hanging up to sent, under 10 minutes. Put a 15 minute block after every call in your calendar so you
   always have time.
6. Screen. Log it in the CRM. In HubSpot, open the contact, click **Log activity** (or the call icon), choose **Call**,
   paste the four boxes, set the outcome. Create or update the deal and set the stage, for example **Discovery done**.

7. Screen, other CRMs. Look for "log a call" or "activity" on the contact, and "tasks" for the next step.

**Do it now.** Write the follow up for practice pack transcript 3 using the prompt, check it, and time yourself. 10
minutes.

| Next step booked with a date | No next step | "Stay in touch" or a vague step | A dated next step agreed on the call, invite sent | |
| Logged in the CRM | Not logged | Call logged, no task or stage | Call, stage and next step task logged | |

**Good example.** Prep for a 70 truck haulier (names removed). Why they agreed, "the mornings are a mess, send the
video". Decider question asked in the confirmation, the owner joined. Notes, "we redo the plan four or five times before
8", "two planners, one leaving in March". Next step, a demo the following Tuesday at 10 with the owner and head of
planning, invite sent on the call. Hung up 11.32, follow up sent 11.40, 118 words. Deal moved to Discovery done, task for

**Bad example.** A prep sheet with the company's "About us" text pasted in, no notes, and a follow up sent two days later
that says "As discussed, our solution will save you 30%". It fails because the prep isn't about their problem, the claim
wasn't said on the call, and there's no next step.

**Stuck?**
- **"No call came in yet."** Use transcript 3 now and submit the real one later. Calls often come in week 4 or 5.
- **"I forget to propose the next step."** Write it in big letters at the bottom of your prep sheet and set a phone
  timer for 5 minutes before the end.
- **"The follow up takes 30 minutes."** Your notes are too long. Fill only the four boxes, then run the prompt.

### Module 7. Your CRM and your scorecard

**Goal.** Keep your CRM correct in a few minutes a day, track the handful of numbers that show what brings meetings, and
run a 20 minute review every week that decides one change.

**Time.** 25 minutes of video, plus about 1 hour of assignment and 5 minutes a day of logging for 2 weeks.

**You need.**
- Your CRM (company or free HubSpot)
- The **Personal Scorecard** template in Google Sheets
- Your Assignment 0 audit and your sequence's report

#### Lesson 7.1 Logging without pain (6 min)

**In plain words.** Logging means writing what happened into your CRM, the calls, replies, meetings and deal stages. Most
sellers hate it because they do it in one painful batch. Done at the right moment it takes seconds, and it's the only way
your numbers are true.

**You'll be able to.**
- Log a reply, call or meeting in under a minute
- Connect your inbox and calendar so most logging happens by itself
- Use one end of day check to catch what you missed

**What's shown.**
1. The rule. Log at the moment it happens, never at the end of the week. A reply gets logged when you answer it. A call
   when you send the follow up.
2. Screen, HubSpot. Connect your inbox and calendar. Click **Settings** (gear icon), then **General** or **Email**, find
   the email integration and connect your inbox and calendar. Emails with contacts and meetings now show on their records.
   In your company's CRM, ask your admin whether email and calendar sync is on.
3. Screen. The HubSpot browser extension or add in for your email. When you send an email, tick **Log** so it saves to
   the contact.
4. Screen. lemlist and other sequencers can push activity into your CRM. In lemlist, look under **Settings**,
   **Integrations**, and connect HubSpot or your CRM. Check with your admin first in a company CRM.
5. What you still log by hand. LinkedIn conversations (copy the key message into a note), the outcome of each call, deal
   stage changes, and next step tasks.
6. The end of day check, 3 minutes. Open today's calendar and today's replies. Is every call, meeting and reply on a
   record with a next step? Fix what's missing.

**Do it now.** Connect your inbox and calendar to the CRM, and log today's replies and calls. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 7.2 Hygiene rules for one person (6 min)

**In plain words.** CRM hygiene means keeping the data correct, complete and without duplicates. You can't fix your whole
company's CRM, but you can keep your own records clean with five rules.

**You'll be able to.**
- Apply the five hygiene rules to your own records
- Merge a duplicate contact
- Keep deal stages honest

**What's shown.**
1. **Rule 1. Search before you create.** Always search the CRM for the company and person before adding them. This stops
   duplicates (two records for the same person or company).
2. **Rule 2. Every open deal has a next step and a date.** If it doesn't, it's not really open.
3. **Rule 3. Stages mean what they say.** Only move a deal to "Meeting booked" when there's an invite they accepted, and
   to "Proposal sent" when it's sent. Use your company's stage definitions, or the practice pack's (New, Meeting booked,
   Discovery done, Demo done, Proposal sent, Won, Lost).
4. **Rule 4. Close what's dead.** A deal with no reply in 60 days goes to Lost with a reason. Hopeful deals make your
   numbers lie.
5. **Rule 5. Opt outs marked at once.** "Do not contact" set the same day.
6. Screen. Merging duplicates in HubSpot. Open one of the two contacts, click **Actions**, **Merge**, search for the other,
   choose which details to keep, click **Merge**. In other CRMs, look for merge on the record, or ask your admin.
7. Screen. Practice pack. Open the messy CRM export. Find two duplicates of the same person and the three spellings of
   the Netherlands. Show how sorting by email finds duplicates fast.
8. A 10 minute Friday clean up, inside the weekly review. Filter your open deals with no next step task, and fix them.

**Do it now.** Filter your open deals for ones without a next step date and fix every one. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 7.3 Your personal scorecard (7 min)

**In plain words.** A scorecard is a short list of the few numbers you check every week. It shows which activity actually
brings meetings. This one has the numbers Raka's own engine tracks, plus your hours, so you can compare with your Module 0
audit.

**You'll be able to.**
- Fill the Daily log tab in 5 minutes a day
- Read the Weekly tab and its conversion rates
- Explain each number in one sentence

**What's shown.**
1. Screen. Open the **Personal Scorecard** template. Three tabs. Daily log, Weekly, Notes.
2. The numbers, one sentence each.
   **Invites and first messages sent.** How many people you contacted for the first time.
   **Accepts.** How many accepted your LinkedIn invite.
   **First messages sent within 2 days of an accept.** How fast you act when someone accepts. Slow means warm contacts go
   cold.
   **Positive replies.** Replies that show interest, like a yes or a real question.
   **Meetings booked.**
   **Meetings held with the person who decides.**
   **Proposals sent.**
   **Wins and their value.**
   **Hours on prospecting.** Finding, researching and writing, from a quick daily estimate.
3. Screen. Daily log. One row per day. Type the counts from your sequencer report, CRM and calendar.
4. Screen. Weekly tab. Each row is one week. It adds up the daily rows with a formula like this one.

   ```
   =SUMIFS(Daily!C:C, Daily!A:A, ">="&A2, Daily!A:A, "<"&(A2+7))
   ```

   You don't need to write it. It's in the template. Column A holds the Monday of each week.
5. Conversion rate, explained. The share of things that move to the next step. Positive reply rate is positive replies
   divided by people messaged. The Weekly tab shows reply to meeting and meeting to proposal too.
6. Where to get each number fast. Sequencer report for sent, accepts and replies. CRM for meetings, proposals and wins.
   Your calendar for meetings held and who was there.
7. A filled example week. 40 first messages, 22 accepts, 18 of them messaged within 2 days, 5 positive replies, 2
   meetings booked, 1 held with the person who decides, 0 proposals, 4.5 hours prospecting. These are example numbers
   for the practice pack, not targets.

**Do it now.** Copy the scorecard and fill today's row. 5 minutes.

#### Lesson 7.4 The weekly 20 minute review (6 min)

**In plain words.** Once a week, you look at your scorecard for 20 minutes and decide one thing to change. Without this,
you keep doing what you did, even when it isn't working. With it, the engine gets better every week.

**You'll be able to.**
- Run the review in 20 minutes with a fixed agenda
- Find where your numbers drop and what that usually means
- Write one change for next week and check it the week after

**What's shown.**
1. Same time every week, 20 minutes, a calendar block called "Weekly review". Friday afternoon works for most.
2. Minutes 0 to 5. Fill the week's numbers and do the 10 minute Friday clean up from lesson 7.2 if you haven't.
3. Minutes 5 to 10. Compare with last week and with your one number from Module 0. Up, down, flat.
4. Minutes 10 to 15. Find the biggest drop between two steps and read what it usually means.
   **Few accepts.** The list or your LinkedIn profile.
   **Accepts but few replies.** The message, usually the first line or the problem.
   **Replies but few meetings.** The offer or the question at the end.
   **Meetings but not with the person who decides.** Targeting, or not asking who decides.
   **Meetings but no proposals.** The call, the next step wasn't booked.
5. Minutes 15 to 20. Write one change in the **Notes** tab with the date. "Next week, step 1 asks to send a sample plan
   instead of a video." One change only, so you know what worked.
6. Next week, the first thing you check is whether last week's change moved the number.
7. Screen. Three filled weeks of Notes, with the change and the result each time.

**Do it now.** Put the weekly 20 minute review block in your calendar and write your first change idea in Notes. 5
minutes.

### The brief

For 30 days, run the engine every week.

| Every week | Time | From |
|---|---|---|
| 15 minute signal routine, up to 5 actions | 15 min | Module 2 |
| Research the accounts from those actions with your prompt and the falsification pass | About 50 min | Module 3 |
| Add them to your sequence, answer every reply the same working day | About 50 min | Modules 4 and 5 |
| Prep and follow up every call, next step booked on the call | About 15 min per call | Module 6 |
| Log daily, run the 20 minute weekly review, make one change | 5 min a day plus 20 min | Module 7 |

Keep at least 30 accounts in the sequence over the 30 days, and add new researched accounts each week as signals come in.

### Steps

1. On day 1, write the start date in your scorecard's Notes, with your one number, its starting value from Module 0 and
   your target.
2. Run the weekly routine above for 4 weeks plus 2 days. Don't skip a weekly review.
3. In the last week, log your time in the Module 0 buckets again for 5 working days, using the same Time log tab in a new
   copy of the audit sheet. This gives you a fair before and after.
4. On day 30, open your scorecard and total the 30 days. First messages sent, accepts, first messages within 2 days,
   positive replies, meetings booked, meetings held with the person who decides, proposals, wins and value.
5. Open your Module 0 audit. Put the numbers side by side in the **Before and after** table in the Capstone One Pager.
   Meetings per month, meetings by source, hours per week on finding, researching and writing.
6. Count meetings that came from the engine (from your sequence or a signal) and meetings from other sources separately.
   Don't give the engine credit for a referral.
7. List your weekly changes and what each did to the numbers.
8. Write the one page (template below). Keep it to one page.
9. Remove buyer names and anything confidential. Use "a 90 truck haulier" instead of the company name.
10. Share as a view link with the scorecard, the second audit and the one pager.

### The one page, section by section

| Section | Length | What goes in |
|---|---|---|
| My patch and my one number | 2 lines | Who you sell to, the number you chose, start value and target |
| Before and after | A small table | Meetings per month, meetings held with the person who decides, hours per week on finding, researching and writing. Module 0 against the 30 days |
| What the engine produced | 3 to 5 lines | Accounts contacted, positive replies, meetings booked and held, proposals and wins if any. Engine meetings and other meetings shown apart |
| What I changed and what it did | 3 to 4 bullets | One per weekly review, with the number before and after |
| What didn't work | 2 to 3 lines | Honestly. A signal that never fired, a message that got no replies |
| What I'll keep doing for the next 90 days | 3 bullets | The routine as you'll run it from now on |

### Hand in

The view link to your Capstone One Pager, with links inside it to your scorecard (30 days of data) and your second time
audit. 1:1 learners hand in at least 48 hours before the 30 day check in. Self learn learners who want Certified buy the

### Marking rubric

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 | Must? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Engine ran for 30 days | Under 2 weeks | 2 to 3 weeks, or weeks skipped | 30 days, weekly signal routine and reviews all logged | Must |
| Scorecard data is real and complete | Missing or typed in at the end | Gaps of more than 3 days | Daily data for the whole period, matches sequencer and CRM | Must |
| Fair comparison with Module 0 | No comparison | Compared, but different measures or no second time audit | Same measures before and after, second 5 day time audit done | Must |
| Engine credit is honest | Every meeting claimed for the engine | Sources mixed up in places | Engine and other meetings shown apart, nothing claimed that isn't backed | |
| Changes tested | No changes | Changes listed without results | One change per review, each with its number before and after | |
| What didn't work | Missing | Vague | Specific, with what it means for next time | |
| Messages stayed honest and legal | Evidence of invented facts, tricks or ignored opt outs | Minor slips fixed during the 30 days | Checked facts, opt outs honoured, country rules followed | Must |
| One page, clear | Several pages or unreadable | Long or missing a section | One page, every section, plain words | |

**Pass.** At least 70% of the points, so 12 of 16, and no 0 on a criterion marked must. A pass doesn't depend on getting
more meetings. An honest 30 days with a flat result and a clear reason passes. A great number you can't back up fails.

**Good example.** An AE selling HR software to Dutch companies with 50 to 250 staff. One number, outbound meetings held
per month, from 2 to 5. Before and after. Meetings per month 3 (2 outbound, 1 referral) against 6 (4 from the engine, 2
from other sources). Held with the person who decides, 1 of 3 against 4 of 6. Hours on finding, researching and writing,
14 a week against 6. Engine produced 52 accounts contacted, 9 positive replies, 5 meetings booked, 4 held, 1 proposal.
Changes. Week 1, checked accepts every morning, messages within 2 days went from 11 of 20 to 18 of 19. Week 2, asked who
decides in every confirmation, 3 of 3 meetings that week with the person who decides. Week 3, swapped the video offer
for a one page sample, positive replies went from 2 to 3 out of a similar number sent, too few to judge. Didn't work. The
funding alert fired twice in 30 days, replaced with a job ad alert. Next 90 days. Signal routine on Monday, research on
Tuesday, review on Friday.

**Bad example.** "I did loads of outreach and got way more meetings, around 10, the course really works." No scorecard
link, no second time audit, no split between engine and referral meetings. It fails because nothing is measured against
the starting point and the number can't be checked.

**Stuck?**
- **"My numbers went down."** That's allowed. Find the step where they dropped (lesson 7.4), say what you'd change, and
  hand it in. Honesty passes, inflation fails.
- **"I missed a week."** Say so in the one pager, and extend the 30 days by a week if you can. Don't fill the gap with
  guesses.
- **"I can't fit it on one page."** Cut adjectives and history. The table and the bullets carry it.

### The presentation, in the 1:1 check in (30 minutes)

| Minutes | Part |
|---|---|
| 0 to 10 | You present from the one pager. Your patch and one number, before and after, what you changed, what didn't work, your next 90 days. Screen share your scorecard |
| 10 to 20 | Raka asks questions. He'll open a random week of your scorecard and one random message from your sequence and ask you to show the source of its first line and the falsification check you ran on it |
| 20 to 27 | Raka scores the rubric out loud, criterion by criterion, and says what would make each 1 into a 2 |
| 27 to 30 | Result. Pass means Certified. If not, the one thing to fix and a date to resubmit (two reviewed resubmissions) |

**Self learn, for Certified.** Record the same 10 minute presentation on video (any screen recorder) and hand it in with

would have asked.
