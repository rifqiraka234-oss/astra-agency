The role course for sales managers. Five modules, every lesson, quiz and assignment, the practical test, the final exam
and the capstone. It follows the rulebook in `README.md` in this folder. Prices set by Raka on 6 Nov 2026.

## Who it's for

Sales managers, heads of sales, sales directors and founders who run a sales team of 3 to 30 people.

You don't build the flows yourself. You decide what gets built, you get the team to use it, and you answer for the
forecast. You may know selling very well and still feel lost when someone says "webhook" or "enrichment waterfall".
That's fine. Every technical word is explained in plain language the first time it comes up, and nothing asks you to code.

**Not for you if** you have no team and want to build the systems yourself. Take the ladder (Level 1 Operator, then
Level 2 Builder). If you're a seller who wants more meetings for yourself, take GTM Seller.

## Where it sits

The academy is a ladder of skill (Level 1 Operator, Level 2 Builder, Level 3 Architect) plus two role courses. GTM Leader
is a role course. It doesn't need a high GTM Engineering Score, because it teaches you to run the system, not to build it.
If your free score comes out low and you'd like to build things yourself later, add Level 1 Operator after this course.

**You're a GTM Leader when**
- you can show where your pipeline leaks, with numbers, in under 30 minutes
- your team sells from a written sales bible they helped write, and the CRM enforces its stages
- every lead has an owner and a time limit, and the data your team is paid on is checked
- you run the week from one dashboard and make a forecast you can defend deal by deal
- you can say whether to build, buy or hire, with the cost written down and sourced

### Module 1. Quick win and the operating model

**Goal.** Find your single biggest pipeline leak today, understand what GTM engineering changes in your job, and draw how
revenue moves through your team with an owner on every step.
**Time.** 40 minutes of video + 1.5 hours assignment.
**You need.** Your CRM login (or the practice pack), a Google account, templates `T3-00 Team current state` and
`T3-01 Operating model` (you'll copy them in Lesson 1.3).

#### Lesson 1.1 Quick win. Your biggest leak in 30 minutes (10 min)

**In plain words.** A leak is a place where deals fall out of your process for a reason you could fix. You'll pull last
quarter's deals out of your CRM, count how many reached each stage, and see which step loses the most. It takes about 30
minutes, and it's the number the rest of the course works on.

**You'll be able to.**
- Export last quarter's deals from your CRM
- Work out the **conversion rate** between stages (the share of deals that move from one stage to the next)
- Name your biggest leak with the number that proves it

**What's shown.**
1. Why numbers first. People remember the dramatic lost deal. The numbers show the quiet leak that costs the most.
2. Export from HubSpot. Click CRM, then Deals. Switch to the table view (the list icon). Click the Create date filter and
   set it to last quarter. Click Export, choose CSV, include all properties. The file arrives by email.
3. Salesforce and Pipedrive, briefly. In Salesforce, open Reports, click New Report, choose Opportunities, filter Close
   Date or Created Date to last quarter, then Export. In Pipedrive, open the Deals list view, filter, then the three dots
   menu and Export filter results.
4. Open it in Google Sheets. Click File, Import, Upload, pick the file, choose Insert new sheet, click Import data.
5. Find out how far each deal got. The practice pack has a column called Furthest stage. In a HubSpot export, look for the
   columns named "Date entered" plus the stage name. A deal reached a stage if that column has a date.
6. Build the leak table on a new tab. Click the + at the bottom left to add a sheet. Type the stage names down column A,
   in order (New, Meeting booked, Discovery done, Demo done, Proposal sent, Won).
7. Count deals that reached each stage. With the practice pack, first add a helper column next to Furthest stage that
   turns the stage into a number. `=MATCH(D2,{"New","Meeting booked","Discovery done","Demo done","Proposal sent","Won"},0)`
   (change D to your Furthest stage column). Then on the leak tab, in B1 type `=COUNTIF(Sheet1!K:K,">="&ROW())` where K is
   the helper column, and drag down to B6. With HubSpot "Date entered" columns, use `=COUNTA()` on each column instead.
8. Conversion. In C2 type `=B2/B1`, drag down to C6, then click Format, Number, Percent.
9. Read it. The lowest percentage is your biggest leak. Select that cell, click the paint bucket and choose red.
10. Write the leak as one sentence. "Our biggest leak is Demo done to Proposal sent. 18 deals reached Demo done, 9 got a
    proposal, 50%. My guess at why is that AEs wait for the buyer to ask for pricing." (Illustrative numbers.)
11. Three kinds of leak to guess from. Speed (leads wait too long). Fit (wrong companies get in). Process (a step people
    skip, like sending the proposal). Your guess gets tested later in the course.

**Do it now.** (30 min) Do steps 2 to 10 on your own export or `routeful-pipeline-q3.csv`. Keep the sheet. It goes into
Assignment 1 and you'll measure against it at day 90. If your export is blocked, ask your CRM admin today and use the
practice pack until it arrives.

#### Lesson 1.2 What GTM engineering changes for a manager (9 min)

**In plain words.** For years the answer to "we need more sales" was "hire more people". GTM engineering is the other
answer. You build systems (lists, data, automations, dashboards) so each person you already have spends more time selling.
A GTM engineer builds them. Your job is to know what to ask for, and your week changes from chasing updates to reading
numbers and coaching.

**You'll be able to.**
- Explain GTM and GTM engineering in one sentence each
- Write one clear request a GTM engineer could act on
- Compare your current week with the week you want

**What's shown.**
1. **GTM (go to market)** is everything a company does to find customers and sell to them. **GTM engineering** is building
   the systems behind that, so selling runs on a machine instead of on memory.
2. The headcount answer. One SDR (sales development representative, the person who books first meetings) books a number of
   meetings. Hire two more and you expect three times as many. It rarely happens, because each new person searches by
   hand, writes from scratch and types notes differently.
3. The systems answer. The same SDR gets a ready list of good fit companies every Monday, a research summary per account,
   a tested message and a CRM that fills half the fields by itself.
4. Astra's own numbers. Raka's engine sent 330 researched first messages between 14 July and 25 October 2026, and 26 people
   said yes (7.9%). In October, 119 first pitches got 30% replies and 15% said yes, against 4% in September. 7 meetings by
   25 October, no paid project yet. The lesson. Consumer businesses said yes most often and couldn't pay. B2B companies
   with a budget were the ones worth meeting. Who you target is a management call.
5. What a GTM engineer builds, one line each. **Lists** (the companies and people that fit). **Enrichment** (adding missing
   facts to a record, like an email or company size). **Signals** (events that make a company more likely to buy now).
   **Sequences** (a planned series of messages). **Automations** (steps that run by themselves). **Dashboards** (a page of
   charts showing how things are going). **RevOps** (revenue operations) runs the processes and data. In small teams one
   person often does both.
6. **API**, in one line. A way for one piece of software to talk to another. If two tools "have an API", they can usually
   be connected. You never need to use one.
7. A bad request. "Can you make the CRM better?" A good one. "When a deal moves to Demo done, I want the AE to get a task to
   send the proposal within 2 days, and I want a weekly report of deals where that didn't happen." The shape is when this
   happens, do this, show me this number.
8. How to judge their work. Does it run without them? Is it written down? Does a number show whether it helped?
9. Your week, before and after. Before, hours asking "what's the status?", fixing the CRM, forecasting from memory. After,
   a 30 minute pipeline review from a dashboard, a 10 minute hygiene check, two hours of coaching from calls, one hour on
   the process. You still own the forecast, still hire, still help close the big deals. The first month is more work, not
   less.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Open last week's calendar. Mark each block "chasing", "admin", "coaching", "selling" or
"improving the process" and add up the hours. Then write one request in the shape from step 7 for the admin task that
annoys you most.

#### Lesson 1.4 Your revenue model (6 min)

**In plain words.** A revenue model is how your company actually makes money. Who pays, how much, how often, and how long
they stay. Your process has to fit it, and the model tells you how many opportunities you need each month.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write your revenue model in four lines
- Work out the opportunities the team needs per month
- Spot when the process doesn't fit the model

**What's shown.**
1. The four lines. Who pays. What they pay (average deal value). How often (one off, monthly, yearly). How long they stay.
2. Routeful. Logistics firms pay a yearly licence based on fleet size and renew yearly.
3. Working backwards. In a new Sheet type the yearly target in B1, average deal value in B2, win rate in B3 (as a percent).
   In B4 type `=B1/B2` for deals needed. In B5 type `=B4/B3` for opportunities needed. In B6 type `=B5/12` for per month.
4. **Win rate** means won deals divided by won plus lost.
5. When model and process clash. Small deals with six meetings each. Huge deals with no step for legal review.
6. What it tells you to automate. Many small deals, automate volume work. Few big deals, build research and account plans.

**Do it now.** (5 min) Build the six cell sheet with your target and your Lesson 1.1 numbers. Write down the
opportunities needed per month.

#### Lesson 1.5 Owners and handoffs (8 min)

**In plain words.** Every step a customer goes through needs exactly one owner. When two people own a step, nobody does it.
A **handoff** is the moment one person passes a lead or customer to the next, and it's where information gets lost. This
lesson gives every step an owner and writes every handoff down.

**You'll be able to.**
- List every step from first touch to renewal and give each one owner
- Write each handoff as trigger, information and time limit
- Run a two minute handoff check

**What's shown.**
1. The steps as boxes. Attract, Capture, Qualify, Meet, Discover, Demo, Propose, Close, Onboard, Grow and renew.
2. Routeful's owners. Marketing owns attract and capture. SDRs own qualify and meet. AEs own discover to close. Nobody owns
   onboard or renew.
3. In Google Sheets, put steps down the side and roles across the top. Type R (responsible, does it) and A (accountable,
   answers for it) in the cells. One R and one A per step. Select the rows, click Format, Conditional formatting, choose
   "Custom formula is", type `=COUNTIF($B2:$G2,"*A*")=0`, set the fill to red. Rows with no owner turn red.
4. The four handoffs. Marketing to SDR (a lead came in). SDR to AE (a meeting is booked). AE to CS (customer success, who
   look after customers after the sale) when a deal is won. CS back to AE (upsell or renewal).
5. For each one write three things. The trigger, the information that must travel, and how fast the next person acts.
6. Routeful's SDR to AE handoff, before. Sanne books a meeting and Lotte walks in knowing only the company name. After.
   Sanne fills four CRM fields (fleet size, current planning tool, who will attend, why they agreed to meet) before the
   meeting counts as booked.
7. Time limits as numbers. A website lead is called within one working hour. A won deal gets a kick off call within five
   working days. "Quickly" means something different to everyone.
8. The two minute check. Open three deals booked last week and see if the four fields are filled.
9. The fix isn't always a hire. Often it's one line in the sales bible or one automation ("when a deal is won, create an
   onboarding task").

**Do it now.** (10 min) Build the steps and roles table for your team and mark the red rows. Write your most painful
handoff in three lines. Trigger, information, time limit.

#### Lesson 1.6 After the sale (5 min)

**In plain words.** Most sales teams stop thinking when the contract is signed. A customer who stays and buys more is worth
far more than a new one. **Loyalty or retention flows** are the steps after the sale that keep customers using and buying.

**You'll be able to.**
- Map the steps from signed contract to renewal
- Name the warning signs that a customer might leave
- Add one loyalty flow with an owner

**What's shown.**
1. **Churn** means customers who stop buying. **Customer health** is a score that shows whether a customer will likely stay.
2. The steps. Kick off call. First value (the moment they get something real from you). Regular check in. Renewal talk.
   Upsell.
3. Warning signs. Usage drops. The person who bought from you leaves. Support tickets pile up. They stop replying.
4. Routeful. A customer's planners stop logging in for three weeks. Nobody notices until the renewal is refused.
5. A simple flow. When logins drop by half for two weeks, create a task for the account owner to call within 3 days. When
   the main contact changes job, send a welcome note to the replacement.
6. No CS person? Write the AE's name in the box. An owner who also sells beats no owner.

**Do it now.** (5 min) Write three warning signs for your customers and who would notice each one today.

#### Quiz 1 (pass 80%)

1. 40 deals reached Demo done and 12 reached Proposal sent. What's the conversion?
   A. 30%
   B. 70%
   C. 333%
   D. 52%
   **Answer.** A. **Why.** 12 divided by 40. **Rewatch.** 1.1
2. True or false. You should find leaks by asking the team first, then check the numbers.
   **Answer.** False. **Why.** Numbers first, because people remember dramatic deals and miss quiet leaks. **Rewatch.** 1.1
3. What is GTM engineering?
   A. Hiring more SDRs
   B. Running ads
   C. Building the systems (lists, data, automations, dashboards) behind finding and selling to customers
   D. Writing code for the product
   **Answer.** C. **Why.** It's the machinery behind selling, so selling runs on a system. **Rewatch.** 1.2
4. True or false. If one SDR books 10 meetings a month, hiring two more always gives you 30.
   **Answer.** False. **Why.** Without shared lists, tested messages and clean data, each new hire repeats the manual work.
   **Rewatch.** 1.2
5. In Astra's own outreach, which leads said yes most often and then couldn't pay?
   A. B2B software firms
   B. Engineering firms
   C. Logistics companies
   D. Consumer businesses
   **Answer.** D. **Why.** A yes from someone who can't buy is worth little, so who you target is a management call.
   **Rewatch.** 1.2
6. Which is the best request to a GTM engineer?
   A. "Make the CRM better" B. "When a deal moves to Demo done, create a task for the AE to send a proposal within 2
   days, and show me a weekly list of deals where it didn't happen" C. "Automate sales" D. "Fix our data"
   **Answer.** B. **Why.** It says when, what, and which number shows it worked. **Rewatch.** 1.2
7. Your contact export holds names and email addresses. What do you do with it?
   A. Keep it in your company Drive, share only what's needed, delete it after the course
   B. Upload it to a free online tool to clean it
   C. Email it to Raka
   D. Print it
   **Answer.** A. **Why.** It's personal data under GDPR. Not legal advice, check your policy. **Rewatch.** 1.3
8. Target €600,000, average deal €20,000, win rate 30%. How many opportunities do you need for the year?
   A. 30
   B. 90
   C. 100
   D. 180
   **Answer.** C. **Why.** 600,000 / 20,000 = 30 deals, then 30 / 0.3 = 100. **Rewatch.** 1.4
9. True or false. It's fine for two people to both be accountable for the SDR to AE handoff, so there's a backup.
   **Answer.** False. **Why.** Two owners usually means nobody does it. One R, one A. **Rewatch.** 1.5
10. What three things do you write down for every handoff?
    A. The trigger, the information passed on and the time limit
    B. The lead source, the industry and the company size
    C. The rep's name, the deal value and the close date
    D. The meeting notes, the email thread and the recording
    **Answer.** A. **Why.** Without these, information drops and nobody acts fast. **Rewatch.** 1.5
11. Scenario. Routeful's AEs walk into first meetings knowing only the company name. What's the best fix?
    A. Hire another AE
    B. Cancel SDR meetings
    C. Require the SDR to fill four fields before a meeting counts as booked

### Module 2. The sales bible

**Goal.** Write version one of your team's sales bible, with your team, so everyone sells the same way and means the same

**Time.** 30 minutes of video + 2 hours assignment (including a 45 minute team session).
**You need.** Template `T3-02 Sales bible` (a Google Doc), Assignment 1, your CRM stage list, 45 minutes booked with your

#### Lesson 2.1 Stages with exit criteria (8 min)

**In plain words.** A sales bible (or playbook) is the written way your team sells. Who you sell to, how, what to say, and
what each stage means. Its backbone is the list of stages, and for each one the **exit criteria**, the things that must be
true before a deal moves to the next stage.

**You'll be able to.**
- Explain what a sales bible is for
- Write exit criteria that two people would judge the same way
- Tie each criterion to a CRM field

**What's shown.**
1. Why it exists. When every rep decides what "Demo done" means, the pipeline is a collection of opinions, and so is your
   forecast.
2. The test for a good criterion. Could two people look at the same deal and agree whether it's true? If not, rewrite it.
3. Routeful's Discovery done, bad. "We understand their needs." Good. "Fleet size, current planning tool, who signs and
   target start date are all filled in the CRM."
4. Every Routeful stage with a good criterion. New (fits the ICP, one named contact). Meeting booked (date in calendar, four
   handoff fields filled). Discovery done (the four facts above). Demo done (held with the signer or their direct report,
   next step agreed with a date). Proposal sent (sent by email, price included, follow up date set). Won (contract signed).
   Lost (loss reason chosen from the list).
5. If you can't see it in a field, you can't check it. Each criterion names its field.
6. Three to five criteria per stage at most. More and nobody reads them.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write your stages down. Next to each, one exit criterion that passes the "two people agree" test.

#### Lesson 2.2 Definitions everyone uses the same way (6 min)

**In plain words.** Words like "lead", "qualified" and "opportunity" mean different things to different people. A
definition list fixes the meaning of the five to ten words your team argues about, so reports and conversations line up.

**You'll be able to.**
- Find the words your team uses differently
- Write a one sentence definition for each
- Put definitions where people see them while they work

**What's shown.**
1. The test. Ask three people separately "what's a qualified lead?" Show three different Routeful answers.
2. The usual suspects. Lead, contact, qualified, opportunity, decision maker, pipeline, closed lost, churned.
3. Routeful's. **Lead** is a person at a company that fits the ICP, with no conversation yet. **Qualified** means we've
   spoken, they run 15 or more trucks, plan by hand or in an old TMS, and want to change within 12 months.
   **Opportunity** is a qualified company with a meeting booked. **Decision maker** is the person who signs.
4. Put them on page one of the bible and in the CRM. In HubSpot click Settings (gear icon), Properties, search for the
   field, click it, type the definition in the Description box, Save. It now shows when anyone hovers over the field.
5. When the team disagrees, you decide, write it down, and review in a month. A decided definition beats a perfect one
   argued about forever.

**Do it now.** (5 min) Message three team members the same question. "What makes a lead qualified, in one sentence?"

#### Lesson 2.3 Playbooks per segment and objection handling (8 min)

**In plain words.** A **segment** is a group of companies inside your ICP that you treat the same way. A playbook is the
one page plan for selling to one segment. Who to talk to, what problem to lead with, which proof to use, and how to answer
the objections that come up.

**You'll be able to.**
- Split your market into two or three segments
- Fill a one page playbook for each
- Write objection answers from what actually worked

**What's shown.**
1. Routeful's segments. Small fleets (15 to 40 trucks, owner decides). Mid fleets (40 to 150, operations director). Large
   fleets (150 or more, head of planning plus IT).
2. The playbook page, five boxes. Who we talk to. The problem we lead with. The proof we use. Objections and answers. The
   next step we ask for.
3. Filled for small fleets. The owner. "Your planners rebuild the plan in Excel every morning." A short story from a similar
   customer. Objection "we're too small for software". Next step, a 20 minute screen share of their own routes.
4. The message structure for your team, six lines. A greeting with a specific observation about their company. The
   problem you noticed. What it costs them. Why it matters now. One line of proof. A low effort question (offer to show
   something, not "book a call"). One problem from start to finish.
5. An **objection** is a reason a buyer gives for not moving forward. Write it in the buyer's words, then the answer your
   best rep actually used, then the next question.
6. Routeful. "We already have a TMS." Answer. "Most of our customers do. We plan the routes, your TMS still handles the
   paperwork. Which part of planning takes your team the longest today?"
7. Answers come from won deals and your best reps, not from you. That's what the team session is for.
8. Honesty rule. No made up proof, no fake "Re:" subject lines, no invented personal connections. If you can't back a
   claim, it doesn't go in the bible.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write the top three objections your team hears, in the buyer's own words. Leave the answers blank.

#### Lesson 2.4 Writing it with the team, and keeping it alive (8 min)

**In plain words.** A bible written by the manager alone gets ignored. One written with the team gets used, because it
holds their words. Most bibles then die within three months, so this lesson also gives you owners, one way to suggest
changes, and a 15 minute monthly review.

**You'll be able to.**
- Run a 45 minute session where the team writes the bible
- Handle the rep who says "I don't need this"
- Set owners, a change process and a monthly review

**What's shown.**
1. Why people resist. They think it's about control, or that their way works fine. Both are reasonable.
2. The pitch, in your words. "This is so I stop asking you for updates, so leads get shared fairly, and so new people get
   up to speed faster."
3. The 45 minute session. 5 minutes why. 15 minutes stages and exit criteria (you show your draft, they correct it). 10
   minutes definitions (show of hands). 15 minutes objections (each person brings one, the team agrees the best answer).
4. Share the doc as Commenter. In Google Docs click Share, add the team, choose Commenter. Show a rep highlighting text,
   clicking the comment icon and suggesting a change.
5. The rep who pushes back. Give them a section to own, usually the one they complained about. Owners defend what they
   wrote.
6. The cover page. Version number, last updated date, owner per section (Routeful, Sanne owns SDR, Lotte owns objections,
   Eva owns stages and definitions) and "suggest changes as comments".
7. The monthly review, first 15 minutes of a team meeting. Read new comments, accept or reject each, bump the version.
   Update sooner for a new competitor, a price change, a new segment, or a deal lost for a reason not in the bible.
8. Name versions. Click File, Version history, Name current version, type "v1.0".
9. New hires read it in week one and suggest one change. In 1:1 the session becomes Raka's 90 minute team workshop.

**Do it now.** (5 min) Book the 45 minute session in the next 7 days. Write the invite with the why in two sentences.

### Module 3. ICP, routing and CRM hygiene

**Goal.** Agree an ICP from won deals, give every lead an owner and a time limit, then set the minimum data your team keeps,
protect the data commission is paid on, and check it all in 10 minutes a week.
**Time.** 40 minutes of video + 2.5 hours assignment.
**You need.** Last quarter's won and lost deals, your account list and a contact export (or the Routeful files),
Assignment 2, templates `T3-03 ICP territory routing` (tabs ICP, Territories, Signals, Routing) and `T3-04 Hygiene rules`
(tabs Rules, Audit, Weekly check). Admin or manager access to your CRM helps but isn't required.

#### Lesson 3.1 Agreeing the ICP from won deals (7 min)

**In plain words.** An **ICP** (ideal customer profile) describes the companies most likely to buy from you and stay. Most
teams have one someone wrote years ago. You'll build yours from evidence, by comparing the deals you won with the ones you
lost.

**You'll be able to.**
- Compare company facts across won and lost deals
- Write an ICP as must haves, nice to haves and disqualifiers
- Define tiers the team agrees with

**What's shown.**
1. **Persona** is the type of person you talk to inside those companies. **Firmographics** are facts about a company like
   size, industry and location.
2. Astra's lesson again. Consumer businesses said yes most often and couldn't pay. B2B companies with a budget were the
   ones worth meeting. The ICP decides who your effort goes to.
3. In the Routeful pipeline add columns for employees, fleet size, country and current planning tool (from the accounts
   sheet). Click Data, Create a filter, filter Stage to Won and look. Then filter to Lost.
4. The comparison table. For each fact, the share of won deals that have it and the share of lost deals.
   `=COUNTIFS(stage_col,"Won",fleet_col,">=15")/COUNTIF(stage_col,"Won")`, then the same with "Lost".
5. Read it. Won deals mostly plan in Excel or an old TMS. Several lost deals already had a modern TMS. That's a
   **disqualifier**, a fact that rules a company out.
6. Write the ICP in three parts. Must have. Nice to have. Disqualifiers.
7. **Tiers.** Tier 1 matches every must have and two nice to haves and gets the most effort. Tier 2 matches the must haves.
   Tier 3 is the rest that isn't disqualified.
8. Getting agreement. Show the team the table, not your opinion. Ask "does this match what you see?" and add what they know
   that the data doesn't.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Build the won against lost comparison for two facts. Write down which one separates them more.

#### Lesson 3.2 Territories and signals (6 min)

**In plain words.** A **territory** is the set of accounts a seller owns. Good ones are fair and clear. A **signal** is
something that happens which makes a company more likely to buy now, like a job ad, a new boss or a pricing page visit.
Signals tell your team who to contact this week.

**You'll be able to.**
- Split and balance territories by Tier 1 count
- Write the ownership rules for edge cases
- Pick three to five signals and the action each triggers

**What's shown.**
1. Four ways to split. Region, segment, industry, named accounts. Routeful. Lotte has the Netherlands, Daan Belgium, Mira
   Germany plus large fleets everywhere. Sanne supports Lotte and Daan, Joris supports Mira.
2. Balance check. Add a Territory column, then Insert, Pivot table, Territory in Rows, count of Tier 1 accounts in Values.
   If one seller has 1.5 times another's Tier 1 count, rebalance or write why not.
3. Edge cases, written down. Two countries, head office decides. A referral goes to the territory owner. A rep leaves, the
   manager holds the accounts until reassigned.
4. **Intent** means signs a company is actively looking for something like what you sell.
5. Routeful's signals. A job ad for a transport planner. A new depot. A new operations director. A pricing page visit. A
   customer contact moving to a new company.
6. The signal table. Signal, where we see it, what it triggers, who acts and how fast. Example. "Planner job ad, job
   boards weekly, a message about planning time, territory SDR within 2 working days."
7. How a GTM engineer collects them, briefly. Clay watches job boards and news, HubSpot tracks known visitors, n8n (a tool
   that connects apps) posts a signal into team chat. You decide what's collected, you don't build it.
8. A signal is a reason to look, never proof. Astra checks every claim before a message goes out and tries to prove it
   wrong first. A job ad might be old. Keep signals you can find in a few won deals. Start with three, not thirty.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write your split in one line per seller and a rule for the edge case that causes most arguments.
Write three signals with where you'd see each.

#### Lesson 3.3 Routing and speed to lead (7 min)

**In plain words.** **Routing** means automatically giving a lead to the right person. **Speed to lead** is how fast someone
follows up after a lead comes in. You'll write routing rules as plain sentences, measure your current speed and set an
alert for slow leads.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write routing rules as "if this, then that", with a catch all
- Measure your median speed to lead
- Set an alert when a lead waits too long

**What's shown.**
1. The shape. If (source, type of company, territory), then (owner, task, how fast).
2. Routeful's rules. "A website demo request from the Netherlands goes to Lotte with a call task due within 1 working
   hour." "Fewer than 15 trucks goes to the nurture list, not a seller." "Already owned keeps its owner."
3. The catch all. "No rule matches, the manager assigns within 4 working hours." Without it, odd leads are lost.
4. **Round robin** gives leads in turn to each person in a group. Use it where territory doesn't decide.
5. In HubSpot, click Automation, Workflows, Create workflow, From scratch, Contact based. Trigger "Form submission". Add
   "Rotate record to owner", or an If/then branch on Country, then "Create task". Workflows depend on your plan, check it.
   Without them, the rules go in the bible and you route by hand twice a day.
6. Measure speed. Export last month's new contacts with Create date and Last contacted. In Sheets add `=(D2-C2)*24` for
   hours, then `=MEDIAN(E2:E)`.
7. **Median** is the middle value when sorted. One lead that waited three weeks won't distort it.
8. The target as a rule. "Inbound demo requests get a first call within 1 working hour, other inbound within 1 working day."
9. The alert. In a workflow add a 1 hour delay, an If/then "Last contacted is unknown", then "Send internal notification"
   to the manager. Without workflows, a saved view of new leads with no activity, checked at 11.00 and 16.00.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write at least three routing rules and a catch all. Measure the median for last month, or the
Routeful inbound rows.

#### Lesson 3.4 The minimum data, and required fields per stage (7 min)

**In plain words.** **CRM hygiene** means keeping CRM data correct, complete and without duplicates. Reps skip updates
because it's slow, they get nothing back, and the data gets used against them. So ask only for what a report or decision
uses, and ask for it at the stage where it matters, so the CRM enforces your exit criteria.

**You'll be able to.**
- Cut required data to the minimum
- Set required fields per stage
- Swap free text for dropdowns

**What's shown.**
1. A **field** is one piece of information on a record, like job title.
2. The fix for each reason. Fewer fields and automation. Use the data to help them (their hottest deals listed every
   Monday). Bring it up when you coach, never as blame.
3. The minimum data test. For every field ask "which report or decision uses this?" None means not required.
4. Routeful's set. Contact, name, email, job title, company, country. Company, fleet size, current planning tool,
   territory, owner. Deal, amount, stage, close date, next step, next step date, loss reason if lost.
5. Your bible's "CRM field that proves it" column is the required fields list. Meeting booked needs the four handoff
   fields. Discovery done needs fleet size, current tool, signer, start date. Proposal sent needs amount and close date.
   Lost needs a loss reason.
6. In HubSpot click Settings (gear icon), Objects, Deals, the Pipelines tab. Next to a stage click Edit stage properties
   (the name varies by version), add the fields, Save. Test it by dragging a deal to the next stage and seeing the pop up.
   Salesforce and Pipedrive have the same idea under other names, ask your admin.
7. A **dropdown** is a field where you pick from a fixed list. Free text gives "NL", "Netherlands" and "Holland". In
   Properties, change Country to a dropdown. Loss reasons too, under eight options. Price, Chose competitor, No decision or
   went quiet, Bad fit, Timing, Other (with a note).
8. The deal with the team. "I'll cut required fields to these and stop asking for updates in Slack. In return, these are
   always filled."

**Do it now.** (10 min) Mark each deal field "used" or "not used". Write the required fields for your three most important
stages, and set one up on a test deal if you have admin rights.

#### Lesson 3.5 Duplicates, ownership and automating the boring part (6 min)

**In plain words.** A duplicate is the same person or company in the CRM twice. **Deduplication (dedup)** means finding and
merging them. Ownership rules say who owns each record. And the best hygiene rule is one a tool does, so people only type
what only people know.

**You'll be able to.**
- Find duplicates in an export and set a merge rule
- Write ownership rules
- Sort hygiene tasks into "tool" and "person"

**What's shown.**

#### Lesson 4.1 The five numbers and stage aging (8 min)

**In plain words.** A **dashboard** is a page of charts and numbers that shows how things are going. Five numbers tell a
manager almost everything. **Stage aging**, the days a deal has sat in its current stage, is the fastest way to spot deals
that are quietly dead.

**You'll be able to.**
- Name the five numbers and what each tells you
- Tell a leading number from a lagging one
- Flag deals stuck at twice the normal time

**What's shown.**
1. The five. **Pipeline created** (value of new deals this month). **Pipeline coverage** (open pipeline for the period
   divided by target). **Conversion by stage** (your Lesson 1.1 table). **Win rate**. **Sales cycle** (average days from
   created to won).
2. What each answers. Are we filling the pipe? Is there enough? Where do we leak? Do we win when it matters? Faster or
   slower?
3. A **lagging** number tells you what happened (revenue won). A **leading** one tells you what's coming (meetings booked
   this week). You manage the leading ones.
4. Astra's weekly scorecard as leading numbers. Invites or messages sent, accepts, first messages sent within 2 days,
   positive replies, meetings booked, meetings held with the decision maker, proposals sent, wins and value.
5. Coverage you need is about 1 divided by your win rate. Win 30%, you need more than 3 euros of pipeline per euro of
   target.
6. Count meetings held and proposals sent, not calls made. Busy isn't progress.
7. Stage aging in Sheets. With a "last stage change date" in column H, type `=TODAY()-H2` and drag down.
8. Normal time per stage is the median days won deals spent there. Flag with `=IF(I2>2*normal_days,"STUCK","")`. In
   HubSpot, search the properties list for "time in" and add those columns to a deals view.
9. Routeful has several deals in Proposal sent for over 60 days, still counted in the forecast. They shouldn't be.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write your five numbers for last month. Add stage aging to your open deals and count the stuck ones.

#### Lesson 4.2 Forecast categories (7 min)

**In plain words.** A **forecast** is your best estimate of what will be sold this month or quarter. Instead of one number
from gut feel, every deal goes into one of three categories with written rules. Then the forecast is built from deals you
can name and defend.

**You'll be able to.**
- Put any deal in the right category
- Compare it with a weighted forecast and explain the gap
- Track your own accuracy

**What's shown.**
1. Routeful's rules. **Commit**, you'd bet your bonus on it. Proposal accepted verbally, signer engaged, close date agreed
   with the buyer, no open blocker. **Best case**, could close this month. Proposal sent, signer met, close date this
   month, one open question. **Pipeline**, open and real, not this month.
2. The rules go in the bible next to the stages, so reps and manager categorise the same way.
3. Report two numbers. Commit is your call. Commit plus Best case is your upside.
4. A **weighted forecast** is each deal's value times its stage win chance, added up. In Sheets add "Stage win chance" with
   `=VLOOKUP(F2,stage_table,2,FALSE)`, then `=G2*J2`, then sum it.
5. Compare. Commit far above weighted means you're optimistic. Far below, check for sandbagging (reps keeping good deals out
   of Commit to look good later).
6. In HubSpot add a forecast category field (Settings, Properties, Create property, dropdown with the three values). Some
   plans have one built in, check yours.
7. At month end write what you forecast and what closed. Over three months you'll see your own bias.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Put 10 open deals into the three categories using the rules. Write the totals.

### Module 5. Rolling it out

**Goal.** Plan the next 90 days so the team actually uses what you designed, decide what to automate first, and choose
whether to build, buy or hire a GTM engineer with the cost written down.
**Time.** 27 minutes of video + 1.5 hours assignment.
**You need.** Every earlier assignment, template `T3-07 90 day plan` (tabs Plan, Automate first, Build buy hire, Payback).

#### Lesson 5.1 Adoption, making it stick (6 min)

**In plain words.** **Change management** means helping people move from the old way to the new way so it sticks. Most new
processes fail because people slip back after two weeks. The design is rarely the problem.

**You'll be able to.**
- Introduce each change in the right order
- Spot early that people are slipping back
- Handle the people who resist

**What's shown.**
1. Why changes fail. Too many at once. No reason given. The manager stops checking after week two. The old way still works.
2. The order for each change. Tell them why. Show them how (5 minutes on screen). Make the old way harder (required fields
   mean they can't skip). Check it weekly. Praise the first people who do it well, by name.
3. One change at a time per area. Not the bible, new routing and a dashboard in the same week.
4. Adoption you can measure. Share of deals meeting exit criteria. Hygiene score trend. Bible comments per month. Who opened
   the dashboard (HubSpot and Looker Studio both show it).
5. The person who resists. Ask what they'd lose. Often it's real (more clicks). Fix what you can, explain what you can't.
6. Your own behaviour. Ask for updates in Slack instead of reading the dashboard, and the team learns the dashboard doesn't
   matter.

**Do it now.** (5 min) List every change from your assignments. Put them in the order you'd introduce them.

#### Lesson 5.2 Sprints and what to automate first (6 min)

**In plain words.** A **sprint** is a fixed block of time, usually two weeks, with three to five tasks and one goal.
Sprints keep the rollout moving without flooding the team, and force you to choose what to automate first.

**You'll be able to.**
- Score automation ideas and pick the first ones
- Write a sprint goal that can be checked

**What's shown.**
1. Each sprint ends with a 20 minute look back. What worked, what to change.
2. Score each idea 1 to 5 on four questions. Hours saved per week. How often errors happen today. How easy it is to build.
   How much the team will notice. Add them up.
3. Routeful scored. Stale deal alert high (easy, ends Eva's Sundays). Inbound routing high. Full enrichment of every account
   lower (more cost, less urgent).
4. Usually first. Required fields per stage, the stale deal alert, inbound routing, email and meeting logging. Cheap, and
   reps feel them fast.
5. Usually later. Complex scoring, AI research agents, full multichannel sequences. They need clean data first.
6. A good goal. "By 20 March, every inbound demo request is routed and called within 1 working hour, measured on the
   dashboard." A bad one. "Improve routing."

**Do it now.** (10 min) Score five automation ideas from your assignments.

#### Lesson 5.3 Build, buy or hire a GTM engineer (7 min)

**In plain words.** Three ways to get the systems built. Build it yourself or with someone on the team. Buy it from an
agency or freelancer. Or hire a GTM engineer. This lesson gives you a cost comparison so you decide with numbers.

**You'll be able to.**
- Say when each option fits
- Fill a 12 month cost comparison from sourced figures
- Explain the choice to your boss

**What's shown.**
1. **Build.** Low cash cost, high time cost, risky if that person leaves. Fits small teams with one technical person.
2. **Buy.** Faster, someone has done it before. A project fee, and someone must keep it running after. Fits teams that need
   the base built quickly.
3. **Hire.** Ongoing salary, keeps improving the system, takes months to hire. Fits teams big enough to keep one person busy.
4. The template rows per option. Setup cost. Monthly cost (salary, fees, tools). Your own hours per month times what your
   hour is worth. Months until it's running. Risks.
5. Getting numbers. Salaries, look up current figures for your country (job sites, recruiter reports) and write the date.
   Agency fees, ask two for a quote. Tools, each pricing page. Never guess.
6. 12 month cost in the sheet. `=setup+(monthly*12)+(your_hours*hourly_value*12)`.
7. A common mix. Buy the first build, then someone on the team (or a part time GTM engineer) keeps it running.
8. Astra is one of the options you could buy from. The template works the same whichever you choose.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Fill the hours and months rows for all three. Leave the money rows until you've looked up real
figures.

#### Lesson 5.4 Payback and your 90 day plan (8 min)

**In plain words.** **Payback** is how long it takes for what you gain to cover what you spent. Your boss will ask. Then
everything comes together in one page, six sprints over 90 days, each with a goal, owner and measure.

**You'll be able to.**
- Estimate payback in months, honestly
- Lay out six sprints with goals, owners and review points
- Fit the plan on one page

**What's shown.**
1. Two sources of gain. Time saved (hours per week times people times hourly cost). More revenue (better conversion times
   average deal value).
2. Payback months is total cost divided by monthly gain. Routeful example, Eva's 3 hours a week plus 2 per rep, and Demo
   done to Proposal sent rising from 50% to 60%. (Illustrative numbers.)
3. Your before numbers come from Assignment 1 and the dashboard. That's why you wrote them down on day one.
4. Separate what you measured (hours saved, conversion change) from what you estimate (revenue it caused). Raka reports
   what Astra's engine produced and doesn't claim revenue that hasn't closed. Do the same with your boss.
5. The Plan tab. Sprints 1 to 6 with Goal, Changes, Owner, Build buy or hire, Measure, Done when.
6. Routeful's sprints. Bible v1.1 and required fields. Routing and speed to lead. Dashboard and the first reviews. Hygiene
   automation and the stale deal alert. Win and loss review and coaching plans. Signals and the first message changes.
7. Review at day 30, 60 and 90 against the before numbers. List one or two habits that stop (the Friday status email) and
   your risks (a key rep leaves, the admin is busy, budget isn't approved).
8. One page. If it doesn't fit, cut the least important change.

**Do it now.** (10 min) Write the three before numbers you'll compare at day 90, then fill sprints 1 and 2.

6. Rehearse once with a timer, 15 minutes maximum. Present it. Ask one person to note questions.
7. Write a half page reflection. What they asked, what they agreed, what you'll change.

**Good example.** Eva presents to Routeful's managing director. Slide 1, "We lose half our deals between the demo and the
proposal". Slide 7, Commit €37k, Best case €55k more, "last month I forecast €40k, we closed €33k". Slide 9 recommends
buying the first build, every figure sourced and dated. Slide 10, "Approve the first build and 4 hours a week of Sanne's
time to maintain it, decision by 30 November." (Illustrative numbers.)

**Bad example.** 34 slides, the first eight about GTM engineering in general, undated numbers, no forecast reasoning, and a
last slide saying "Questions?". It fails because the system isn't shown on the team's own data, it runs over time, and
there's no ask.

**Stuck?**
1. *Too much to fit.* One picture and one claim per slide. The rest goes in the notes.
2. *My boss won't give me 15 minutes.* Present slides 1, 7, 9 and 10 in five minutes and send the rest.
3. *My numbers got worse during the course.* Show them. Often the data just got honest (dead deals closed out). Say so.
