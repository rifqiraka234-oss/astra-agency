## Who it's for

Account executives, SDRs and BDRs, founders who still do the selling, consultants and freelancers who sell their own
work. You don't want a new job. You want more meetings with the right people and less time lost copying, pasting and
remembering.

You don't need a technical background. If you can use a spreadsheet and LinkedIn, you can do every step here.

**Not for you if** you want to build engines for other people as your job (start at Level 1 Operator), or you manage a
team and want them all working this way (take GTM Leader).

## Where this sits

The academy is a ladder of three levels (Operator, Builder, Architect) plus two role courses. GTM Seller is a role
course. It takes the parts of the ladder a seller needs and leaves out the building.

## The promise

In three weeks you run your own small pipeline engine. A pipeline engine is a simple routine you repeat every week that
brings you meetings without depending on your memory. It has four parts.

1. The right accounts, written down and ranked.
2. A reason to reach out now, from alerts you set up once.
3. A message about their problem, researched in about 10 minutes with AI and checked by you.
4. Follow ups that go out by themselves, and a weekly review that tells you what's working.

Once it's set up it takes about 2 to 3 hours a week. Your first result comes in the first 30 minutes, in Lesson 1.1.

### Module 1. Quick win, your pipeline and your list

**Goal.** Get one researched account and an opener in your first 30 minutes, then measure where your time and meetings
come from and build a tiered list of 50 accounts that look like your best customers.

**Time.** 39 minutes of video. The quick win takes 30 minutes. Assignment 1 is about 1 hour 15 minutes plus 5 minutes a
day for 5 working days.

**You need.**
- A Google account and a free Claude account (or your company's approved AI tool)
- Your calendar and your CRM (or your inbox and LinkedIn if you have no CRM)
- The **Time and Source Audit** and **ICP and Tier List** templates, and the **Prompt Library** doc (course Drive folder)
- Your won deals from the last 12 months, or the practice pack's 50 accounts and pipeline sheet
- LinkedIn, and Sales Navigator if you have it

#### Lesson 1.1 Quick win, one account and a 4 line opener in 30 minutes (9 min)

**In plain words.** Before any setup, you'll do the thing this course is about, once. Pick one company you'd like as a
customer, let AI research it, check what it found, and write a short first message about one problem they probably have.
It takes 30 minutes and it's the shape of everything that follows.

**You'll be able to.**
- Run the quick win prompt on a real company
- Open the source of each fact and keep only the ones that are true
- Write a 4 line opener about one problem

**What's shown.**
1. Pick the account. A company you'd like to sell to, that nobody else at your company is talking to. Practice pack
   learners use Brugveld Transport (made up) and its pages in the pack.
2. Screen. Go to claude.ai and sign up for a free account, or open your company's approved AI tool. Rule for the whole
   course. Only paste public company information into an AI tool, never private customer data, unless your company says
   you may.
3. Screen. Open the **Prompt Library** doc, find **Quick win prompt**, copy it.

   ```
   I sell [what you sell, one sentence] to [who buys it, one sentence].
   Research [company name], website [URL]. If you can't browse the web, use only the text I paste below.
   [paste the homepage, the about page and one job ad or recent post, or write "search the web"]
   Give me four short parts.
   One. What they do and for whom, in two sentences.
   Two. Three facts that matter for what I sell. Each fact with the exact link or pasted text it came from.
   If you can't point to a source, leave it out.
   Three. The one problem these facts most likely point to, marked as a guess, and what it would cost them.
   Four. A four line LinkedIn message. Line 1 a greeting and one fact from part Two. Line 2 the problem.
   Line 3 what it costs them. Line 4 a question they can answer yes to in one word, offering to send something small.
   Don't invent numbers, names or quotes. Write "not found" when you don't know.
   ```

4. Screen. Fill the brackets, paste the page text, run it. If your tool can't browse, open the homepage, the about page
   and one job ad yourself, select all the text, copy and paste it in. Pasting is slower but you know what it read.
5. Screen. The check, the step that matters most. Click each fact's link. Press Ctrl+F (Cmd+F on a Mac) and find the
   sentence on the page. If it's there, keep it. If it isn't, or the page is about a different company, delete the fact.
6. Screen. Write the 4 lines yourself, using the AI's draft only as a starting point. Routeful example.

   > Hi Mark, I saw Brugveld opened a second depot last month and is hiring a planner with strong Excel skills.
   >
   > Two depots in one spreadsheet usually means every morning change lands on one person.
   >
   > That's drivers waiting and empty kilometres while the plan gets redone.
   >
   > Shall I send a 2 minute video of what a two depot morning looks like in Routeful?

7. Read it on your phone and cut until you don't have to scroll. Don't send it yet. It becomes your first contact when
   your sequence goes live in Module 3, where you'll learn the checks to run before anything goes out.

**Do it now.** The full quick win. Pick the account (5 min), run the prompt (10 min), check every fact (10 min), write
the 4 lines (5 min). Save the opener in your Prompt Library under "Quick win". 30 minutes.

#### Lesson 1.3 Where your time and meetings come from (8 min)

**In plain words.** Most sellers think they spend their week selling. When they log it, a big share goes on finding
people, copying data and admin. This week you measure where your time goes and where your meetings come from, then pick
one number to move. At the end of the course you'll compare against it.

**You'll be able to.**
- Log your working time in six buckets without slowing down
- List every meeting from the last 3 months with its source
- Choose one countable number to move in 30 days

**What's shown.**
1. Screen. Open your copy of **Time and Source Audit**. Tabs are Setup, Time log, Meetings, Summary, One number.
2. The six buckets. **Finding** (looking for accounts and people). **Researching** (reading about an account before you
   contact it). **Writing** (messages and notes). **Talking** (calls and meetings with buyers). **Admin** (CRM, internal
   meetings, reports). **Other**.
3. Screen. **Time log**. One row per block of 15 minutes or more. Date, Start, End, Bucket (dropdown), What I did.
   Minutes fills itself. Example row. "Mon, 9.00 to 9.40, Finding, searched LinkedIn for planners at 6 firms."
4. The rule. Log at lunch and at the end of the day, never from memory on Friday. Memory flatters you.
5. Two words for the Meetings tab. A **meeting** is a first call with a possible buyer that actually happened. A
   **source** is how it started. **Outbound** (you contacted them first), **Inbound** (they contacted you first, for
   example through a website form), **Referral** (someone introduced you), **Event**, **Existing customer**.
6. Screen. Go back 3 months in your calendar and add a row per first call. Date, Company, Person, Role, Source, Became a
   deal (yes or no). In HubSpot, click **CRM** then **Deals**, filter **Create date** to the last 3 months, add the
   column **Original source**, export. In another CRM, look for the deal list, a created date filter and a source field,
   or ask your admin. Practice pack learners filter the pipeline sheet to **Meeting booked** or later.
7. Screen. **Summary** shows hours per bucket, meetings per source and meetings per hour by source.
8. The one number. Three examples. "Outbound meetings held per month, from 2 to 5." "Hours a week finding people, from 6
   to 2." "Meetings held with the person who decides, from 1 in 4 to 3 in 4." Pick one you can count every week. Only one,
   or you can't tell what moved it.

**Do it now.** Log today so far, then fill the Meetings tab with every first call from the last 3 months (the last 30 if
there are more). 15 minutes.

#### Lesson 1.4 Your ICP and tiers, read off your wins (8 min)

**In plain words.** An ICP (ideal customer profile) is a short description of the companies most likely to buy from you
and stay. You don't guess it, you read it off the customers who already bought. A tier is how good a fit an account is.
Tier 1 gets your best effort, Tier 3 waits.

**You'll be able to.**
- Write your ICP in one sentence with numbers in it
- Name 2 or 3 personas (the types of people inside those companies who buy)
- Write a tier rule anyone could apply, and an out list

**What's shown.**
1. One word. **Firmographics** are facts about a company, like size, industry and location. Your ICP is firmographics plus
   one or two facts about how they work today.
2. Screen. In HubSpot, click **CRM**, **Deals**, filter **Deal stage** is **Closed won** and **Close date** in the last
   12 months, export. Practice pack learners filter the pipeline sheet to **Won** and look each company up in the 50
   account sheet.
3. Screen. Paste into the **Won deals** tab of **ICP and Tier List**. Company, Industry, Staff, Country, What they used
   before, Who signed, Deal value. Read the rows and look for what repeats.
4. Routeful's ICP. "Transport and logistics firms in the Netherlands, Belgium and Germany with 20 to 500 staff, 15 or more
   own trucks, planning in Excel or an old TMS." Personas, operations director, head of planning, owner or managing
   director.
5. Astra's lesson. In Raka's own outreach, consumer businesses said yes most often and couldn't pay. B2B companies with a
   budget were the ones worth meeting. Put "can they pay" into your ICP.
6. Screen. **Tier rules** tab. **Tier 1** matches every part of the ICP and has a sign they could buy soon (signals come
   in Module 2). **Tier 2** matches the ICP, nothing about timing. **Tier 3** matches most of it, one part missing or
   unknown. One line each.
7. Effort per tier. Tier 1 gets full research and a written message. Tier 2 gets one checked fact and the template. Tier
   3 waits for a signal.
8. The out list. Who you never contact. For Routeful, freight brokers with no trucks and firms under 15 trucks. For you,
   think of who wasted your time before.

**Do it now.** Fill the Won deals tab, write your ICP sentence, personas, tier rules and out list. 15 minutes.

#### Lesson 1.5 Lookalikes and a clean list (9 min)

**In plain words.** A lookalike is a company that looks like one of your best existing customers. It's the fastest honest
way to grow a list. A list you build from your ICP is smaller than a bought one and much better, because bought lists are
usually old and wrong.

**You'll be able to.**
- Find lookalikes with LinkedIn or Sales Navigator
- Add 1 to 3 contacts per account who match a persona
- Check for duplicates and accounts a colleague owns

**What's shown.**
1. Pick your 3 best customers. Best means they bought, paid, stayed and were easy to work with. Not just the biggest.
2. Screen, free LinkedIn. Type an industry word, for example "transport", press Enter, click **Companies** at the top,
   then **All filters**. Set **Locations** and **Company size**.
3. Screen. On a best customer's LinkedIn page, find the list of similar companies in the right column (often called
   **Pages people also viewed**). Open each and check it against your ICP.
4. Screen, Sales Navigator. Click **Account filters**, set **Industry**, **Headquarters location** and **Company
   headcount**, then **Save search** with a name like "ICP NL hauliers". On an account page, open **Similar accounts**.
5. Screen. The **Account list** tab. Company, Website, LinkedIn page, Country, Staff, ICP fit, Tier, Why this tier, Owner,
   Contact name, Contact role, Contact LinkedIn, Source. One minute per account on the website is enough. Deep research is
   Module 2.
6. The duplicates check. Search your CRM before adding an account. If a colleague owns it or there's an open deal, move
   it to the **Parked** tab or ask first. Two people from one company contacting the same buyer kills trust.
7. Screen. Contacts. On the company's LinkedIn page, click **People**, type a persona word like "planning", pick 1 to 3
   people, copy name, role and profile link. In Sales Navigator, use **Decision makers** on the account.
8. Data minimisation (keep only what you need). Name, role, company, LinkedIn link, a work email later, and where you
   found it. No private phone numbers or home addresses. More on the rules in Lesson 3.5. Not legal advice, check your
   country.
9. Screen. Sort by Tier and count. A good first list of 50 has 10 to 20 Tier 1.

**Do it now.** Find 10 lookalikes of your best customer, tier them, and run the duplicates check on each. 15 minutes.

- **"Everything looks like Tier 1."** Your rule is too loose. Tier 1 needs a sign of timing. Without one, it's Tier 2.

### Module 2. Who to contact and why

**Goal.** Set up alerts that tell you which accounts have a reason to talk this week, then research those accounts in
about 10 minutes each with AI and turn every one into a checked reason to reach out.

**Time.** 38 minutes of video, plus about 1 hour 30 minutes of assignment (30 minutes of setup, a 15 minute routine a
week later, and about 10 minutes per researched account).

**You need.**
- Your Assignment 1 list
- LinkedIn (Sales Navigator if you have it), a Google account for Google Alerts, Gmail or your email

#### Lesson 2.1 Signals, and the three that matter for you (7 min)

**In plain words.** A signal is something that happens at a company that makes it more likely to buy now. A new boss, a
job ad, a new site. Your ICP says whether an account is the right type (fit). A signal says whether now is a good moment
(timing). You want both.

**You'll be able to.**
- Name the six signal types with an example for your market
- Pick the 3 signals that came before your own wins

**What's shown.**
1. **Hiring.** A job ad for a role your product helps. A haulier hiring a second planner means planning is stretched.
2. **Funding or growth.** New money, a new site, a takeover, a big contract. Growth breaks old ways of working.
3. **Leadership change.** A new operations director in their first 90 days often reviews tools and suppliers.
4. **Tech change.** They start or stop using a tool, often visible in a job ad ("experience with [tool]").
5. **Content.** The person or company posts about the problem you solve. "Our planners worked the weekend again."
6. **Website visits.** If your company tracks which companies visit its site, a pricing page visit is a strong signal.
   Ask marketing if this exists. If not, skip it.
7. Picking your 3. Look at 3 won deals from Assignment 1. What changed at each company in the months before they bought?
   Those are your signals.
8. Screen. Open **Signal Tracker**, **Signals** tab. One line per signal, the won deal that showed it, and what you'd say
   about it.

**Do it now.** For 3 won deals, write what changed before they bought. Pick your 3 signals and fill the Signals tab. 10
minutes.

#### Lesson 2.2 Alerts and your 15 minute routine (9 min)

**In plain words.** You don't check 50 accounts every morning. You set alerts once and they bring the news to you. Then
once a week, for 15 minutes, you turn what came in into a short list of actions.

**You'll be able to.**
- Follow Tier 1 accounts and set a LinkedIn job alert and a Google Alert
- Save a Sales Navigator search that watches your accounts, or do the same check by hand
- Run the 15 minute weekly routine and keep a list of buyers' own words

**What's shown.**
1. Screen. On each Tier 1 account's LinkedIn page, click **Follow**. Tier 1 only, or your feed turns to noise.
2. Screen. Click **Jobs**, type a role that signals need ("transport planner"), set the location, press Enter, turn on
   **Set alert** near the top of the results, choose daily or weekly.
3. Screen. Go to google.com/alerts. Type a company name in quotes, or a topic and a place, for example "new distribution
   centre" Utrecht. Click **Show options**. **How often** at most once a day, **How many** only the best results, your
   language and region. Click **Create Alert**. Test the query on Google News first. Nothing in the last month means
   change it.
4. Screen. In Gmail, open one alert, click the three dots, **Filter messages like these**, **Create filter**, tick **Skip
   the Inbox** and **Apply the label**, new label "Signals".
5. Screen, Sales Navigator. Click **Lists**, **Account lists**, **Create list**, add your Tier 1 and 2 accounts. Run a

6. Without Sales Navigator. Once a week, check the **People** tab of each Tier 1 company for new names in your persona

7. Reading a job ad in 2 minutes. The role, the tools named (Excel, an old TMS), the words about pain ("growing fast",

9. The eavesdrop habit. Raka's name for listening to what buyers say in calls, posts and reviews and using their words.
   Screen. The **Their words** tab, one phrase per row with where you heard it. "Half the loads change the same
   morning." In Module 3 these phrases go into your messages.

**Do it now.** Follow your Tier 1 accounts, set one job alert and one Google Alert, make the Signals label, and book the
weekly 15 minute block. 15 minutes.

#### Lesson 2.3 The research prompt (7 min)

**In plain words.** A prompt is the instruction you give an AI. The research prompt is a longer version of your quick win
prompt. It asks for facts with their source, keeps them apart from guesses, and focuses on what you sell. You write it
once and reuse it on every account.

**You'll be able to.**
- Fill the course research prompt for your market
- Give the AI the right pages to work from

**What's shown.**
1. What you want. Three things only. What they do and for whom. What might be hard for them in the area you help with.
   Why now.
2. Screen. The research prompt in your **Prompt Library**.

   ```
   You are helping me prepare to contact a company. I sell [what you sell, one sentence] to [your ICP sentence].
   The company is [company name], website [URL]. The person is [name], [role].
   Here is what I found [paste page text, job ads, posts, or say "search the web"].
   Give me five short sections.
   One. What they do and for whom, in two sentences.
   Two. Facts that matter for what I sell. Each fact on its own line with the exact source link or the pasted text it
   came from. If you can't point to a source, leave it out.
   Three. What might be hard for them right now in [your area]. Mark every point as a guess and say what it's based on.
   Four. Why now. Any recent change, such as hiring, a new site, a new leader, a post. Give the date and source.
   Five. Three things I should check myself before I write to them.
   Don't invent numbers, names or quotes. Say "not found" when you don't know.
   ```

3. What to feed it. Four pages are enough. The homepage, the about page, one job ad or post from your signal, and the
   person's LinkedIn headline and recent posts.
4. Screen. Run it for Hollandse Ritten BV (made up) with the pack pages pasted in. Point at a fact with a source, a guess
   marked as a guess, and a "not found".
5. Save your filled prompt in the Prompt Library so you never write it from scratch again.

**Do it now.** Fill the research prompt for your market and run it on one Tier 1 account with a signal. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 2.4 Check every fact, then try to prove it wrong (9 min)

**In plain words.** AI tools sometimes state things that aren't true, with total confidence. A wrong fact in a message
loses the buyer. So you open every source yourself, and then you run a falsification pass, which means trying to prove
your own claim wrong before you use it.

**You'll be able to.**
- Open each fact's source and tick, fix or delete it
- Spot the usual AI mistakes
- Run the falsification pass in 3 to 5 minutes and set a confidence level

**What's shown.**
1. The rule. A fact goes into a message only if you opened the source yourself and it says the same thing. A search
   snippet isn't a source. The page is.
2. Screen. Click each fact's link, press Ctrl+F, find the sentence. Tick it, fix it or delete it.
3. The usual mistakes. **Same name, different company** (check the address or people match). **Old news** (a job ad from
   two years ago isn't a signal). **Made up numbers** (not on the page, deleted). **Mixed up people** (check they still
   work there).
4. Why the next step matters, told from Astra. Raka's research gate and falsification pass caught wrong claims before they
   went out. A different company with the same name, an old page, something called missing that was on another page.
5. The falsification question. "If I'm wrong about this, where would I see it?" Then open that page, not the one that made
   you think you're right.
6. Brugveld example. Claim, "they plan in Excel". Where would I see I'm wrong? Their careers page, their posts, a
   planner's LinkedIn profile. Screen. Search the careers page for "TMS", "software", "system". Open one planner profile.
   No tool named, the planner lists Excel. Claim survives.
7. Claims that something is missing are the riskiest. "You have no online booking" is the sentence most likely to be
   false. Check at least two pages and search the site for the word first.
8. Confidence. **High** if two different sources agree. **Medium** if one solid source. **Low** if it's a guess. Only state
   high or medium as fact. Write a low as a question or drop it.
9. Screen. The **Research Card**. Facts (each with link and date checked), then the **Falsification** box. What I opened,
   what it showed, keep or change or drop, confidence. Leave out anything private about the person.

**Do it now.** Check every fact from your Lesson 2.3 answer, run the falsification pass, and fill the card. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 2.5 One reason to reach out (6 min)

**In plain words.** Research is only useful if it gives you a reason to talk the buyer would care about. A good reason is
one problem they probably have, what it costs them, and why it matters now. One problem, not five.

**You'll be able to.**
- Turn checked facts into one likely problem, its cost and a why now
- Write the reason in two sentences in the buyer's words

**What's shown.**
1. Three parts. **The problem** (what's hard for them in your area). **The cost** (time, money, customers, staff, risk).
   **Why now** (the signal, with its date).
2. Screen. Brugveld's card. Facts, hiring a second planner, the ad asks for strong Excel skills, a second depot opened last
   month. Problem, two depots in Excel is too much for one person. Cost, late changes mean empty kilometres and overtime.
   Why now, the depot and the job ad, both this month.
3. Found three problems? Pick the one that costs them most, not the first one you found.
4. Use their words from the Their words tab. "Loads changing the same morning" beats "dynamic route optimisation".
5. Screen. The **Reason to reach out** box. "Brugveld opened a second depot and is hiring a planner who knows Excel.
   Planning two depots in a spreadsheet usually means late changes and empty kilometres, which is what we fix."
6. A reason isn't a pitch. No features. It shows you understood their situation.
7. No good reason? Write "no reason yet", leave the account at Tier 2 and wait for a signal. That's a correct answer.

**Do it now.** Write the two sentence reason for the account you researched. 5 minutes.

### Module 3. Messages that go out and get replies

**Goal.** Write a 3 step sequence about one problem, build it in a sequencer, send it safely and legally to at least 20
contacts, and answer every reply the same working day.

**Time.** 40 minutes of video, plus about 1 hour 45 minutes of assignment (writing, setup and launch), then about 10
minutes a day on replies.

**You need.**
- Your Research Cards and the Their words tab
- Your company's sequencer, or a lemlist trial (start it in Lesson 3.3)
- Your email account, and a separate sending domain if you send cold email from your own setup
- LinkedIn
- The **Sequence Template**, **Legitimate Interest Note** and **Reply Playbook** templates

#### Lesson 3.1 The six part message (7 min)

**In plain words.** An opener is the first message you send someone. Your quick win opener had four lines. The full
version has six short parts, and every part is about the same single problem. Each part comes straight from a box on your
Research Card.

**You'll be able to.**
- Name the six parts and fill each from a Research Card
- Write an opener in about 10 minutes

**What's shown.**
1. The six parts.
   **1. Greeting and a specific observation.** Their name and one checked fact only true of their company.
   **2. The problem you noticed.** What that fact points to, in their words.
   **3. What it costs them.** Time, money, customers, staff, in their terms.
   **4. Why it matters now.** The signal, or their current goal.
   **5. One line of proof.** Something real you've done or seen. Never invented.
   **6. A low effort question.** Offer to send something small. Not "can we book a 30 minute call".
2. Screen. The Hollandse Ritten BV (made up) card next to the **Sequence Template**. Map each card box to a part, then
   write it.

   > Hi Mark, I saw Hollandse Ritten opened a second depot last month and is hiring a planner who knows Excel.
   >
   > Two depots in one spreadsheet usually means every morning change lands on one person, and drivers wait while the plan
   > gets redone. That's paid hours and empty kilometres every day.
   >
   > With the new depot just open, the way you plan now is the way you'll plan all winter.
   >
   > Hauliers your size use Routeful to plan both depots on one screen and redo a plan in minutes.
   >
   > Shall I send a 2 minute video of what a 60 truck Monday looks like in it?

3. About 90 words. Read it on your phone. If you have to scroll, cut.
4. Why the question works. "Shall I send" can be answered with one word. A meeting request asks a stranger for 30
   minutes.
5. Proof rules. Only true proof you can back up. No customer story yet? "I've spent 5 years with planning teams" is proof
   if it's true. Routeful's proof line is made up because Routeful is. Yours is always real.

**Do it now.** Write a six part opener from your best Research Card. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 3.2 One problem, honest, and the follow ups (7 min)

**In plain words.** Messages fail for the same few reasons. Too long, about too many things, about you instead of them,
or dishonest in a small way the reader spots at once. A follow up is a later message to someone who hasn't replied, and
each one adds something new about the same problem.

**You'll be able to.**
- Run the edit checklist on any message
- Spot fake personalisation and tricks
- Write steps 2 and 3 of the sequence

**What's shown.**
1. One problem start to finish. Screen. A draft about planning, then invoicing, then driver apps, cut to planning.
2. About them. More than one sentence starting with "we" or "I" means rewrite.
3. Phrases that look automated, cut them all. "I hope this finds you well." "I came across your profile." "Quick
   question." "Just following up."
4. Fake personalisation is a detail that looks personal but says nothing, like "great post!" without saying what was in
   it. It reads worse than none. Tricks cost trust. No "Re:" or "Fwd:" on a first email, no made up mutual connection, no
   "as promised" when nothing was promised.
5. The edit checklist in the Sequence Template. One problem. Under about 100 words. One "we" sentence at most. A checked
   fact in line 1. Real proof. A one word yes question. No tricks.
6. The 3 step sequence. **Step 1, day 1**, the opener, as a LinkedIn message if you're connected, otherwise email. Not
   connected? Send a LinkedIn invite the same day with no pitch. **Step 2, day 4**, the other channel, one new thing about
   the same problem. **Step 3, day 10**, a short last note that makes it easy to say no. Never two steps on the same day.
7. Screen. Step 2. "Mark, one more thought on the second depot. The planners we talk to say the hard part isn't the plan,
   it's the five changes before 8 am. I can show you how one gets redone in under a minute. Worth a look?"
8. Screen. Step 3. "Mark, I'll leave this here. If the second depot is running smoothly, ignore me. If the mornings are
   getting long, the 2 minute video is ready whenever you want it."
9. Email steps need a subject of 2 to 4 plain words, like "second depot planning". After step 3, stop. A new signal later
   means a new reason and a fresh start.

**Do it now.** Run the checklist on your opener, then write steps 2 and 3. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 3.3 Into the tool, and what you never automate (8 min)

**In plain words.** A sequencer sends your steps on time across email and LinkedIn and stops when someone replies. This
lesson builds your 3 steps in lemlist click by click and names the matching steps in other tools. It also draws the line
between what a tool may do and what you always do yourself.

**You'll be able to.**
- Connect your email and LinkedIn and build the 3 steps
- Import contacts with a checked first line each
- Run the per thread check before anything goes out

**What's shown.**
1. If your company has a sequencer, ask your admin three things. Which inbox you send from. Whether LinkedIn steps are
   allowed. Whether new sequences need approval. Outreach and Salesloft call it a sequence or cadence. HubSpot sequences
   need a paid Sales Hub seat, check your plan. Apollo has sequences. LinkedIn steps may be manual tasks there, which is
   fine.
2. Screen, lemlist. Start a trial from lemlist.com (check the pricing page for what it includes). Open **Settings**, the
   email or **Integrations** section, connect your sending inbox. For LinkedIn steps, install the browser extension from
   lemlist's settings and log in to LinkedIn in the same browser.
3. Screen. Click **Campaigns**, **Create campaign**, start from scratch, name it "[Your name], Tier 1, [problem],
   [month]".
4. Screen. Add leads, **Import a CSV**. Columns firstName, lastName, email, companyName, linkedinUrl, Country, Source, and
   **firstLine** holding the checked line from each Research Card. Match each column.
5. Screen. Build the steps. **LinkedIn invite** with no note or a very short one, a **delay** of 1 day, a **condition**
   "LinkedIn invite accepted". Yes branch, a **LinkedIn message** with step 1. No branch, an **Email** with step 1. Then
   3 days and step 2 on the other channel, then 6 days and step 3 by email.
6. Screen. Insert variables with the variable button, such as {{firstName}} and {{firstLine}}. A variable is a placeholder
   the tool fills from your CSV. Click **Preview** on 3 contacts and read each.
7. Screen. Campaign **Settings**. Stop for a lead when they reply. Working days, working hours, the buyer's time zone.
   Open tracking off, because it's unreliable and can hurt delivery.
8. What a tool may do. Send on time, stop on reply, fill name and company. What you always do. The research and the
   reason, the first line of every Tier 1 message, reading and answering every reply, deciding who goes in. Bulk AI first
   lines invent things unless you check every one.
9. The per thread check. Before anything goes to someone, open your whole conversation with them on LinkedIn and email,
   from the first message. Have they replied? Is a colleague talking to them? Did you promise anything? Raka's outreach
   runs this before every send, because a cold opener on top of a reply can't be undone.
10. Astra's numbers, honestly. Between 14 July and 25 October 2026 Raka's engine sent 330 researched first messages and
    26 people said yes, 7.9%. In October, 119 first pitches got replies from 30% and yeses from 15%, against 4% in
    September. By 25 October that had led to 7 meetings, and no paid project yet. Consumer businesses said yes most often
    and couldn't pay. B2B companies with a budget were the ones worth meeting. Your numbers will differ, and Module 5
    shows you how to measure them. Be wary of anyone who promises revenue from messages alone.

**Do it now.** Connect your email and LinkedIn and build the empty 3 step sequence, then write your "never automate" list
of 3 to 5 lines at the top of the Sequence Template. 15 minutes.

#### Lesson 3.4 Sending safely (7 min)

**In plain words.** Deliverability means whether your emails land in the inbox instead of spam. A sending domain is a
separate web address you send cold email from, so your company's main address isn't put at risk. Safe limits keep your
email and your LinkedIn account in good standing.

**You'll be able to.**
- Decide whether you need a sending domain and set it up
- Explain SPF, DKIM and DMARC in one sentence each
- Set safe daily limits and check a test lands in the inbox

**What's shown.**
1. Do you need one? If your company already runs cold email, use their setup and rules, and ask which domain and inbox to
   use. On your own, yes.
2. What it is. If your company is routeful.com, you register something like getrouteful.com, make one inbox on it with
   your real name, and send cold email from there. Replies reach a real person, and if it gets a bad reputation your main
   address is safe. A registration costs about the price of a lunch a year.
3. Screen. Register the domain, create the inbox in Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, and set the domain to forward
   visitors to your main website (a "redirect" or "forwarding" setting at the registrar).
4. Three settings. **SPF** lists which servers may send email for your domain. **DKIM** adds a signature that proves the
   email wasn't changed. **DMARC** tells inboxes what to do with mail that fails the other two. Your email provider's help
   pages give the records to paste into the domain's DNS settings (the page at the registrar where these records live).
   Send a test to a free checking site. All three green before any cold email.
5. Warm up. A new inbox has no reputation. Warming up means sending a few emails a day and slowly increasing. Turn on your
   sequencer's warm up the day the inbox exists and give it at least 14 days. Not finished by launch? Start with LinkedIn
   steps only.
6. Safe limits, the course's cautious defaults, not official numbers. 15 to 20 cold emails a day per warmed inbox, staying
   at or under about 40. 10 to 15 LinkedIn invites a day in the first week, never in bursts. Set them in the sequencer's
   daily limit settings, and check the tool's current advice and LinkedIn's own terms.
7. Verification means checking an address exists before you send. A bounce is an email that couldn't be delivered, and
   bounces hurt your reputation. Verify every address with your sequencer's checker or a verification tool.
8. Plain text. No images, no attachments, no links in the first email.

**Do it now.** Decide your path. Company setup, write down the inbox and limits you'll use. Your own, register the domain,
make the inbox and turn on warm up today. 15 minutes.

#### Lesson 3.5 The legal basics (5 min)

**In plain words.** Cold outreach uses people's personal data, so data protection law applies. These are the basics every
seller in Europe should follow. It isn't legal advice. Check your own country's rules and your company's policy.

**You'll be able to.**
- Fill a one page legitimate interest note
- Answer "where did you get my data?"
- Handle an opt out at once and know which countries need extra care

**What's shown.**
1. GDPR is the EU law on personal data. It covers business buyers' names, work emails and LinkedIn profiles too.
2. For B2B outreach the lawful reason is usually **legitimate interest**, a real business reason the person would
   reasonably expect that doesn't override their rights. You must be able to explain it and write it down.
3. Screen. The **Legitimate Interest Note**. Why I contact these people. Why I need their data (only name, role, company,
   work email, LinkedIn). Why it's fair to them (it's about their job, it's relevant, they can opt out in one reply).
4. Asked where you got their data? Tell them. "From your company's website and your LinkedIn profile." That's why the
   Source column exists.
5. Opt outs. "Stop", "remove me" or "unsubscribe" means out of every sequence at once and "do not contact" in your CRM the
   same day. Never argue.
6. Countries differ. The UK and the Netherlands are more permissive for emailing business addresses. In Germany,
   unsolicited B2B cold email is risky without consent, so use LinkedIn or a call first, or ask your legal team. Check
   every country you email.
7. LinkedIn has its own terms and limits. Stay inside the limits from Lesson 3.4. Your real name and company in every
   message.

**Do it now.** Fill your Legitimate Interest Note and add a **Do not contact** column to your account list. 5 minutes.

#### Lesson 3.6 Handling replies (6 min)

**In plain words.** Replies are where meetings happen, so you answer each one yourself, the same working day. An
objection is a reason the buyer gives for not going further. Most are a question in disguise.

**You'll be able to.**
- Sort every reply into one of eight types
- Answer each with the Reply Playbook, in your own words

**What's shown.**
1. Per thread check first. Read the whole conversation on both channels.
2. The eight types. **Yes**, **Question**, **Objection**, **Not now**, **Not me**, **Opt out**, **Out of office**, **No**.
3. **Yes.** Send what you offered the same day, then two specific times. "Here's the 2 minute video. If it's close to your
   mornings, would Tuesday at 10 or Thursday at 2 work for 20 minutes?"
4. **"Just send me some information."** One short thing about their problem, not a brochure, and one question.
5. **"We already have a tool."** Don't attack it. Ask what it does well and where planners still use a spreadsheet next
   to it.
6. **"No budget"** or **"Not now."** Ask when they plan spending or when to come back, put a dated task in your CRM, and
   name the signal that would bring you back.
7. **"Not me."** Thank them, ask who it is, and contact that person saying who pointed you there, if they agreed.
8. **Opt out** and **No**, one polite line, removed at once, do not contact. **Out of office**, note the return date.
9. Screen. In lemlist a reply stops the sequence. Mark the lead interested or not interested. Log the reply on the contact
   in your CRM (Lesson 5.1 shows a quick way).

**Do it now.** Copy the **Reply Playbook** and rewrite all eight answers in your own words. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 4.1 AI call prep in 10 minutes (8 min)

**In plain words.** Call prep means knowing before the call who you're talking to, what you think their problem is, and
what you want to happen next. Most of it is already on your Research Card. This lesson turns it into a one page sheet.

**You'll be able to.**
- Fill the Call Prep sheet from your card and the thread
- Draft questions and likely objections with a prompt
- Make sure the person who decides will be there

**What's shown.**
1. Screen. The **Call Prep** template. Who (name, role, time in role). Company in one line. Why they agreed (their words
   from the thread). The problem we think they have. Questions to check it. Questions about the cost. Who decides and how
   they buy. What I'll show. The next step I want. Likely objections and my answers.
2. Screen. Copy problem, cost and why now from the card. Copy their reply word for word into "Why they agreed".
3. Screen. The prep prompt from the Prompt Library.

   ```
   I have a 30 minute first call with [name], [role] at [company]. They agreed after this message [paste thread].
   My research card says [paste the card]. I sell [one sentence].
   Write 3 open questions to check whether the problem is real for them, 2 questions to understand what it costs them,
   1 question about who else decides and how they buy, and the 3 objections they're most likely to raise with a short
   answer to each. Use plain words. Don't invent facts about the company.
   ```

4. Rewrite the questions in your own voice. Delete any that assume a fact you haven't checked.
5. The person who decides. A proposal stalls when the person who signs never heard the problem in their own words. Ask in
   the confirmation. "Will anyone else be part of this decision? Happy to include them."

#### Lesson 4.3 Follow up in 10 minutes, next step before you hang up (8 min)

**In plain words.** The best time for a follow up is straight after the call, while it's fresh for both of you. The best

3. Screen. The follow up prompt.

   ```
   Write a short follow up email after a sales call. Use only these notes [paste the four note boxes or the transcript].
   ```

**Do it now.** Write the follow up for transcript 3 with the prompt, check it, and time yourself. 10 minutes.

#### Lesson 5.3 The weekly 20 minute review (6 min)

**In plain words.** Once a week you look at your scorecard for 20 minutes and decide one thing to change. Without it you
keep doing what you did, even when it isn't working.

**You'll be able to.**
- Run the review on a fixed agenda
- Find where your numbers drop and what that usually means

**What's shown.**
1. A 20 minute "Weekly review" block, same time every week. Friday afternoon works for most.
2. Minutes 0 to 5. Fill the week and do the Friday clean up (open deals without a next step).
3. Minutes 5 to 10. Compare with last week and with your one number. Up, down or flat.
4. Minutes 10 to 15. Find the biggest drop between two steps. **Few accepts**, the list or your profile. **Accepts but few
   replies**, the message, usually the first line or the problem. **Replies but few meetings**, the offer or the question.
   **Meetings but not with the person who decides**, targeting or not asking. **Meetings but no proposals**, the next step
   wasn't booked on the call.
5. Minutes 15 to 20. Write one dated change in **Notes**. "Next week, step 1 offers a sample plan instead of a video." One
   change, so you know what worked. Don't judge it on one or two replies.
6. Next week, the first thing you check is whether last week's change moved the number.

**Do it now.** Book the weekly block and write your first change idea in Notes. 5 minutes.
