# OneFortyNine Games. Should we rebrand, and how would we do it (5 Nov 2026)

Built on `call-analysis-2026-11-05.md` (what Owen needs from the site), the 3 Nov call transcript, `brand.md`,
`site-notes.md`, `social.md`, and fresh web research on 5 Nov (sources at the end). Where something is my judgement,
it says **(judgement)**. Where something couldn't be checked, section 9 says so.

---

## 1. The short answer

**Don't rename or fully rebrand him now. Clear the name first, tidy his pieces into one system with the website, and
build the brand on "149", which is the one thing nobody else has.**

Why.
- **The name "Breakout Box" has a real problem.** The biggest escape room company in the UK trades as Breakout (Manchester
  since 2014, Liverpool, 16 and 17 rooms per site), and it already sells **"Breakout Unboxed, an escape room in a box
  delivered to your door"**. Breakout EDU sells lock box kits, and in the US someone has filed BREAKOUT BOX as a trade mark
  for interactive education (pending since March 2025). Owen can't own "Breakout" on Google, and he may not be able to
  protect it. This needs a proper trade mark check before anyone prints more boxes.
- **But a rename this week would hurt him.** He launched at the NEC on 4 and 5 Nov with this name, the strap, the cups,
  the lid labels and The Heist cards are printed, and a park group is trialling the boxes as they are. He's pre revenue
  and careful with money.
- **His strongest brand asset isn't the name, it's 149.** The lock in his logo reads 1 4 9, the company is OneFortyNine,
  and the registered office is 149 High Street. It's already on every cup, lid and decal. If the brand lives in 149, the
  product name can change later without losing anything.
- **His real brand problem is that nothing matches**, not that it's weak (section 3). That's fixable inside the website
  project, cheaply, and it's what he asked for, "we look good, we look professional, we look organised".

So the approach in three steps. Now, a one page brand system shipped with the website. Next, the trade mark check and a
naming decision. Later, when revenue comes in, the full brand and the product range.

## 2. His storyline, the raw material

Everything below is his own words or his own public pages.

- **Born June 2026 next to another business**, and already "further along than I thought". Wants to step out of the other
  business by the end of next year.
- **"Everyone's building AI. We built a box!"** (LinkedIn, 11 Aug). **"An analog product in a digital world. We want people
  off their screens, back sitting around together, having some fun chatting"** (call). **"Doom-scrolling off. People on."**
  (LinkedIn).
- **"The game does the work. The reaction does the marketing."** (his site).
- **Hands off for venues.** "Our customers, their staff, don't have to manage it, don't have to look after it, don't have
  to watch it." Every future product has to fit that, "otherwise, we might also just do something else".
- **Low cost of entry** against digital products that "cost thousands", £350 to own or £60 a month.
- **A range is coming.** A library of games, seasonal games (Christmas, Easter, Halloween), a kids version, a whodunit hamper
  for Airbnbs and premium lodges, a login to reorder.
- **UK first**, "there's no one else out there doing this in the UK". He turned down licensing a French product as "not
  worth the money. We can do it ourselves".
- **149.** The registered office is 149 High Street, Swindon, the company is OneFortyNine Games, and the lock in the
  product logo reads 1 4 9 (brand.md). Whether 149 means more to him than the address is a question for Wednesday.

## 4. The competitors and where he fits

| Who | What they sell | Who runs it | Who they target | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Breakout** (Liverpool, Manchester) | Fixed escape rooms, 16 and 17 rooms per site, plus **Breakout Unboxed**, a boxed game posted to your door, now The Wizard's Apprentice | Their staff (rooms), the buyer (boxes) | Consumers, groups | Not shown |
| **The Escape Game, Party Box** (UK licence holder of **Happy Hour Escape Box**, "originally created in France") | A "cooler-style box" where players "unlock their food and drinks", 3 to 8 players, about 2 hours, plus a kids party version | The players, delivered or collected | Consumers, **campsites and holiday parks**, activity centres, team building. "Campsites and holiday parks wanting a high-value, low-staff activity" | Enquiry only, no prices. Hire or venue partnership, no purchase |
| **Escape Game Events** | Hosted mobile escape rooms, inflatable, gazebo, table top, online | Their game hosts | Corporate, conferences, hotels, schools | "Prices Starting from £500" per event |
| **Breakout EDU** (US) | Lock box kits with a games subscription | Teachers | Schools | $160 to $199 a kit |
| **Pub games, competitive socialising** | Darts, bowling, mini golf, pool | The venue | Pubs, bars | Capital spend, floor space |
| **Digital and VR** | Screens, headsets | Often staff | Venues | "Thousands", in Owen's words |

**What this shows (judgement).**
- **His closest rival is Happy Hour Escape Box**, the same idea (a cool box, food and drink unlocked by puzzles), selling
  in the UK to holiday parks. It's very likely the French concept he was offered. Its venue model is hire or partnership,
  with no prices on the site.
- **His white space is ownership.** He's the only one a venue can **buy or rent at a fixed low price, run with no staff and
  reuse with new games.** Everyone else is a one off hire, a hosted event, a consumer box or a big capital install.
- **The category looks dark.** Breakout is black and grey with padlocks, red and teal, condensed capitals, a man walking
  into a beam of light, and a padlock standing in for the O. **Owen's lock in the O, and our sketch's dark heist look,
  sit inside that cliché.** His buyers are pubs, parks and cafes selling a good time, and his promise is people laughing
  round a table. The brand should look like that, warm, bright and social, which also sets it apart from every escape
  room brand.

### What his buyers already care about, in their own trade press

- **Pubs.** Visits are getting longer while drinking volumes fall. Average dwell time rose about 12 minutes year on year to
  154 to 157 minutes, and spend per head holds near £26.90 (Oxford Partnership figures in The Morning Advertiser and
  Caterer Licensee, 2026). Competitive socialising is forecast at £1.2bn by 2027 (Mintel, via The Morning Advertiser).
  **His promise, more people in, staying longer, spending more, is exactly their language.**
- **Holiday parks.** The big groups are spending on all weather, all age activities for 2026 (several parks investing in
  indoor entertainment and new activities). A no staff, rainy evening activity fits.
- **Everyone.** Phone free socialising is rising. Eventbrite reports UK phone free event listings up about 1,200% (from a
  small base), and Mintel's 2026 UK Leisure Time report says activities that create phone free moments "will become
  increasingly valued". **His "analog product in a digital world" is a trend, not a quirk.** That's worth leaning on.

## 5. The name. Three options

| Option | What it means | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Keep "Breakout Box"** | Clear it legally, add "by OneFortyNine" everywhere | Free now, stock stays valid, the NEC launch keeps its value | Shares a name with the UK's biggest escape room brand and its boxed game. Hard to win on Google. A trade mark objection could force a change later, after he has spent more on it |
| **B. Rename the product now** | A new product name, same 149 system | Clean, ownable, searchable | Wastes printed stock and the NEC launch, costs money he hasn't earned yet, and a new name needs its own clearance |
| **C. Make 149 the brand, the product names descriptive (recommended)** | OneFortyNine (or "149") is the brand venues remember. Products become editions, for example "149 Escape Box, The Heist", "149 Kids", "149 Whodunit Hamper". "Breakout Box" can stay on the current stock and retire at the next print run if the check says so | 149 is unique, already on every product, has a real story (the address, the lock code) and scales to his whole range | Needs one decision from Owen, and a check that "149" and "OneFortyNine" are clear too |

**(judgement) C, done in steps.** Keep the current name on the boxes he has. Run the trade mark check now. Put 149 at
the front of everything new (website, stand, LinkedIn). Decide the product name before the next print run, with the
check in hand. If the check comes back clear and he loves the name, A is still open.

## 6. The brand, if we do it

### The idea

Everything he says comes back to one picture, people round a table, phones away, cracking a code together, and a venue
that did nothing but earn from it. **(judgement) A working line, "149. The code that gets people together."** The three
digits are the lock, the address and the brand all at once.

### The system

- **Mark.** The three lock wheels reading 1 4 9 become the master symbol, on their own as an icon and favicon, with the
  wordmark beside them. It already exists on the cups, the lid and the decal, so current stock stays on brand. **Retire
  the padlock-as-O habit** in headlines, because that's Breakout's look. The wheels are ours, the padlock isn't.
- **Colour.** His navy stays as the anchor, the one colour he really owns. Warm white becomes the main background, so the
  brand reads daylight and hospitality, not night and thriller. One signal colour for action and "unlocked" moments.
  **(judgement)** his orange `#ff914d`, warmer and friendlier, with his red kept as a small accent, since red and black
  padlocks are Breakout's codes. Seasonal editions get their own accent (Christmas red, Halloween orange and so on) on
  the same navy and white.
- **Type.** One chunky soft serif for headlines, close to his Cooper Black (or Cooper Black itself where the licence
  allows), and one clean sans for everything else. Drop Bitter and the typewriter face, and set "GAMES" in the logo in
  the sans.
- **Pictures.** Real people, hands on locks, laughs, tables, warm light, his real product. No AI images, no stock party
  photos. A short list for Owen, people playing (his NEC stand, the trial if allowed), the box in a pub, a clubhouse, a
  lodge and a training room, a flat lay of what's inside, and the box next to a chair for scale.
- **Shapes and motion.** The maze from his B and X becomes a pattern for packaging, cards and backgrounds. Lock wheels
  rolling to 1 4 9 are the one motion signature, the website's opening and the end of every video.
- **Voice.** His own, playful, short runs, lock and code wordplay ("Crack it", "Unlock"), plain money talk on business
  pages, British spelling. Two registers. To players it's fun and in character. To venues it's straight about money, staff
  and space.
- **Architecture.** OneFortyNine Games at the top, then one product family with editions. Breakout Box or its successor
  for venues, The Heist and future games as titles, then Kids, Seasonal and Whodunit as editions, each with its own
  accent colour and the same 149 mark.

### Where it shows up, in order of value

1. The website, with the hidden hints pages per game.
2. LinkedIn, logo, cover and About (fix "final testing").
3. A one page sales sheet and a short deck for park groups and pub companies to forward internally. Big buyers pass
   things up the chain, and he said they have "lots of people involved".
4. The NEC stand banner and next show material.
5. Product, at the next print run. Lid label, strap, cups, cards and the hints QR cards.
6. Email signature, invoice and proposal templates.

## 7. How I'd run it, the process

| Phase | What happens | Owen's part | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Discover (1 week) | A one hour workshop on his story, his 149, what he wants it to feel like and what he hates. Feedback from the park trial and his NEC conversations. An audit of every touchpoint (section 3). The competitor map (section 4) | The workshop, plus photos and any buyer feedback | A brand brief on one page |
| 2. Decide (1 week) | Positioning in one line, the four audiences and what each must believe, the architecture, the naming decision with the trade mark check | Approve | Strategy sheet, name decision |
| 3. Design (1 to 2 weeks) | Two routes for the mark, colour, type, pictures and voice, shown on real things (the box lid, a cup, the website hero, a LinkedIn post), not on empty boards | Pick one, one round of changes | The chosen identity |
| 4. Apply | Website first, then LinkedIn, the sales sheet, the stand and templates | Content and photos | Live site, assets |
| 5. Hand over | A short brand guide, what to use, where, and what never to do, with every file in the right format, and a print plan that uses up current stock first | Use it | Guide and file pack |

**Rules for the process.** Show everything on real objects, because he's a product person. Never reprint stock early. Every
choice is tested against the four buyers, a park manager, a pub manager, a cafe owner and a training lead. Check the name
before money is spent on it.

## 8. Commercially, how to offer this (Raka's call)

- **(judgement) Don't pitch a full rebrand this week.** He told us the tweaks are cosmetic and the concept is great. He's pre
  revenue, and he chose us partly on cost. A rebrand pitch now risks sounding like a bigger bill.
- **Fold the brand system into the website.** The website needs colours, type, a mark and photo direction anyway. Deliver
  them as a one page brand sheet he keeps. That's the "we look organised" he asked for, and it's the first step of this
  plan.
- **Raise the name gently on Wednesday as a favour, not a sale.** "We noticed Breakout, the big escape room chain, sells a
  boxed game called Breakout Unboxed. Have you had the name checked?" It shows we did our homework, and it may save him a
  lot of money. If the answer is "no", the naming and full brand become a natural second project. That one is worth more
  than the website, and it's his to price.
- **Never claim** his pipeline, the park group or the trial anywhere in the brand or the site until he says so.

## 9. What I can't stand behind yet

- **The trade mark position is unknown.** The UK IPO register refused our fetch (403) and TMview was down (503). Using a
  tool to get round the register's bot check was blocked, rightly. Someone needs to search "BREAKOUT BOX", "BREAKOUT" and
  "149" or "ONEFORTYNINE" in classes 28 (games) and 41 (entertainment) in a browser, at trademarks.ipo.gov.uk and in
  TMview for UK and EU marks.
- The US BREAKOUT BOX application (serial 99070381, Learning Undefeated, filed 6 Mar 2025, Class 41, pending) comes from
  a trade mark directory listing, not from the USPTO itself.
- The French creator behind Happy Hour Escape Box isn't named on the UK licence holder's site, so I can't confirm it's the
  company that approached Owen.
- Breakout Unboxed's site loaded as text through curl, but its rendered page came up blank in our browser, and the Party Box
  site failed to load in our browser ("upstream request failed"). That's our side, so I describe neither one's look. Only
  Breakout Liverpool's look was seen.
- "Biggest escape game company in the UK" is Manchester's Finest's 2017 claim and Breakout Unboxed's own, not checked
  independently.
- The trade figures (dwell time, spend per head, the competitive socialising market, phone free events) come from trade
  press and vendor data as reported, not from the original reports.
