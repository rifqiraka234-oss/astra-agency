## 1. What he actually needs, in plain words

Owen sells a product nobody is searching for yet, to busy venue owners who only ever meet it through his LinkedIn post or
cold email. They click through to the website once, decide in a minute whether he looks like a real, organised company
and whether the thing makes them money, and either get in touch or don't. **The website's one job is to earn him the
conversation.** He told us that once he's talking to someone he can close it himself.

So he needs a site that does four things, in this order.

1. **Starts with their problem, not his puzzle.** Getting more people in the door, keeping them longer, and getting them to
   spend more per head. He said the puzzles themselves are "secondary to them almost".
2. **Answers the money question in seconds.** What it costs, what to charge, and what it earns, with "a really easy
   calculator".
3. **Kills the objections before the call.** Price, floor space, staff time, will guests use it, will they come back, and
   what if it gets damaged. He wants the visitor to think "why wouldn't we do this?"
4. **Makes it dead easy to get in touch**, with a form that lands with him already saying how many boxes and what kind of
   venue.

And it has to **look like a proper, trustworthy company** and **grow with him** without being rebuilt. That means new
pages for each type of customer, a page per game as the library grows, the hidden hints pages, and later a login to
reorder.

### Between the lines, the things he didn't say outright

- **He's cost conscious and proud of doing it himself.** He's pre revenue, turned down a French licence as "not worth the
  money, we can do it ourselves", and wants "people who can work with us as we grow". A small first build at £500 with a
  clear path to grow will land better than a big quote.
- **He's selling to two very different buyers.** Big groups (a 65 site park group, a 270 venue cafe group, a 60 pub
  brewery) need to trust the company and will talk first. Small independents ("the quick and easy ones") should be able to
  decide on the page. One site, two speeds, so the form has to suit both.
- **He's about to have real proof.** The park trial runs to the end of the year, and the NEC show this week will give him
  photos of people playing on his stand. The best images he'll ever have are being made right now.
- **He thinks in uses, not in one product.** Rent it out, bundle it with drinks, run event nights, use it in training. The
  calculator should ask "how will you use it", not just "how many stays".

## 3. What the sketch gets right, so keep it

- **The lock in the headline that rolls to 1 4 9.** It's his logo, it's the signature moment, and it's cheap to keep.
- **The calculator, and the form that arrives filled in from it.** He named both ("you read my mind", "the concept is
  great"). Keep them, but simplify (section 5).
- **His navy and his real product.** The honesty of real photos is right. They just need better ones.
- **Rent or own, side by side, with the six month crossover.** Clear, true and his own numbers.
- **One page that's easy to read.** He said "simple". The fix is reordering and adding pages, not more effects.

## 4. Where the sketch is wrong for him

1. **It sells the box to holiday cottage owners only.** Every preset is a cottage, lodge or pod, the steps say "lodge", the
   numbers come from holiday cottage occupancy (Key Data). Pubs, bars, cafes, training companies and attractions, half his
   pipeline, aren't there.
2. **It describes the model his biggest buyer rejected.** "Fill it and leave it in the lodge", "one box can go from unit to
   unit", "units sharing one box".
3. **It opens on the product, not on the buyer's problem.** Mechanics first, money eighth.
4. **It feels like a different brand.** A thin, elegant high fashion serif (Fraunces at weight 100 to 300 with SOFT 0, in `site/css`) where his brand is chunky,
   warm Cooper Black. A space nebula of purple and orange with a starfield where his brand is flat navy, white and red. A
   dark, moody "heist at night" where his promise is people laughing around a table.
5. **No people.** Every picture is the box on its own in the dark. The product's whole point is the reaction, and his own
   line is "The reaction does the marketing".
6. **The objections he named aren't answered.** Floor space, staff time, damage or loss, will they come back, cost against
   digital alternatives.
7. **No "who we are".** Nothing says a real UK company with a real person behind it.
8. **The action is wrong for big buyers.** "Order a box" and "Book a 15 minute call", when he wants an enquiry form and
   can't take calls in his diary yet.
9. **It's one page with no room to grow.** No pages per type of customer, no games page, no hints area, and it's hand coded,
   so he can't edit it himself, which Raka promised on the call he could.
10. **The NEC section is out of date.** The show is 4 to 5 Nov and ends today.
11. **The header shows only "BREAKOUT"**, not his full Breakout Box lock logo or the OneFortyNine Games logo.

## 5. What to change. Colours, type, pictures, gradients, layout, pages, words

### Colours

His real palette, from his own Squarespace theme (brand.md), navy `#09004a`, white `#ffffff`, red `#ff0000` (his
headline colour), orange `#ff914d` (only in his logo's "GAMES"), logo grey `#d9d9d9`.

- **Navy stays the base**, flat and confident, not faded into near black and purple.
- **Bring his red back as the accent.** His headlines are red Cooper Black on every page he made. Use it for the key word in
  each headline and for the buttons. Red on his navy is 4.7 to 1, readable even as body size. On white, darken it slightly
  (`#d8000f`, 5.4 to 1) for small text.
- **Orange becomes the second accent**, the "unlocked" moment, small highlights and the chart line.
- **Add light sections.** The money, objections and form sections on white or a warm off white with navy text. It reads
  "professional and organised", breaks the endless dark, and matches his own white /hints page. Brief point 2 already
  guessed he might want lighter.
- **Remove** the starfield particles and the purple to peach nebula.

### Gradients

Not deleted, redesigned so they mean something (RULES section 5).

- **Hero, a warm table lamp glow.** A low orange pool of light under the box, the light of a table where people are
  playing, on flat navy. One light source, not a nebula.
- **The unlock moment.** When the lock lands on 1 4 9, a red to orange sweep spills out of the lid, his two accents. It
  happens once, on the lock.
- **The calculator result.** The number sits on a small orange to red bar that grows as the earnings grow. The gradient is
  the result itself.

### Type

- **Headlines in a chunky soft serif that matches his Cooper Black.** Two options. If the site is built on his Squarespace,
  use his actual Cooper Black (Adobe Fonts, already loaded on his site). If we build it ourselves, keep Fraunces but at
  weight 800 to 900 with the SOFT axis at 100, which is close to Cooper Black and licensed for us (SIL OFL, already in
  `site/fonts`). Light Fraunces goes.
- **Body**, keep Mona Sans, or Helvetica Neue to match his. Clean and readable, about 18 to 20px.
- **Use less of the mono.** Keep it for codes, prices and small labels only.
- **The voice gets warmer.** His own lines can carry it, "Think escape room meets unboxing.", "No screens, no VR
  headsets.", "The game does the work. The reaction does the marketing." A few exclamation marks are fine, he uses them.

### Pictures

What we have is his phone shots of the box on its own, cut out with rough edges and upscaled. What it needs, in order of
value.

1. **People playing around a table.** The reaction. He'll have this from his NEC stand this week, and from the park trial
   with the park's permission. Ask him first thing.
2. **The box where it's used.** On a pub table, in a park clubhouse, in a lodge, in a training room. One per type of
   customer.
3. **A clean flat lay of what's inside.** The cool box open, the cases, bottle locks, The Heist card, cups and notepads laid
   out on one plain surface, shot from above in daylight.
4. **Scale.** The box next to a chair or a pint glass, which answers "how much space does it take".
5. **A 10 second clip** of a lock clicking open and people cheering, for the hero or the "reaction" moment.

Until he sends them, licence checked stock of people round a table in a pub or a lodge can hold the space. It is never
captioned or claimed as his customers, and the handover says he replaces it.

### The calculator, made "really easy"

- **Two inputs, one answer.** Venue type sets the defaults. Then "How many games a week?" and "What would you charge a
  game?". The answer is one line, "about £X a month, roughly N times what the box costs you", with buying paid off in about
  N weeks.
- **One optional toggle** for his park model, "sell a drink or food package with it", which adds spend per head.
- **Defaults per venue, all labelled estimates.** Pubs and cafes in games a week on quiet afternoons and event nights,
  parks in games a day on site. **Ask Owen for real numbers from his park trial at the session.**
- **Drop** the cottage occupancy data, "units sharing one box" and the drum. Keep the 12 month own against rent chart
  behind a "see the year" link.

### Pages, the first build and later

**First build (what fits now)**
- Home, as above.
- One page per type of customer, all from one template. Their problem, their way of using it, the calculator preset, their
  objections and the same form. Parks, pubs and cafes, and training at least. Holiday lets can stay a section on the home
  page.
- Games, The Heist now, room for more.
- Contact, the enquiry form.
- **Hidden hints per game**, at an address only the QR code knows, not in the menu, not on Google, phone first. One
  puzzle at a time, Hint 1, then Hint 2, then the walkthrough, then "sure you want the answer?" before the code. A new game
  is a new copy of the template.

**Later, as he grows**
- Customer stories once the park trial gives him a real one.
- Kids version, seasonal games and the hamper as their own pages.
- Login to reorder boxes and refills (his "next year's problem").
- Writing for Google, for when people start searching for it next year.

### The words, specific swaps

- "An escape room in a cool box." moves to the line under the headline. The headline is the buyer's problem.
- "Order a box" becomes "Get prices for your venue" or "Get your box". "Book a 15 minute call" becomes "Prefer a call
  back?", a tick box in the form.
- "Fill it and leave it in the lodge" and "one box can go from unit to unit" come out. Each venue page says how that venue
  plays it.
- "Pit team against team" stays for team days. The training page adds his line about watching leadership in practice.
- The NEC section comes out, or becomes "Where to see us next" for his next show.
