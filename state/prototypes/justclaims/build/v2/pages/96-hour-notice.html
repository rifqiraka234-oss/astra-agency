1 The rule

96 hours, from the moment the journey ends.

Where a passenger may have a right to compensation, the airline sends the information electronically, on a durable medium, with clear instructions on how to claim.

2 Who and to whom

The operating airline sends it to every passenger who may be owed.

For a delay that means an arrival more than three hours late. A cancellation or a missed connection can trigger it too.

{{IC:user}}Art. 3(5)

Who sends it

The operating air carrier. Art. 7(4) is addressed to it, and Art. 3(5) makes it responsible for the regulation's duties unless the text says otherwise.

{{LAW:operating-responsible}}
{{IC:users}}Art. 5(4), 6(4), 6b(3)

Who gets it

Passengers who may have a right to compensation. For a delay that's Art. 6(4), for a cancellation Art. 5(4), and for a missed connection Art. 6b(3).

3 How it arrives

Six rules for the message itself.

It has to say two things, the passenger's right to compensation and clear instructions on how to request it. The text sets no wording for either, but it does set how the message arrives.

4 What you prove

If a passenger says nothing arrived, the airline has to show it did.

Art. 14(7) names the 96 hour notice when it puts the burden of proof on the airline. The answer is a record of what was sent, and when.

5 What's left open

Three things the text doesn't say.

These are questions for counsel, not answers.

{{IC:clock}}

The exact moment a journey ends

The text defines a journey and a time of arrival, but not the moment a journey terminates. The claim clock uses the time of arrival.

{{IC:mail}}

A message that bounces

The duty is to provide the information, and the text doesn't address a failed delivery.

{{IC:search}}

A second message

The text sets one notice and no reminder, even if the passenger doesn't respond.
