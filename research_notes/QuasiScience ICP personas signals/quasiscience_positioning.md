## 3. The real alternatives a buyer weighs, and where QuasiScience wins and loses

### Takeaway
I found no rigorous sourced comparison of the four alternatives (in-house, large consultancy, off the shelf, university collaboration) specific to simulation and optimisation work. What follows is largely inference anchored on the sourced facts about QuasiScience and the university route. Treat the strengths and weaknesses as hypotheses for the sales team to test, not findings.

### Cited Findings
- [SOURCED FACT] UK universities and Innovate UK Business Connect actively offer applied-maths and optimisation collaboration to firms through knowledge exchange hubs, consultancy projects and student projects. — [Warwick KE Hub](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/sci/camacs/whatwedo/kehubwg/), [Southampton](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/maths/business_partnership/index.page), [IUK Business Connect](https://iuk-business-connect.org.uk/events/optimal-control-and-scientific-computing-webinar/)
- [VENDOR CLAIM] QuasiScience's own contrast: "neither a traditional consultancy nor a simple software development agency" and "we only deliver tools that we believe will have a genuine impact on KPIs, if it is not evidence based we won't build it" — [FROM BRIEF], sales decks (confidential, not reproduced as public fact). The public site version: "measurable business impact in weeks, not months". — [Products](https://quasiscience.com/products/)
- [VENDOR CLAIM] Delivery windows of 7 to 16 weeks — [FROM BRIEF], sales decks.

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] vs hiring in-house: Strength of in-house is domain context, retention and no per-project fee; weakness is the time and cost to recruit scarce simulation or optimisation engineers and to keep them busy between problems. QuasiScience wins when the problem is episodic, needs several specialisms at once (simulation, ML, automation) and the buyer cannot hire fast. Loses when the buyer already has a strong data science team and wants a permanent capability, or when IP and data must never leave the building.
- [INFERENCE] vs big consultancy: Big firms bring procurement-friendly paper (framework agreements, large balance sheets, insurance, brand, change management) but typically staff juniors on analytical builds and price on day rates and team pyramids. A 7 person firm wins on seniority of the people doing the work, speed and cost; loses on risk transfer, scale, multi-country delivery and the "nobody was fired for hiring X" effect.
- [INFERENCE] vs off-the-shelf software: Software wins on price, support and procurement familiarity; it loses where the problem is non-standard (custom simulation, unusual constraints) or the vendor model cannot encode the buyer's physics or rules. QuasiScience's best position is "the problem the packaged tool can't model", and its two products (MMO, KYC) compete directly with packaged tools, where it is the weaker, unproven party.
- [INFERENCE] vs university collaboration: Universities are cheap, grant-fundable and credible on method, but slow, publication-driven, and rarely deliver production-grade, maintained tools. QuasiScience wins on delivery to production and timeline, loses on academic credibility and grant leverage unless it can actually evidence the "relationships with top Universities" claim.
- [INFERENCE] Where QuasiScience probably loses regardless: security-heavy regulated buyers (no certifications shown), buyers demanding named references, and anything requiring 24/7 support or large teams.

### Gaps
- No independent study comparing build vs buy vs consultancy vs university for optimisation or simulation work.
- No evidence on which alternative buyers actually choose in this segment.
- No evidence that QuasiScience's university relationships are active (none are named).

## 6. Procurement objections for a 7 person supplier and how firms of that size address them

### Takeaway
The sourced material covers public-sector financial standing guidance and Cyber Essentials; private-sector enterprise procurement practice, professional indemnity thresholds, key person and IP terms were not found in sourced form. QuasiScience currently shows no public certification, no named references and a single active director, which are the likely friction points.

### Cited Findings
- [SOURCED FACT, low confidence on date] UK Cabinet Office guidance on supplier financial risk states a supplier's financial position should not exclude a bidder unless clear evidence shows unacceptable risk, that SMEs should not be disadvantaged, and that alternatives such as parent company accounts or guarantees can be used. File metadata dates the PDF to 6 March 2013 even though the filename says Feb-18; the PDF text could not be decoded by my fetcher, so these points come from a search-result summary, not my own read. Check the current Procurement Policy Notes before relying on it. — [Cabinet Office PPN PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/137569/PPN_Supplier_financial_risk_Feb-18.pdf)
- [SOURCED FACT, via search summary] Under PPN 014, central government and NHS buyers may require Cyber Essentials, Cyber Essentials Plus or equivalent evidence of cyber controls where relevant; not required for every public contract. Cyber Essentials certification for organisations under £20m turnover has been bundled with cyber liability insurance by some providers (figures differ between sources, so check current scheme terms). — [GOV.UK press release on supplier cyber standard](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/government-mandates-new-cyber-security-standard-for-suppliers)
- [SOURCED FACT] Companies House shows QuasiScience's sole active director is Marco Ghilardi (the other director resigned in May 2019), and he is the only officer. — [Officers](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/11409242/officers)
- [VENDOR CLAIM] KYC page offers TLS, encryption at rest, EU hosting, retention controls, audit logs and "security documentation on request", but cites no certification or independent audit. — [kyc.quasiscience.com](https://kyc.quasiscience.com)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] Likely objections, in rough order: (1) key person risk, one director and a small team; (2) financial standing, unread accounts and an unusual record of address changes; (3) security and data handling, no ISO 27001, SOC 2 or Cyber Essentials listed publicly (the KYC product is the most exposed, since it handles identity data and sanctions screening); (4) lack of named references, as every testimonial and case study is anonymous; (5) IP ownership and licence terms for bespoke code plus "200+ proprietary libraries" (a buyer will ask who owns what, whether libraries are embedded and what happens on exit); (6) liability cap and insurance.
- [INFERENCE] Mitigations common to small suppliers, to be confirmed with QuasiScience before use: Cyber Essentials (low cost and quick) as an entry badge; professional indemnity and cyber insurance certificates ready in the sales pack; escrow or source code handover and clear IP clauses (client owns bespoke deliverables, QuasiScience keeps pre-existing libraries under licence); a named delivery lead plus documented back-up; paid pilots with small contract values that fall under procurement approval thresholds; and routing via a larger partner or framework.
- [INFERENCE] The KYC product likely cannot sell into regulated financial services without evidence of security certification and data protection documentation.

### Gaps
- No sourced guidance on professional indemnity or cyber insurance levels for SME suppliers (explicitly not found).
- No sourced evidence on how small technical firms in practice address key person risk or IP clauses.
- Whether QuasiScience holds Cyber Essentials, ISO 27001, insurance, or a UK GDPR / data protection registration: unknown (a quick check of the ICO register, NCSC Cyber Essentials directory and the company's own security page is the next step).
- Current PPN text not read in decoded form.
